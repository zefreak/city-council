# Housing in Vancouver, Washington — what the new comprehensive plan can and cannot deliver

**Question.** How much housing can Vancouver WA realistically expect to build under the
comprehensive plan adopted 1 June 2026, and how much would have to come from social housing,
public development or other non-market schemes to actually solve the crisis and support a
Housing First policy that moves people off the street into permanent housing?

**Short answer.** The plan's *total unit count* is roughly achievable. Its *affordability
composition* is not, by a factor of about twenty-four. Vancouver's own housing element says so
in a table on page 117, and then the plan proceeds as if that table did not exist. Filtering —
the mechanism that is supposed to make old market-rate housing affordable over time — runs
**backwards** in this metro. And the specifically Housing-First part of the problem is the
cheap part: housing everyone counted homeless in Clark County costs a small fraction of what
it costs to stop the next cohort arriving.

Status: Parts I–VI complete. Part VII is a claim-by-claim source review. **Part VIII checks the
analysis against the city's own council proceedings** — read that first if you are close to this
work, because it corrects the framing. Part IX is the continuation roadmap.

Parts V and VI answer a follow-on question: what do places that solved this actually do, and
what would Washington's tax code have to look like if it were designed for the problem rather
than inherited from a 1933 court ruling and a 2001 initiative?

# Part I — Zoned capacity versus actual production

## 1. What the plan commits to

Our Vancouver 2026–2045 was adopted by City Council on 1 June 2026 and takes effect
31 July 2026. It is a full rewrite — the first since 2011 — and it comes with a new zoning
map and a new Title 20.

| | |
|---|---|
| Housing units, 2023 baseline | 86,878 |
| Clark County's allocation to Vancouver | 36,527 |
| The City's own planning target | **38,129** (+1,602 above allocation) |
| Implied 2045 stock | 125,007 |
| Zoned capacity under the new map | **44,112** |
| Stated surplus | 5,983 (15.7% above target) |
| Required production | **1,733 units/yr for 22 years** |

The plan's conclusion, in Chapter 4: *"The analysis indicates a net capacity surplus of 5,983
units across the city and capacity surplus for all income levels."*

The zoning is a genuine liberalisation. Minimum densities are set at 8 units/acre in Low-Scale
Neighborhood, 16 in Medium-Scale, 32 in Mixed-Use and 64 in Regional Activity Center. Most
parking minimums are eliminated. HB 1110 forces 4 units on every residential lot and 6 near
frequent transit. Emergency housing, shelters and transitional housing become allowed uses by
right in every zone that allows residential or lodging use.

**The uplift required is +27% on the eleven-year mean and +14% on the five-year mean.** That
is not a fantasy. Upzoning of this magnitude plausibly delivers it — Portland's Residential
Infill Project, the closest analogue, permitted over 1,400 ADU and middle-housing units in
single-dwelling zones between August 2021 and June 2024 and made middle housing the dominant
type built in those zones.

## 3. Capacity is not the binding constraint, and never has been

At the eleven-year mean it would take **32.3 years** to absorb 44,112 units of capacity. At the
five-year mean, 28.9 years. At the best year Vancouver has ever had, 18.4 years.

Capacity only binds if it is absorbed faster than 2,005 units/yr. Vancouver has never reached
that in a single year. **The 5,983-unit surplus is not a margin of safety; it is a measure of
how far the constraint sits from the thing being measured.**

## 4. The strongest evidence *for* the plan

Washington OFM's April 1 housing unit estimates, which count net units added rather than
permits:

| | |
|---|---|
| Vancouver housing units, April 2020 | 81,809 |
| Vancouver housing units, April 2025 | 90,780 |
| Net addition | 8,971 over 5 years = **1,794/yr** |
| Against the required rate | **104%** |

Stated plainly: over 2020–2025 Vancouver added units at slightly *above* the rate the 2045
target requires, under the *old* zoning. The permit series says the same thing about 2021–2022
and something much worse about 2025 — a 44%-of-need year, and a 49% collapse from the 2021
peak.

So: **Part I's verdict is that the headline unit target is achievable.** The rest of this
document is about why that is not the same as solving the problem.

# Part II — The income-band gap

## 5. Half the target has to be below-market, and no zone reaches it

Our Vancouver 2026–2045, Chapter 4, Table 3:

| Income category | Units needed | /yr | Share | Market can deliver? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 0–30% AMI (PSH) | 2,600 | 118 | 6.8% | no |
| 0–30% AMI (non-PSH) | 4,423 | 201 | 11.6% | no |
| >30–50% AMI | 5,890 | 268 | 15.4% | no |
| >50–80% AMI | 5,788 | 263 | 15.2% | no |
| >80–100% AMI | 3,351 | 152 | 8.8% | yes |
| >100–120% AMI | 3,175 | 144 | 8.3% | yes |
| >120% AMI | 12,902 | 586 | 33.8% | yes |
| **Total** | **38,129** | **1,733** | | |

- At or below 80% AMI: **18,701 units, 49% of the target, 850/yr**
- At or below 50% AMI: **12,913 units, 34% of the target**

The "market can deliver?" column is not editorial. It comes from the plan's own Table 4, which
records the *"lowest potential income level served, market rate"* for every zoning designation
in the city. Outside 205 acres of Manufactured Home Park, the answer everywhere is **80% AMI or
above**. Low-Scale Neighborhood serves 100–120%+ at market rate. Urban Mixed-Use and Employment
Mixed Use serve >80–120%.

**Rezoning produces none of the 18,701 units.** It produces the land they could sit on.

## 6. The plan prices the gap and does not fund it

Chapter 4, Table 6 — *Annual Subsidy Needed to Deliver Below Market Rate Housing* — is the most
important table in the document:

| | 0–50% AMI | 50–80% AMI |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total units needed | 12,913 | 5,788 |
| Units needed annually | 646 | 289 |
| Applicable program | Tax credits | MFTE |
| Units delivered annually by that program | 130 | 195 |
| Share covered | **20%** | **67%** |
| Units left unfunded | **516** | **94** |
| Subsidy needed per unit | $400,000 | $325,000 |
| **Total subsidy needed annually** | **$206,260,000** | **$30,680,000** |

**$236.94 million a year, every year, for 22 years — $5.21 billion.**

Per Vancouver resident: **$1,155/yr**. Per housing unit in the city: **$2,727/yr**.

The plan states, immediately above this table: *"To date, the state has allocated no new
resources to support the development of additional rent restricted units affordable at this
level."*

**Sanity check on $400,000/unit.** The Washington State Housing Finance Commission's FY2025
cost data report to the Legislature puts the median total development cost of a Clark County
affordable unit at **$361,221** (the single Clark project completed that year was Fourth Plain
Commons, Vancouver, 106 units); the statewide median was $321,967. The plan's $400,000 subsidy
figure is 111% of Clark County TDC. That looks wrong until you remember what subsidy means at
this depth: at 0–30% AMI rents a project supports almost no permanent debt, so the subsidy
approaches the entire capital stack, plus the soft costs and reserves a tax-credit deal carries.
It is high, but it is not implausible for the deepest band.

## 7. Against the money Vancouver has

| | |
|---|---:|
| Affordable Housing Fund, all uses | $10.0M/yr |
| — production and preservation share | $6.0M/yr |
| Subsidy the plan says is needed | **$236.9M/yr** |
| AHF as a share of the need | **4.2%** |
| Multiple of the current levy required | **23.7×** |

The AHF is not a small or badly designed programme. Voters replaced it in February 2023 with a
$100M / 10-year levy, capped at $10M/yr, running 2024–2033. Its production allocation of $6M/yr
targets 80 direct and 240 leveraged units a year — 2,400 over the decade. The predecessor levy
distributed $42M and created or preserved 1,048 affordable homes.

It is simply an order of magnitude too small for what the comprehensive plan asks of it.

## 8. The property tax cannot raise it — this is a legal fact, not a political one

| | |
|---|---:|
| City assessed value (derived) | $36.5B |
| Current city regular levy rate | $2.41 / $1,000 |
| Statutory cap, RCW 84.52.043 | $3.60 / $1,000 |
| Headroom | $1.19 / $1,000 = **$43.4M/yr** |
| Rate needed for the full subsidy | **$6.50 / $1,000** |
| Implied total city levy | $8.91 / $1,000 — **2.5× the cap** |

RCW 84.52.043 caps a city's regular levy at $3.375 per $1,000, plus $0.225 for a pre-LEOFF
firefighters' pension fund. Vancouver is at $2.41.

**If Vancouver abolished its police, fire, parks and general government levies, took the
regular property tax to its statutory ceiling and spent every marginal dollar on housing, it
would raise $43M/yr — 18% of what its own plan says is needed.**

Assessed value is derived as (General Fund property tax $77,388,214 + Affordable Housing Fund
property tax $10,532,691) ÷ $2.41 × 1,000. Cross-check: the City publishes an AHF cost of $122
a year on a median home; at the December 2025 median value of $489,995 that implies $0.249 per
$1,000 against $0.289 derived here, a gap consistent with the usual lag between assessed and
market value.

# Part III — Filtering runs backwards, on the half of the stock anyone has measured

## 10. The mechanism, and the number everybody quotes

The standard argument against subsidy is filtering: build market-rate housing, and as it ages
it passes down to lower-income households. Rosenthal (2014, *AER* 104(2):687–706) established
this empirically with a "repeat income" model — **rental stock filters down at about 2.5%/yr
in real terms, owner-occupied at 0.5%/yr.** That 2.5% is the number quoted in most arguments
that filtering can substitute for subsidised production.

## 11. The number nobody quotes

Liu, McManus and Yannopoulos (2022, *Regional Science and Urban Economics* 93:103758) ran the
same estimator MSA by MSA on Freddie Mac appraisal data. Two findings:

- The **national average is 0.42%/yr downward**, not 2.5%.
- **Roughly 20% of MSAs filter upward** — homes serve *higher* real incomes as they age.

Portland–Vancouver–Hillsboro OR–WA, the MSA Vancouver WA sits in, is one of them.

| | |
|---|---:|
| Portland–Vancouver–Hillsboro annual filtering rate | **+0.0035** (upward) |
| Standard error | 0.0004 |
| 40-year cumulative | **+15.12%** |
| Rank among 179 MSAs (1 = fastest downward) | **166** |
| Measured against local median income | +0.0003 (rank 156) |
| National mean | −0.0046 |
| National mean, relative to median income | −0.0036 |

Washington MSAs, for local context:

| MSA | Rate | 40-yr | Direction |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Seattle | +0.0063 | +28.8% | upward |
| Bellingham | +0.0015 | +6.1% | upward |
| Olympia | −0.0003 | −1.2% | downward (negligible) |
| Spokane | −0.0014 | −5.3% | downward |
| Kennewick | −0.0107 | −34.8% | downward |
| **Portland–Vancouver** | **+0.0035** | **+15.1%** | **upward** |

Read the Portland line as: **a 40-year-old home in this metro is occupied by a household with
15% more real income than the household that moved into it new.** The stock does not become
affordable with age here. It becomes less affordable.

## 12. What that costs in units

Model: take Clark County's 2023 housing supply by affordability band (Commerce Housing for All
Planning Tool, via Clark County Issue Paper 5 Table 3), scale to Vancouver's 42.2% share of the
county stock, assume units are uniformly distributed within their band, age the stock 22 years
at each filtering rate, and count the units that cross from above 80% AMI to at or below it.

| Filtering scenario | Rate | Units by 2045 | /yr | % of the 18,701 needed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Rosenthal 2014, rental (national) | −0.0250 | 33,464 | 1,521 | 179% |
| Rosenthal 2014, owner (national) | −0.0050 | 7,315 | 332 | 39% |
| Liu et al. 2022, national average | −0.0042 | 6,090 | 277 | 33% |
| **Liu et al. 2022, Portland–Vancouver (actual)** | **+0.0035** | **0** | **0** | **0%** |

Two things follow.

**First, even the optimistic literature does not close the gap.** At the *national average*
filtering rate, Vancouver's existing stock would deliver 6,090 units into the sub-80% band by
2045 — a third of the need, not all of it. Only Rosenthal's rental-specific 2.5% covers it, and
that rate applies to no market in this half of the country.

**Second, Vancouver does not get the national average.** It gets zero. This is the single
strongest empirical argument in the whole exercise against a market-only strategy in this
specific city, and it is a local fact, not an ideological position: Liu et al. estimate it with
a standard error of 0.0004.

The mechanism is house price appreciation. Liu et al. find filtering rates are largely explained
by price growth, and that markets with elastic supply and low Wharton regulatory index scores
filter down fastest. **This is the part of the argument the comprehensive plan actually
addresses** — a genuine upzoning should, over decades, slow appreciation and push the filtering
rate toward or below zero. But the plan's own 22-year window is far too short for that to
deliver the 18,701 units, and nothing in Part I suggests production will be fast enough to
break the appreciation trend on its own.

# Part IV — What could actually close the gap

## 13. Sizing the options

| Source | $M/yr | % of the $237M gap |
|---|---:|---:|
| Affordable Housing Fund, as levied today | 10.0 | 4.2% |
| HB 1590 0.1% sales tax, already levied (§18b) | ~7.0 | ~3.0% |
| — its production/preservation share only | 6.0 | 2.5% |
| Per-capita share of a record state Housing Trust Fund | 7.6 | 3.2% |
| Every remaining dollar of city levy capacity | 43.4 | 18.3% |
| Seattle Prop 1A tax, scaled by population (ceiling — see §18b, this is the wrong instrument) | 12.6 | 5.3% |
| **All of the above, stacked** | **73.6** | **31.1%** |

The Seattle line needs a caveat. Proposition 1A, approved February 2025, is a 5% employer
payroll tax on individual compensation above $1M, yielding roughly $50M/yr for the Seattle
Social Housing Developer. Population scaling gives Vancouver $12.6M — but that is a *ceiling*,
not an estimate. Seattle's yield comes from a handful of employers paying individual
compensation over $1M. Vancouver has no comparable concentration. The realistic figure is a
fraction of $12.6M.

Worth noting what Seattle got for it: SSHD's first acquisition, approved May 2026, was Elara at
the Market in Belltown — **150 units for $60.9M**, an acquisition rather than new construction,
against a stated target of 1,670 units. Social housing is a real institution with real capital,
and it is also slow and expensive to start.

## 14. The most useful comparison in the exercise

| | |
|---|---:|
| Vancouver's subsidy gap per resident | $1,155/yr |
| Same rate applied to Washington State | **$9.4B/yr** |
| Actual Housing Trust Fund, 2025–27 biennium | $302M/yr ($37/resident) |
| Ratio | **31×** |

The 2025–27 capital budget's $605M for the Housing Trust Fund was described by the legislature
as record-setting. **The subsidy implied by Washington's own HB 1220 housing element
requirements is roughly 31 times the money the state has ever put behind them.**

This is not a Vancouver failure. Vancouver did the arithmetic honestly and published it. The
state wrote a statute requiring cities to plan for housing by income band and did not attach a
financing mechanism that operates at the scale of the requirement.

## 15. A revolving public development fund — solves one band, not the other

Montgomery County, Maryland's Housing Opportunities Commission runs a $100M county-backed
revolving Housing Production Fund, used as short-term construction equity on publicly owned
mixed-income projects and recycled on permanent financing. Over 20 years it is expected to
support 6,000 units, of which 1,800 (30%) are permanently affordable — 20% at ≤50% AMI, 10% at
moderately priced dwelling unit limits (65–70% AMI).

That is **90 affordable units/yr per $100M of revolving capital**.

| Band | Units/yr unfunded | Revolving capital implied |
|---|---:|---:|
| 50–80% AMI | 94 | **$104M** |
| 0–50% AMI | 516 | $573M |

**The shallow band is solvable.** $104M is a single bond issue for a city with a $36.5B tax
base. Vancouver could close its entire 50–80% AMI shortfall with a revolving public development
fund and never write a recurring cheque.

**The deep band is not a financing problem, and no financing innovation touches it.** A
revolving fund has to revolve. Rents at 30% AMI do not refinance anything — that is the
definition of the band. The Montgomery County model does not reach below 50% AMI, and it is not
because nobody thought of it. This is arithmetic and it stands on its own: Vienna confirms it
independently, since its own deepest-need housing is not funded from the loan book either.

## 16. Building at cost

A public developer avoids two frictions a tax-credit deal pays. Both are now sourced ranges
rather than assumptions:

- **Developer fee.** WSHFC 2025 Bond/Tax Credit Program Policies §2.6, implementing
  WAC 262-01-130(8)(f): fees are capped in aggregate at *"15% of Total Residential Project Costs
  less reserves, Donation, Intermediary Costs, and less the requested developer fee amount"*, with
  only 10% of that net base allowed into eligible basis. Because the base excludes the fee itself,
  those convert to **9.1%–13.0% of total development cost**.
- **LIHTC syndication loss.** Novogradac equity pricing: about **85 cents** per credit dollar for
  both 9% and 4% credits as of June 2025, down from about 92 cents immediately after the 2017 Tax
  Cuts and Jobs Act — a loss of **8%–15%**.

| Scenario | Saving | Gap falls to | Cut by |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Conservative — 9.1% fee, 92¢ equity | 16.4% | $198.2M | $38.8M |
| **Current market — 13.0% fee, 85¢ equity** | **26.1%** | **$175.1M** | **$61.8M** |
| *The earlier uncited 12%/12% assumption* | *22.6%* | *$183.5M* | *$53.5M* |

The old assumption sat inside the sourced range — but at today's equity pricing the saving is at
the **top** of it, not the middle.

Real money, and worth having — even the low end is four times the entire AHF levy. But a
16–26% cost reduction against a 24× revenue shortfall does not change the conclusion. **Public development
is a way of spending the money better, not a substitute for having it.**

## 17. Housing First, priced honestly

Clark County Point-in-Time Count, 29 January 2026 (CoC WA-508): **1,260 people**, down 18% from
1,530 in 2025. 659 unsheltered, 478 in emergency shelter, 123 in transitional housing.
Chronically homeless: 305 individuals plus 62 people in chronically homeless families = **367,
about 29% of the total**.

At Clark County median TDC of $361,221/unit capital and $25,000/unit/yr operating:

| Scope | Capital | Amortised /yr | Operating /yr | All-in /yr | % of the Part II gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Everyone counted unsheltered (659) | $238M | $11M | $16.5M | **$27.3M** | 12% |
| Everyone counted, sheltered or not (1,260) | $455M | $21M | $31.5M | **$52.2M** | 22% |
| The plan's 2045 PSH target (2,600) | $939M | $43M | $65.0M | **$107.7M** | 45% |

**Housing everyone currently on the street in Clark County costs about $27M a year all-in.**
That is affordable. It is two and a half AHF levies. It is well inside what a city with a
$36.5B tax base and $43M of unused levy capacity could choose to do.

## 18. Why that is the cheap half

| | |
|---|---:|
| Renter households in Vancouver | ~40,400 |
| Severely cost-burdened (>50% of income on housing) | **~10,500** (26% of renters) |
| Ratio to the entire county PIT count | **8×** |

Housing everyone currently on the street is affordable. What is not affordable is the ~10,500
Vancouver renter households paying more than half their income in rent — the reservoir the
street population is drawn from, eight times larger than the entire county PIT count.

**A Housing First policy that does not fund the 0–30% AMI pipeline behind it refills the
shelters as fast as it empties them.** The PIT count fell 18% this year; it is a stock measure
of a flow problem, and the flow is set by the gap in Part II, not by the shelter capacity in
Part IV.

## 18b. Seeding a portfolio without a tech-sector tax

§13 scaled Seattle's Proposition 1A by population and flagged it as a ceiling. That was the wrong
instrument to reach for. Prop 1A taxes compensation above $1 million; Vancouver has almost none of
it, and no amount of scaling makes a levy tuned to one tax base generic. The better question is
what a mid-sized city without a tech sector can actually do — and the answer is **balance sheet
and land, not a novel tax.**

### First, a correction: Vancouver's own effort was understated

Under **HB 1590 (2020)** a Washington city may levy a **0.1% sales tax for affordable housing
without a public vote**. Vancouver adopted it in **November 2020** and uses it for homelessness
response — Safe Stay and Safe Park. §13 omitted it entirely.

No published revenue figure was found. The cleanest available proxy is the city's Transportation
Benefit District, which levies an *identically sized* 0.1% city sales tax that raised
**$6.95M in 2024**.

| Committed housing revenue | $M/yr |
|---|---:|
| Affordable Housing Fund levy | 10.0 |
| HB 1590 0.1% sales tax (proxy) | ~7.0 |
| **Total** | **~16.9** |

That is **7.2% of the gap, not the 4.2%** reported in §7. The conclusion is unchanged in
substance — it is still a rounding error against $236.9M — but Vancouver is doing more than the
earlier figure credited.

### Second: the public developer already exists

The **Vancouver Housing Authority** runs 3,410 Housing Choice Vouchers and 175 public housing
units, and like any Washington housing authority it can issue revenue bonds and hold property in
perpetuity. Part VI's asset-lock argument does not require inventing an institution. One is
already chartered.

### Third: land can be seeded at no cash cost

**RCW 39.33.015** permits any Washington municipality to transfer surplus real property at *low or
no cost* where the public benefit is affordable housing. Vancouver has already used it — part of
the City Hall parking lot was sold for **$1** for a housing project in 2025. The city has also
adopted a construction sales tax deferral for affordable housing on parking lots, and has held
**tax increment financing** authority since HB 1189 (2021), good for up to two increment areas
over 25 years.

### Fourth, and this is the actual seed: bond the streams you already have

A flow becomes a portfolio when you borrow against it. At 5% over 30 years:

| Dedicated to debt service | Capital raised | Homes acquired | Homes newly built |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| All committed revenue (~$16.9M/yr) | **$261M** | 847 | 630 |
| Half of it (~$8.5M/yr) | $130M | 424 | 315 |

### And the cost side, from WSHFC FY2025 medians

| | |
|---|---:|
| New construction, statewide median | $413,776/unit |
| **Acquisition and rehab** | **$307,625/unit — 26% cheaper** |
| Land as a share of total project cost | 9% |

Donated public land removes that 9% from a new build. **These are alternative paths, not a
stack** — for an acquisition the purchase price *is* the land cost, so the 9% and the 26% cannot
be multiplied together. Either path can carry the 16–26% public-development saving from §16 on
top.

### The point

Vancouver does not need to invent a tax to start a portfolio. It already has two dedicated
revenue streams, a housing authority that can hold and bond, a statute that hands over public land
for a dollar, TIF authority, and 175 public housing units plus 3,410 vouchers already under public
management. **What is missing is not an instrument. It is a mandate to accumulate rather than to
subsidise transactions** — which is the same conclusion Part VI reaches from the Vienna and Sweden
evidence, arrived at from the opposite direction.

Note the ceiling honestly: $261M of bonding is roughly one year of the subsidy gap. This seeds a
portfolio; it does not close Part II.

# Part V — What other places actually do

## 19. Five comparators, five different mechanisms

Part IV ended on a conclusion about Washington's tax code rather than about housing. That
invites the obvious question: what do places that solved this actually do? Five comparators,
each chosen because it isolates a different mechanism.

| | Mechanism | Scale | Transfers to Vancouver? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vienna** | Dedicated 1% payroll levy; perpetual public and limited-profit ownership | ~50% of the city's residential stock | **Yes** — the levy is the one instrument of the right size |
| **Helsinki** | Housing First at national scale, placing into existing social stock | Long-term homelessness −68%, 2008–2022 | Partly — the *method*, not the cost |
| **Singapore** | Compulsory below-market land acquisition, CPF-financed | ~80% of residents in public flats | **No** — constitutionally unavailable |
| **Tokyo** | National zoning; buildings taxed as depreciating durables | ~5× California's per-capita build rate | Partly — WA reached for half of it in HB 1110 |
| **Sweden** | The Million Homes Programme, then its dismantling | 1M homes 1965–74; municipal share 25% → 17% | **As a warning about durability, not about drift** |

## 20. Vienna — the number people get backwards

Vienna's *Wohnbauförderung* is funded by a **1% levy on wages**, half from the employee and
half from the employer.

> **Correction, from the primary source.** An earlier draft of this section called the levy
> "earmarked and not divertible". The *Berichtsstandard Wohnbauförderung 2023* (p.6) says
> otherwise: dedicated transfers (*Zweckzuschüsse*) ended in the late 2000s, and the 2018
> *Finanzausgleich* converted the contribution into a general *Länder* tax — with the
> often-demanded reintroduction of hypothecation explicitly not on the table. **The revolving
> mechanism is real; the ring-fence is not.**

| | |
|---|---:|
| Population | 1,900,000 |
| Share of residential stock the city owns, co-owns or manages | ~50% |
| Limited-profit housing associations | 58 |
| Levy receipts to Vienna | €250M/yr |
| **Actual housing spend** | **€530M/yr** |
| Loan repayments funding the difference (**Austria-wide**, not Vienna's) | €1.4B |
| **Leverage on the tax** | **2.12×** |

Now the comparison that matters:

| | Per resident, per year |
|---|---:|
| Vienna's *entire* housing outlay | **$301** |
| Vancouver's unfunded *gap* | **$1,155** |

**Vienna does not outspend Vancouver. It spends roughly a quarter as much per resident, while
housing half its population.** Two things explain that, and neither is generosity:

1. **Vienna's spending is marginal; Vancouver's would be catch-up.** Vienna is maintaining and
   incrementally extending a stock it began building in the 1920s. Vancouver would be creating
   one from a base of about 1,447 city-sponsored rent-restricted units.
2. **Vienna lent the money and still owns the asset.** Austria-wide, a ~€1.3bn levy sits
   alongside ~€1.4bn of repayments on outstanding loans, and the primary source says those two
   components together cover most *Länder*'s housing expenditure. A century of lending rather
   than granting compounds. (The €250M/€530M Vienna split is secondary-sourced; the €1.4bn is
   national, not the city's.)

The corollary is uncomfortable for both sides of the argument. Vienna is not evidence that this
is cheap — it is evidence that it is cheap *once you have already done it*, and that the
expensive part is the century you did not spend building the stock.

## 21. Helsinki — Housing First is cheap *after* you have solved Part II

| | |
|---|---:|
| Long-term homelessness, Finland, 2008–2022 | **−68%** |
| Helsinki supported housing units | 127 → 1,309 |
| Helsinki independent rentals for formerly homeless people | 65 → 2,433 |

That 59× ratio is not a like-for-like comparison and the mismatch is the entire point.
Finland's Housing First line item is small because it buys *placements* into a large existing
social-rental stock and a state subsidised-loan system that both predate the programme.
Vancouver would have to build the stock first — which is §17's capital column.

**Finland's number is what Housing First costs after Part II is solved. It is not a shortcut
around Part II.** This is the single most common misreading of the Finnish result in American
policy debate, and it matters here because Vancouver is currently trying to run the Helsinki
method on a housing stock that does not exist.

## 22. Singapore — the instrument is land, and it is unavailable

80% of Singaporeans live in Housing Development Board flats; the ownership rate is 88%; roughly
20% of wages route through the Central Provident Fund, which can be spent on an HDB purchase.

The mechanism underneath all of it is the **Land Acquisition Act 1966**, which let the state
compulsorily acquire land **below market value**, with the decision not challengeable in court.

No amount of political will makes that available in Washington. The Fifth Amendment requires
just compensation and the state constitution adds its own protections. Singapore is worth
reading for what public development can achieve at scale, and worth discounting as a template.

## 23. Tokyo — and the sharpest connection to Part III

Japan is not a public housing story. Its affordability comes from supply: the 1968 City
Planning Law sets **12 use zones nationally**, and local governments cannot block development
that conforms. Tokyo permitted 120,000 units in 2023 and builds roughly **five times California's
per-capita rate**. Washington reached for the same instrument in HB 1110 — but HB 1110 sets a
floor and leaves local discretion, where Japan removed it.

The sharper point is about filtering. **Japanese housing is taxed and financed as a depreciating
consumer durable**, written down over roughly 22–30 years and then demolished. Filtering works
there because the tax code says a building is a wasting asset.

In Portland–Vancouver the tax code, the mortgage market and the planning system all treat a
building as an *appreciating* asset — which is precisely why the measured filtering rate is
**+0.35%/yr instead of negative**.

**Filtering is not a law of nature. It is a policy output.** Part III measured the output.
Japan shows what a different input produces.

## 24. Sweden — and a correction to an earlier version of this analysis

An earlier draft used Sweden as a *structural* warning: that a public developer required to be
self-financing drifts up the income bands. **That framing was wrong on causation and wrong on
dates, and it has been retired.** What replaced it is more useful.

### First: the largest social housing success in the OECD

| Million Homes Programme, 1965–74 | |
|---|---:|
| Homes built | 1,000,000 |
| Substandard units demolished | 400,000 |
| Total national stock at the time | ~3,000,000 |
| **Net addition** | **600,000 = 20% of the stock** |
| Same proportional effort applied to the US | ~25.9M homes in a decade |

Gowan & Cooper frame that last figure as **18.5 million *additional* homes** over what the US
actually built in 2007–17. They also cite a comparative review in *Planning Perspectives*
finding that Swedish housing of this era was better built, better planned and better serviced
than the Berlin, Madrid, Rome, Paris, Riga, Budapest, Bratislava or UK equivalents.

Their reading of the failures — Tensta, post-1970 oversupply, a Blue Line that opened eight
years after the first tenants moved in — is that these were **allocative and infrastructural,
not inherent to state funding.** That reading is defensible and this analysis adopts it.

### Second: the dismantling, and the dates matter

| | |
|---|---:|
| 1991–94, Bildt centre-right government | housing ministry (est. 1974) abolished; interest-subsidy system dismantled |
| Completions trough, 1995 | **12,000 units** — an all-time low |
| Municipal share of national housing stock, 1990 | 25% |
| Municipal share of national housing stock, 2010 | **17%** |
| Lost *before the 2011 Act was even passed* | **8 points of national housing stock** |

**The 2011 Act did not cause the decline.** Essentially all of the damage was done in the twenty
years before it, by sell-offs — frequently well below market value — outstripping a construction
rate that had already collapsed. The earlier draft of this analysis attributed to 2011 an
outcome that predates it. That was a mis-specified cause, not a nuance.

### Third: what the 2011 Act actually was

*Allbolagen* was triggered by a complaint from **Fastighetsägarna**, the property owners'
federation, to the **European Commission** — arguing that cost-rent municipal housing
constituted illegal state aid under EU competition law. It landed under the Reinfeldt
centre-right government (2006–14), so the political colour is real, but the forcing function was
Brussels.

And the resolution was **negotiated, not imposed**. The Tenants' Union and Sveriges Allmännytta
jointly designed the response and won the fight that mattered: they **refused means-testing**.
Sweden kept universal, non-means-tested municipal housing and gave up cost-based rent-setting.
The Netherlands, under the same pressure, chose the opposite — kept the subsidy, accepted income
restriction.

> **The constraint is EU competition law, and it has no United States counterpart.** A US social
> housing developer can be universal *and* subsidised. Sweden was made to choose; Vancouver
> would not be. **This is a point in favour of the Part VI argument, not against it.**

### What does survive: reforms ratchet

| | |
|---|---|
| School vouchers, 1991, Bildt government | 85% of public school cost |
| Social Democrats return, 1994 | not repealed — **raised to 100%** |
| Mandatory primary-care vouchers, 2010 | survived S governments 2014–22 intact |

Swedish governments alternate — centre-right 1991–94, S-led 1994–2006, centre-right 2006–14,
S-led 2014–22, centre-right since 2022. That is roughly **14 of the last 35 years**, not decades
of uninterrupted right-wing rule. The accurate claim is stronger than the loose one: **the
right's reforms outlasted the left's returns to power.**

Pelling's catalogue for the marketised welfare sectors — PISA decline, socio-economic and ethnic
segregation, a National Audit Office finding of *"higher consumption of care, but also more
unequal care"*, organised crime inside care providers — is what accumulates *because nobody
unwinds it*.

### The design conclusion

For a Vancouver endowment this is the real risk, and it is a **design question, not a reason not
to build**:

- **Vienna's** limited-profit associations carry statutory non-distribution and asset-lock
  constraints. Surpluses must be reinvested; the asset cannot be sold out of the sector.
- **Sweden's** municipal housing companies had no equivalent lock — which is how 8 points of
  national housing stock could be sold in twenty years.

**If you build the endowment, the asset lock is the load-bearing clause — not the financing. A
public developer that can be sold is a public developer that eventually is.**

# Part VI — Designing it from scratch

## 25. The one instrument that is the right size

A Vienna-style 1% payroll levy, priced against Washington ESD covered-wage data:

| Applied to | $M/yr | Against the relevant gap |
|---|---:|---:|
| Wages of jobs located in Vancouver | 68.8 | 29% |
| **Wages of jobs located in Clark County** | **124.2** | **52%** |
| All Washington wages (vs the $9.4B statewide need) | 3,373 | 36% |

Clark County covered wages, 2024: **$12.42B** (180,400 workers × $68,840). Washington:
**$337B** (3,544,556 × $95,160).

A 1% countywide payroll levy raises **$124M/yr — 12× the Affordable Housing Fund levy and 2.9×
every remaining dollar of city property tax capacity.** It is the only instrument anywhere in
this document of the same order of magnitude as the problem.

Two honest objections:

- **Cross-river leakage.** A large share of Clark County residents work in Oregon and would pay
  nothing under an employment-based levy; a residence-based levy would reach them. This is the
  same structural feature that shows up in the companion road-funding analysis, where a quarter
  of Clark County's driving happens on Oregon pavement.
- **Regressivity.** A flat payroll levy is regressive. In the second-most-regressive state tax
  system in the country, that is not a small objection — it is the objection.

The residence-based figure is no longer an estimate: Washington ESD's Clark County profile
reports a civilian labour force of **252,007** with **240,925 employed residents**. At the
county average wage that is **$169M/yr, 71% of the gap** — against $126M on the employment base.
(Vintage mismatch worth noting: the labour force count is current, the average wage is 2024.)

## 26. What stands in the way, specifically

| Constraint | What it does |
|---|---|
| **No graduated income tax** | *Culliton v. Chase* (1933) held that income is "property", so the constitution's uniformity clause and 1% rate cap bar a graduated rate. Still the live constraint; still being litigated. |
| **1% levy growth cap** | I-747 (2001) caps annual regular levy growth at 1%, well below inflation plus population. Much of why Vancouver's rate has drifted to $2.41 against a $3.60 ceiling. |
| **$3.60 / $1,000 city cap** | RCW 84.52.043, plus a $5.90 aggregate cap across junior and senior taxing districts. |
| **Gross receipts, not profits** | The B&O tax falls on revenue, so it cannot be made progressive with respect to ability to pay. |
| **Regressivity** | Lowest-income households pay 15.7% of income in excise and property taxes; the highest pay 4.4%. |

Note what this list is. Every one of these is a *tax* constraint. **None of them is a housing
constraint.** Vancouver's housing element fails on arithmetic that was settled in 1933 and 2001,
by voters and judges who were not thinking about housing at all.

### One constraint Washington does *not* have

European social housing systems operate under EU competition law, which treats a subsidy to a
landlord serving the general population as potential illegal state aid. That is what forced
Sweden's 2011 choice (§24) and the Netherlands' opposite one: **universality or subsidy, pick
one.**

There is no US analogue. A Washington social housing developer could be **universal *and*
subsidised** — open to all incomes, cross-subsidising internally, and receiving public capital
without a competition-law challenge. The single biggest legal obstacle facing the European
models this document draws on simply does not apply here. Given how constrained Washington is on
the revenue side, it is worth being precise about where it is *not* constrained.

## 27. Grant versus endowment — the structural finding

This is the part that does not require new revenue to be interesting.

The American model **grants** subsidy per unit in exchange for a time-limited covenant.
Vancouver's Affordable Housing Fund sets a standard affordability period of **40 years**;
federal tax credit covenants are shorter. The Vienna model **lends** into an entity that must
reinvest its surpluses and can never sell.

Same $236.94M a year. Units in the affordable stock:

| Year | Grant model | Endowment model | Ratio |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 12,200 | 16,982 | 1.4× |
| 40 | 24,400 | 41,822 | 1.7× |
| 60 | **24,400** | 67,686 | 2.8× |
| 80 | **24,400** | 93,550 | 3.8× |
| 100 | **24,400** | 119,414 | 4.9× |

**The grant model plateaus at 24,400 units** — 610 a year in, and after 40 years 610 a year back
out as covenants expire. It is a treadmill: $236.9M a year forever, buying a stock that stops
growing in 2065 and never grows again.

The endowment column ramps leverage linearly from 1.0× to Vienna's 2.12× over 30 years rather
than assuming a mature loan book from day one.

**The first load-bearing caveat, and it is the same one as §15:** cost-rent financing only
reaches down to roughly 50–60% AMI, because the model needs rents that service debt. Below that
you still need recurring operating subsidy. **The endowment structure changes what a dollar
buys. It does not change which band a dollar reaches.** Vienna's own deepest-need housing is not
funded by the loan book either.

**The second caveat is not financial at all, and §24 is where it comes from.** Every year in the
endowment column above assumes the asset is still there. Sweden's municipal housing companies
lost 8 points of national housing stock in twenty years because nothing in statute stopped them
being sold; Vienna's limited-profit associations carry non-distribution and asset-lock
constraints that do. **An endowment with no statutory asset lock is an endowment with a sell-by
date**, and the compounding in the table above is exactly what a future government would be
selling.

## 28. The answer to the question asked

**How much can Vancouver realistically expect to build under the new comprehensive plan?**

Roughly the total it is aiming for. 1,733 units/yr requires a +14% to +27% uplift on recent
production; the zoning change is large enough to plausibly deliver that; OFM's unit series says
Vancouver already hit 104% of the required rate over 2020–2025 under the *old* zoning. The
serious risk to the total is not zoning but the 2025 collapse to 771 units — a cost-of-capital
and construction-cost problem the comprehensive plan has no instrument against.

**How much has to come from somewhere else?**

**18,701 units — 49% of the target — cannot come from the market at any zoning.** The plan
prices that gap at $236.9M/yr and funds 4.2% of it. Filtering, which would ordinarily supply
part of it, supplies **zero** in this metro. Stacking every plausible revenue source — the
existing levy, a per-capita share of a record state Housing Trust Fund, every remaining dollar
of statutory city levy capacity, and a Seattle-style social housing tax at its population-scaled
ceiling — reaches **31%** of it.

**What would actually work, in descending order of tractability:**

1. **A revolving public development fund of ~$104M closes the 50–80% AMI band outright.** This
   is the one genuinely shovel-ready answer in the document and it does not require a new tax.
2. **Housing First for the existing homeless population costs ~$27M/yr all-in** and is within
   reach of the city's unused levy capacity alone.
3. **Building at cost through a public developer saves ~$53M/yr** on whatever does get built.
4. **The 0–30% AMI band — 7,023 units, the single largest affordability need in the plan —
   has no local solution under the current tax code.** It requires recurring operating subsidy
   at roughly thirty times current levels.

**And what the comparators add.** A Vienna-style 1% payroll levy on Clark County wages raises
**$124M/yr — 52% of the gap on its own**, twelve times the Affordable Housing Fund and nearly
three times every remaining dollar of city property tax capacity. It is the only instrument
anywhere in this document of the right order of magnitude, and Washington could legislate it
tomorrow without touching the constitution. Whether it *should* be a flat payroll levy, in the
second-most-regressive state tax system in the country, is a separate and harder question —
but the arithmetic says the money exists and the current instruments simply are not aimed at
it.

Separately, and requiring no new revenue at all: **switching from granting subsidy to lending
it into a permanently-held public asset roughly triples the stock the same money buys over
sixty years**, because the American grant model plateaus the moment covenants start expiring
at the rate new ones are signed. That is a design choice, not a budget constraint — and its
load-bearing clause is a **statutory asset lock**, not the financing structure. Sweden lost 8
points of national housing stock in twenty years for want of one.

And one thing Washington is *not* short of: the EU competition-law constraint that forced Sweden
to choose between universality and subsidy has no US counterpart. **A Vancouver social housing
developer could be both.**

## 33. The three that changed shape

**Filtering (§10–12) was the big one.** Liu et al. state on p.11: *"our data cover only
owner-occupied properties."* Their sample is Freddie Mac conforming loans on single-family,
condo and townhouse properties, 1993–2018. Peter & Pinto (AEI, 2024) replicate the finding on
public-records sale pairs matched to HMDA — 2.4M pairs, largest 100 metros, 2009–2022, R²=65%
against price appreciation — but those are mortgage originations too. **The entire modern
metro-resolved filtering literature is owner-occupied**, and about half of Vancouver's stock is
rented. Modelling tenures separately gives 5,086–16,397 units (27–88% of the sub-80% need), not
zero. AEI's own census analysis of 1960–79 stock also shows downward filtering 1980–2020 on a
longer horizon — evidence the 22-year window may be too short.

**Finland (§21) reversed.** Eleven years of decline ended in 2024. Single homeless people are
now 4,579; long-term homelessness +29% to 1,306; rough sleeping +50% to 694. The attributed
causes are cuts to housing allowance and social assistance. *What failed was not the housing
model but the income support behind it* — the Sweden lesson on a ten-year lag, and a direct
argument that the asset lock in §27 is necessary but not sufficient.

**Vienna (§20) has a real insider/outsider critique.** 21,000 households on the subsidised
waiting list; a typical co-op flat requires an entry payment around $38,000; means-testing is
minimal so well-off tenants keep prime units; residency rules exclude newcomers. This *sharpens*
§15 rather than weakening it: even Vienna does not reach the bottom band through the cost-rent
machinery — it reaches it through separate municipal stock and cash benefits.

## 34. What the review did not shake

The core finding does not depend on any single source. **The plan requires 7,023 units at
0–30% AMI. No zoning produces them. No filtering scenario reaches them. No revolving fund
refinances them. The city's entire legal taxing capacity covers 18% of the bill.** Every
correction above makes the shallow bands look easier and leaves the deep band exactly where it
was.

## 32c. HB 1491, from the staff deck rather than the headline

*HB 1491 Code & MFTE Changes*, Patrick Quinton and Chim Chune Ko, 10 August 2026.

The law applies within a **quarter-mile walkshed of Bus Rapid Transit stations** and mandates
**50-year affordability**: 10% of units at 60% AMI, or 20% at 80% AMI, or 10% at 80% AMI where at
least a tenth are two-bedroom or larger. Cities must offer offsets — a 20-year MFTE exemption, a
50% impact fee reduction, minimum FAR, density bonuses, no parking minimums.

Vancouver's objection is specific, and better than "bad policy":

> **"HB 1491 is partially funded inclusionary zoning"** — 50 years of required affordability
> against at most 20 years of offset. Staff argue the other incentives are *already available
> citywide*, so they offset nothing at the margin; that partially funded IZ lowers returns,
> **decreases overall production and directs development away from transit**; and that blanket
> requirements *"undermine the City's redevelopment plans by discouraging mixed income master
> plan projects."*

Scale: **Vancouver has 51 stations along two existing Vine BRT routes** — one of the few
jurisdictions outside Puget Sound with high-capacity transit, so a law written for Sound Transit
light rail lands unusually hard here. The partial fix is exclusions: areas already zoned with
minimum FAR such as downtown, plus about 25% of parcels near stations.

## 32ca. The 2025 Housing Report — the sharpest fact in the project

*2025 Housing Report*, Economic Prosperity and Housing, March 2026. Cached at
`data/vancouver-wa/housing-report-2025.pdf`.

> **"Despite a strong pipeline of shovel ready projects, no Vancouver projects received Housing
> Trust Fund awards in 2025."**

§13 of this document priced Vancouver's *per-capita share* of a record state Housing Trust Fund at
**$7.6M/yr** and treated it as a floor. **The actual 2025 award was zero.** The report adds that
the shortfall came *"despite significant population growth and documented need in Southwest
Washington"*, and that the result is *"a bottleneck of viable projects that must wait or secure
other resources before starting construction."*

That is the single most important correction the record makes. The state money in §13 is not a
share Vancouver receives; it is a competitive award it can lose entirely, and did.

**On production**, the report confirms Part I from the city's own side: *"The City continued to
fall short of annual housing goals. Fewer than half of the targeted housing units were completed
in 2025."* Fewer than half — against the 44% of need this document computes from Census permit
data. The attributed causes are elevated construction costs, tariffs, sustained high interest
rates and stagnating rent growth.

**On the outlook**, the report is bleaker than this analysis was. Rents are forecast to grow *well
below inflation* through 2026–27, which is good for renters and removes the incentive to build:
*"the underlying gap between housing supply and demand is likely to persist — and may worsen over
time."*

**And on the remedy, the city gets to §18b's conclusion first, and says it in print:**

> *"Achieving these goals will likely require a major shift in regional market conditions or a
> significant increase in public investment, **including an expanded role for publicly led and
> non-market rate housing development** to help offset reduced private-sector activity."*

Every argument in Part VI and §18b about public development and accumulation is already the
published position of the City of Vancouver's housing department. This analysis did not reach it
first; it reached it independently, and with the arithmetic attached.

## 32cb. The Affordable Housing Fund's first seven years

*2017–2023 Affordable Housing Fund Report*, March 2024. Cached alongside.

The original $42M levy produced **709 new affordable units, 339 preserved, rental assistance for

## 32cc. The Draft EIS closes the capacity question — and the consultants confirm Part II

The 14-month agenda sweep found the **Draft Environmental Impact Statement** (12.4MB, presented
4 August 2025) and the *Getting to a Preferred Land Use Alternative* deck. Both are now cached.

**On capacity, Part VII's one "unverified" row is resolved.** The DEIS reports:

| Alternative | New housing unit capacity |
|---|---:|
| No Action | capacity only, *no* feasibility adjustment |
| Alternative 1 | **~45,100** — *"based on market feasibility and redevelopment potential"* |
| Alternative 2 | ~50,700 — same basis |

The adopted plan's **44,112** sits just below Alternative 1, which is what a refinement of the
preferred alternative should look like. And the methodology table answers the specific worry:
the residential market factor is **"N/A (embedded in market feasibility analysis)"** — meaning
the analysis does not apply a flat 15–50% haircut because it ran a feasibility study instead. The
employment analysis, by contrast, does apply an explicit 10% market factor and redevelopment
rates. **Capacity is feasibility-adjusted. That row can come off the watch list.**

**And the consultants state Part II's finding directly.** The DEIS, citing ECONorthwest's *Housing
Needs Assessment* (2024b):

> *"…certain lower-income bands are likely unable to be served without additional subsidies for
> housing. Development of regulated affordable housing can face a number of challenges and
> typically requires **sizable gap financing** to make projects feasible."*

The same document independently states the production requirement: *"an estimated **1,733 housing
units** need to be built."* That is this analysis's Part I figure, from the consultant's own
assessment — which also settles the 1,733 vs 2,500 question in §32f: **1,733 is the plan and EIS
figure; 2,500 is the city's separate operational Housing Report target.** Both are the city's; they
answer different questions.

## 32cd. Appendix P — the document flagged as the largest gap, now in hand

Every appendix is published on the engagement site's **project-documents** page
(`beheardvancouver.civilspace.io/en/projects/project-documents`) — not on the city's own comp plan
page, which links only the appendix *list*. That is why it was never found. Appendices **D, E, O
and P** are now cached.

**Appendix P** (ECOnorthwest for WSP, 2 April 2026) confirms every figure in Part II and supplies
the derivation this analysis never had:

> HAPT identifies a total need of **43,198 units 2020–2045**. Subtracting the **5,069 units built
> 2020–2023** gives **38,129 units from 2023 to 2045**. Allocation **Method A**.

That is exactly the target used here, and it confirms the Method A choice made in §12 from Clark
County's Issue Paper 5. Its income breakdown matches Part II line for line — 1,405 shelter beds,
2,600 PSH, 4,423 below 30%, 5,890 at 30–50%, 5,788 at 50–80%, 3,351, 3,175 and 12,902 — and it
states the **5,983-unit net capacity surplus**, with a surplus "for all income levels".

Two details worth having:

- **Existing emergency capacity is 406 beds against 1,405 needed.** §9 used the Clark County
  Housing Inventory Chart's 437 year-round beds; the plan's own figure is 406. Same order, and the
  3.2× expansion finding is unchanged.
- **The plan assumes ADUs deliver almost nothing.** There are **53,269 lots eligible** for an ADU,
  but the analysis forecasts **1,280 ADUs through 2045** — 64 a year, against a recent actual of
  32 a year. That is under 3% of the 44,112 capacity. Whatever else the plan is optimistic about,
  it is not middle-housing uptake.

**Appendix O** explains the density assumptions Part I took on trust: eighteen housing prototypes
built from current development trends and state legislation, each with a dwelling-units-per-acre
value, assigned to zoning districts and run through a **housing feasibility analysis**. So the
"achieved densities" behind the capacity figure are modelled from feasible prototypes, not
theoretical maxima — which is the second half of the answer to Part VII's capacity flag.

**Appendix D**, the ECOnorthwest Housing Needs Assessment, independently states the same
production requirement: *"an estimated **1,733 housing units**"* a year, noting it is an equal
distribution across the planning period. That is the third independent source for Part I's central
number, after the comp plan and the DEIS.

## 32e. The city's housing director states Part II's finding, in his own words

> *"The affordable housing pipeline is one of our biggest frustrations. **We have eight existing
> projects with over 630 units that cannot move forward.**"*
> — Patrick Quinton, City of Vancouver housing and economic director, August 2026

The reported cause is Washington's budget shortfall: nonprofit developers are waiting longer than
ever for the state grants that make deals pencil, and city Affordable Housing Fund money already
committed to those projects is stranded alongside them. **Some commitments expire as soon as May**
if developers cannot close their remaining financing.

That is §6 and §14 of this document, from the inside. It is not a finding this analysis
discovered — it is a finding the city has been reporting publicly, and the analysis converges on
it from the arithmetic. **630 stalled units against the 610/yr this document calculates as
unfunded is close enough to be worth stating plainly.**

## 32f. Where the record corrects this analysis

**The bottleneck is nearer-term and more specific than the framing here.** Part II treats the
problem as a structural shortfall in aggregate subsidy. Staff's diagnosis is sharper: there is a
*designed, permitted, city-funded pipeline* that cannot close because **state gap financing has
dried up**. Those are not the same problem. The structural gap is real, but the thing actually
stopping units in 2026 is the state capital budget, and a document that leads with 22-year
arithmetic buries that.

**The city's own production target is higher than the one used here.** This document uses
**1,733/yr** — the plan's 38,129 units over 22 years. The city's 2025 Housing Report uses
**2,500/yr**, and a city official put it as *"the pace of housing development needs to double."*
The two are not contradictory — 1,733 is net new units from the plan's allocation, while 2,500
appears to be an operational figure including replacement and vacancy — but Part I's "+14% to
+27% uplift" is the gentler of the two framings, and the city uses the harder one.

**The Affordable Housing Fund is being restructured right now.** At a council workshop on
10 August 2026, staff proposed to:

| | Current | Proposed |
|---|---:|---:|
| Units directly supported per year | 80 | **40** |
| Total production goal per year | 240 | 215 |
| Ten-year total | 2,400 | 2,150 |
| New pre-development loan pool | — | **$3M** |
| Maximum city contribution per unit | — | raised |

This is a **proposal, not yet adopted** — but the direction is exactly what §6's arithmetic
predicts when per-unit cost rises against a capped revenue stream: fewer units, more subsidy each.

**Two figures the record independently corroborates.** The 2023 replacement levy is
**$0.30 per $1,000** of assessed value — against the $0.289 this document derives from budget
lines in §8, a 4% gap consistent with the assessment lag already flagged there. And the fund has
awarded **$67.6M to 2,034 units since 2016**, or about $33,000 of city money per unit — close to
the $40,000 estimated in §7 from the prior levy.

## 32g. A new risk this analysis did not have

**HB 1491 (2025)** requires developers to offer affordable units for at least 50 years near light
rail and bus rapid transit stops. Vancouver officials have publicly described it as *"good
intentions but bad policy"* and warned it could **impede** the multifamily development the
comprehensive plan depends on; staff say they may have a partial fix.

Whatever the merits, it is a live downside risk to Part I's production forecast that postdates
every source used here — and it cuts against the assumption that state action on housing is
uniformly additive.

## 32h. What this changes about how to read the rest

Nothing in Parts I–VIII is retracted. But the framing should be adjusted:

- **Staff are not unaware of any of this.** The gap, the stalled pipeline, the state funding
  problem and the production shortfall are all documented in the city's own materials and
  reported in the local press. This analysis is a quantification of a problem the city is already
  working, not a discovery.
- **The near-term binding constraint is state gap financing**, not local instrument design.
  §18b's argument about seeding a portfolio remains valid, but it is a five-year answer to a
  problem that is currently expiring in months.
- **The place to test anything bolder is the city**, which is already the venue for this work.

# Part IX — Continuation roadmap

## 29. What would sharpen this

- **The council record itself — now the largest gap.** Workshop packets, staff presentations and
  consultant reports behind the August 2026 coverage, and the many earlier housing agenda items.
  The agenda portal is a CivicClerk SPA; it needs the UI export or a human with the browser open.
  Everything else on this list is smaller than this.
- **Appendix P, *Housing Needs and Land Capacity Analysis*,** is referenced throughout Chapter 4
  and was not in the appendix bundle retrieved. It contains the "Aligning Vancouver Housing
  Needs with County Allocation" section and the underlying capacity method. It would resolve
  the 36,527 vs 38,129 discrepancy and the assumptions behind the 44,112 capacity figure.
- **The Final EIS** (expected later in 2026) will contain per-alternative capacity and
  absorption modelling that would test Part I's absorption arithmetic directly.
- **Middle housing production specifically.** BPS 2-unit and 3–4-unit columns are the best
  available proxy and they are small (42 units in 2025). Vancouver adopted interim HB 1110
  standards before the full code; separating middle-housing permits from the city's own permit
  data would show whether the RIP analogue holds here.
- **Intra-metro filtering variation.** Liu et al. find filtering varies more *within* MSAs than
  between them, and estimate ZIP-level rates. Vancouver WA ZIPs specifically may differ from the
  Portland-dominated MSA average in either direction. This is the biggest single uncertainty in
  Part III.
- **Annualised homelessness, not PIT.** Every number in §17 is built on a one-night count.
  HMIS annual counts typically run 3–4× PIT. The Housing First cost figures should be treated
  as floors.
- **Clark County's own share.** This analysis is city-only. The county allocation is 103,698
  units countywide, of which Vancouver is 35%. Unincorporated Clark plus Vancouver UGA carries
  44,038, with 22,759 below 80% AMI — a larger absolute problem with a weaker tax base.
- **Stockholm's *ombildning* conversions are not quantified here.** The 25% → 17% national
  figure is sourced, but the split between municipal-company sales, co-op conversions and
  differential new construction is not. That decomposition would sharpen §24 considerably.
- **A residence-based versus employment-based payroll levy.** §25 prices an employment-based
  levy because that is what the wage data supports. Given how many Clark County residents work
  in Oregon, the two bases differ substantially and the gap is not estimated here. This is the
  largest single uncertainty in Part VI.
- **Vienna's leverage ratio is inferred, not sourced directly.** The 2.12× comes from dividing a
  reported €530M of annual spend by a reported €250M of levy receipts. Both figures are from
  secondary reporting and are not from the same document. The direction is solid; the second
  decimal is not.
- **Austria's federal transfer to Vienna is not the whole levy.** The 1% is collected nationally
  and distributed among the Länder. §25 applies the *rate* to Washington wages, not Vienna's
  net receipt, so the two are not directly comparable as shares of local payroll.
- **What happened in 2025.** The 49% collapse in permits from the 2021 peak is unexplained here.
  Interest rates, construction costs, and the pre-effective-date pause on the new code are all
  candidates. It matters a great deal whether it is cyclical or structural.

## 30. Gotchas worth knowing before you continue

- **Vancouver WA vs Vancouver BC.** Every search silently returns BC results. Always qualify
  with "WA" or "Clark County". Likewise **Clark County WA vs Clark County NV** — searches for
  the Point-in-Time count return Las Vegas.
- **The comprehensive plan contradicts itself on the housing unit count.** The Community
  Overview box on page 4 says "Housing Units: 71,649". The housing element uses 86,878 for 2023
  and OFM counts 90,780 in April 2025. Use 86,878 — it is the number every calculation in
  Chapter 4 is built on. The 71,649 appears nowhere else and is not reconcilable.
- **36,527 is the County allocation; 38,129 is the City's target.** They are both correct and
  they are not the same number. Chapter 4 footnote 23 explains why: the City ran its analysis on
  the Commerce HAPT tool before receiving the County allocation.
- **Capacity is a stock, production is a flow.** The plan's "surplus of 5,983 units" is a
  statement about the stock. It is not evidence about the flow and should never be quoted as if
  it were.
- **The AHF's 320 units/yr goal and the plan's 325 units/yr of programme delivery are largely
  the same units counted twice.** AHF money layers on top of tax credits; the "240 leveraged
  units" in the AHF plan are mostly the same LIHTC units in the comprehensive plan's Table 6.
  Do not add them.
- **Rosenthal's 2.5% and Liu et al.'s 0.42% are not in conflict.** Rosenthal's is
  rental-specific and national; Liu et al.'s is all-tenure and MSA-resolved. Both are correct
  about different things. Quoting 2.5% as if it applied to Portland–Vancouver is the error.
- **Liu et al.'s appendix Table A4 has the same three-numeric-column shape as Table A1.** Any
  script parsing the PDF must bound A1 at "Table A2" or it silently parses squared residuals as
  filtering rates. The model in `filtering_model.py` does this; an earlier version did not, and
  reported a national mean filtering rate of +25%/yr.
- **Sign convention in Liu et al.: negative means downward filtering** (getting more affordable).
  Positive means upward. It is easy to get backwards.
- **$400,000/unit is a subsidy figure, not a cost figure.** It exceeds Clark County median TDC
  because deep-affordability projects carry soft costs and reserves and support no debt. Do not
  compare it directly to a construction cost per unit.
- **PSH is counted in beds in the inventory and units in the plan.** 885 existing beds vs 2,600
  needed units is not a clean ratio. Commerce's own HAPT documentation flags the same problem.
- **The Vienna per-capita comparison is spend-against-gap, not spend-against-spend.** €530M/yr
  is Vienna's whole housing outlay; $237M/yr is Vancouver's *unfunded* gap on top of what it
  already spends. The comparison is still meaningful — it shows the orders of magnitude — but it
  is not a like-for-like budget comparison and should not be quoted as one.
- **The 59× Finland ratio is deliberately not like-for-like.** €270M of Finnish Housing First
  capital buys placements into stock that already exists. Vancouver's $52.2M/yr includes
  building the stock. Quoting the ratio as evidence that Finland is 59× more efficient inverts
  the point being made.
- **The Sweden section was rewritten after a challenge, and the earlier version is wrong.**
  It claimed a *structural* result — that a self-financing public developer drifts up the income
  bands — and dated the decline to 2011. Both were wrong: essentially all of the decline predates
  the 2011 Act, and the Act's trigger was an EU state-aid complaint resolved by negotiation in
  which the Tenants' Union preserved universality. If you are quoting an earlier copy of this
  document, don't.
- **Sweden appears twice and points both ways.** The Million Homes Programme is the largest
  social housing success in the OECD; the 1991–2010 sell-off is the clearest case of one being
  dismantled. Quoting either half alone misrepresents the case — which is what the earlier draft
  did.
- **The EU state-aid constraint is the one European barrier that does not transfer.** Do not
  import it into a US argument, in either direction.
- **Singapore and Tokyo are not policy templates and are not offered as ones.** Singapore's
  mechanism is unavailable under US constitutional law; Japan's requires removing local land use
  discretion entirely. They are in the document to isolate mechanisms, not to be copied.
- **The city levy cap is $3.60/$1,000 only for cities with a pre-LEOFF firefighters' pension
  fund**; the base is $3.375. Vancouver's headroom is computed at $3.60 here, which is the
  generous reading. At $3.375 the headroom is $35.1M/yr, not $43.4M.
