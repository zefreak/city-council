### The Madrona vote: what the minutes say happened

From the 25 August special meeting minutes, item 3:

> "The commission held a vote to direct the President to sign the engagement letter for contract
> with Madrona Law, PLLC for CAHC legal counsel; **contingent on consent from commissioner Bennett
> to ensure quorum.** The pending motion was made by Gaspar, seconded by Tan, and unanimously
> approved. **The motion was later approved by Bennett and unanimously passed.**"

Bennett had left before the action items — the minutes say so, "due to a scheduling conflict".
President Lewis was absent throughout. So the body voted knowing it was short of quorum, then
obtained the missing member's assent outside the meeting.

**Why this matters and what the rule is.** The Open Public Meetings Act, RCW 42.30, requires final
action by a governing body to be taken at a meeting open to the public. A member who is not at the
meeting is not present to vote, and collecting an absent member's assent afterwards to validate a
vote is the pattern the OPMA is written against. The minutes are the only record here and they do
not say *how* Bennett's approval was obtained, or whether the commission reconvened.

**I am not asserting the retention is void** — that is a question for the City Attorney, and the
minutes may be describing something more careful than they read. The point is narrower and it is
actionable: **the minutes as drafted describe a defective vote, and the commission is being asked
to approve those minutes as item 2a.** The clean fix is to re-take the vote at a meeting with a
quorum. Asking for that, in the Community Forum before item 2 is reached, costs nothing and
removes the problem.

**And the member whose after-the-fact consent carried it is no longer on the commission.** The
10 September agenda masthead lists nine slots — Lewis (President), *vacant* Vice President,
*vacant*, Gaspar, Mendoza, Tan, Harris, Ford (CACC), *vacant*. **Bennett is gone.** Bennett was on
the 25 August roster. Nothing in the packet announces the departure.

### The other two open questions, answered

**Is the Commission a public development authority?** The minutes now record an answer:
"Perlick provided background on the City Attorney's Office determined that CAHC operates as a
separate legal entity as a Public Development Authority." So the City Attorney has made that
determination. **The minutes cite no creating ordinance.** A PDA is chartered by ordinance under
RCW 35.21.730–.759 and can hold money, contract and sue; that is a much larger claim about a
volunteer arts commission than the phrase in an engagement letter suggested, and the ordinance
number is the one thing that would settle it. Ask for it.

**Is there a cap?** No. Gaspar asked directly whether there is a budget limit for communication
with Madrona. The minutes record the answer as: "Perlick acknowledged that billing rates will be
investment for Madrona to complete initial research, review agendas, and **attend every meeting**.
It was agreed that the commission check in after a quarter to assess if the services are meeting
commission needs." A quarterly check-in is not a cap. The 25 August brief's finding stands: an
open-ended hourly engagement, lead attorney at $340/hour, with attendance at every meeting built
in. What is new is who pays — "**the City is responsible for the contract invoices**" — so this is
City money, not a separate CAHC account, and it is not visible in any published budget line found
here.

## Money and land

### The Cultural Access Tax, from the City's own numbers

Ordinance **M-4481**, passed unanimously by City Council on **2 December 2024**, established
Inspire Vancouver and adopted a **0.1% local sales tax** under RCW 36.160, authorised for **seven
years**, renewal possible in 2031. Source: the City's own programme site,
`inspirevancouver.org/cultural-access-tax`, retrieved 4 September 2026 and cached at
`data/vancouver/cultural-access/`. The City states the cost as "~1 penny per $10 spent, or **$30
per year for the average household**" and expected revenue as "up to **$7 million** for seven
years."

> **This is a third 0.1% sales tax, and it is not either of the other two.** Vancouver's
> Transportation Benefit District tax and the public safety tax under RCW 82.14.345 are separate
> instruments with separate authorities. Do not merge them.

**It was adopted by council action, not a public vote.** RCW 36.160 permits either. Council chose
the councilmanic route for a regressive tax with a seven-year life.

**The 10% administration pledge is measured against revenue, not against spending.** The programme
site says "10% → Administrative costs… 90% → Distributed directly to eligible organizations."
$700,000 is exactly 10% of the $7m projected revenue. But because only $3.85m of that $7m is being
spent in year one, **administration is 18.2% of the money actually moving** — and the $700,000
"flexible" line is not committed to grants either. Competitive grants proper — Impact, Innovation
and Comprehensive — come to **$1,750,000**, or 45% of the year-one allocation and 24% of the
revenue. That is not a scandal; front-loaded setup costs against a seven-year programme are
normal, and the carryforward stays in the fund. It is worth stating precisely because the
programme's public framing does not.

## Watch

### The fiscal-sponsor eligibility question was deferred, and the narrow reading governs cycle one

This is the item with the clearest distributional effect and it is the one that got put off. Policy
says a fiscal sponsor "must have a mission aligned with advancing or preserving arts, culture,
heritage, or science." Staff put the interpretation to the commission on 13 August:

- **Narrow** — the sponsor's *primary purpose* must be arts/culture/heritage/science; or
- **Broad** — the sponsor must *do* such activities but may have a different primary purpose.

Staff's own listed impact: "Asking groups who already have a mission-aligned fiscal sponsor to find
a new sponsor could limit program accessibility," and "orgs that already offer fiscal sponsorship
could have an advantage." Commissioners discussed the broader reading. President Lewis proposed
postponing to the next cycle "since the current cycle is now open for applications", adding "that
legal representation for CAHC will be available in October." The minutes close: "It was determined
that a motion to change fiscal sponsorship to a broader interpretation can be revisited at a later
time, if necessary."

**So the narrow reading stands for a $1.75m grant round closing 10 September.** The groups this
excludes are exactly the ones a left reader cares about: a cultural project carried by a
neighbourhood association, a tenants' organisation, a mutual aid group, a church, a labour
council — an established sponsor whose primary purpose is not "arts." Those applicants must find a
new sponsor inside the application window or not apply. The advantage runs to the handful of
incumbent arts organisations already set up to sponsor, who also collect the 10%.

Note the argument on the other side, because it is real: opening sponsorship widely in a pilot
round pushes public money through organisations with no grant-administration track record, and the
staff memo flags capacity gaps as its own risk. The defensible answer is a published exceptions
route for this cycle, not a rule change mid-round. **Item 4b, "Updates on Inspire Vancouver Grant
Approval Process", is where to raise it** — and it has no attachment, so nobody outside the room
knows what the update is.
