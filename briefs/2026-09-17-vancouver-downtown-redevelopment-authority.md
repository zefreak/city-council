## Bottom line

**The board is asked to ratify $3.72 million in claims and approve a $116,500 architecture
contract that starts the renovation of every guest room in the publicly-owned Hilton — and buried
at page 23 of the packet is a note recording that in January 2026 the City delayed transfers of
pledged lodging tax and sales tax credit revenues to the DRA, that the transfers only completed
after the DRA notified its bond trustee, and that the City then rewrote the DRA's bylaws in March
to take the appointment of its Executive Director away from the board.** That is a governance
fight over bond-pledged public revenue, disclosed in a financial statement note, at an 11am
quarterly board meeting with no in-meeting public comment.

And the phasing, from Attachment A:

|---|---|

**So: a $116,500 design contract is the front door to a whole-hotel renovation whose total cost

and it has not been priced in anything published here. The 2026 capital projects budget is
**$4,903,500**, against **$211,194** actually spent to 31 August (4.3%), which tells you the
renovation money is budgeted and unspent, not that it is small.

**One detail to ask about.** The schedule in the proposal lists, among "anticipated meetings and
site visits", *"(1) Initial fact-finding site survey (Kick-off Meeting, **07/29/2026**)"* — a date
seven weeks before the board approves the contract and nine weeks before its term begins. That may
simply be how the firm wrote its proposal after an RFQ site visit. It is also the shape of work
beginning ahead of authorisation, and the board is entitled to ask which it is.

Project schedule as proposed: notice to proceed 1 Oct 2026 → 90% construction documents 5 Feb 2027
→ procurement 23 weeks → construction start **July 2027** → completion **October 2027**.

### Item 7a — the Capital Asset Policy comes back with a specific unfinished question

The board **adopted** the Capital Asset Policy on 16 July (SR 07.16.26.02). The minutes in this
packet record an action item: President Robertson questioned whether the **$10,000 capitalisation
threshold** made sense when the DRA holds items at $8,500; Jacobson said sub-threshold items get
expensed at year end; Bernatz asked whether that year-end reconciliation is actually written into
the policy; Jacobson *"thought yes but needs to confirm."*

> *"Action item: Harder/Jacobson to review and possibly revise for approval at the September 17,
> 2026 board meeting."*

So "next steps" means a possible amendment on the reconciliation language. Small, but it is the
rule set governing how a public authority capitalises and **disposes of** public property — and
the 2025 financial statements disclose that during 2025 the DRA **disposed of 29 of its 79 booked
assets** (19 no longer in use, 10 below threshold) with a historical cost of $1,923,658.73, all
fully depreciated, so no gain or loss recognised.

### The governance note nobody will read — packet p. 23, Note 3.C "Subsequent Events"

Quoted as printed:

> *"In January 2026, the City of Vancouver's Chief Financial Officer, who had been appointed by the
> Vancouver Downtown Redevelopment Authority (DRA) Board to serve as Executive Director, was
> terminated from employment with the City. Following this action, **transfers of pledged lodging
> tax revenues and Clark County Public Facilities District sales tax credit revenues were
> temporarily delayed by City management. The transfers were subsequently completed after the DRA
> issued a notification to the bond trustee.** In addition, communications between City staff
> providing accounting and administrative support and DRA management, as contemplated under the
> interlocal agreement, were temporarily disrupted. To maintain operational continuity, the DRA
> Board entered into a contract for Executive Director services with the former City Chief
> Financial Officer.*
>
> *In March 2026, the City of Vancouver approved an ordinance amending the DRA's bylaws to require
> that the Executive Director be an **at-will employee of the City**. Previously, the DRA Board
> retained sole authority to appoint or contract for these services. On Thursday, April 16, 2026,
> the Downtown Redevelopment Authority accepted and approved the City of Vancouver's nomination for
> Executive Director for the DRA. On Friday April 17, 2026, the City Manager for the City of
> Vancouver emailed the President of the DRA Board confirming the appointment of the City appointee,
> which procedurally placed the individual as the official Executive Director."*

**What this says, stated flatly.** The City sacked its CFO. The CFO was also the DRA's board-chosen
Executive Director. The City then withheld revenues it is contractually obliged to pass through —
revenues **pledged to bondholders** — and released them only once the DRA escalated to the bond
trustee. The board hired the sacked CFO on contract to keep the lights on. Two months later the
City Council amended the DRA's bylaws so that the Executive Director must be a City employee
serving at will, ending the board's independent appointment power, and the board then accepted the
City's nominee.

**Why it belongs in a left brief, in both directions.** The DRA is an unelected seven-member
appointed board that owns a hotel and controls $19.5 million of restricted public cash; more
democratic control over it is not obviously a bad thing, and an argument that the elected City
Council should direct it has real force. But the mechanism here was not a public policy debate
about accountability — it was a revenue stoppage that touched bond covenants, resolved by a
trustee notification, followed by a bylaw amendment. Those are different things, and the second
does not become legitimate because the first was uncomfortable. **What is missing is any public
record of the argument.** The March ordinance went through the City Council; the January revenue
delay appears nowhere except a footnote in an audited financial statement published eight months
later, in a quarterly board packet.

The board's own conclusion: *"DRA Management, including the Board, have evaluated these subsequent
events and determined that no adjustments to the accompanying financial statements are required.
These matters do not affect the presentation of the DRA as a separate municipal corporation for
financial reporting purposes."*

The financial consequence is visible in the claims list: the July minutes record legal expenses
*"higher due to increased activity regarding unexpected staffing changes and having to obtain
outside legal representation with higher rates."* Kenyon Disend PLLC now sits as DRA counsel, and
the board on 16 July approved a legal contract (SR 07.16.26.01) with a 24-month not-to-exceed
amount — discussed but **not stated in the minutes**. Board member Pace asked whether the board
has direct independent access to counsel given the contract says counsel *"works under the
direction of the Executive Director"*; President Robertson answered that the board as a body has
direct access, individual members do not, and the Executive Director — now a City at-will employee
— *"is included in matters/discussions involving his position."*

| **Collections as % of cap** | **180.7%** | was 133.4% |

The cap fell $721,000 under the 2013 Refinancing Debt Indenture, and it changes each year through
2034. **The 2026 cap was met on 12 May.** Everything collected since is excess: $1,467,248.01 was
applied to the Clark County PFD loan, and **that loan was paid in full on 3 August 2026**. The
remaining balance shown as "$28.95" is twenty-eight dollars and ninety-five cents, a rounding
artefact of payment timing, not $28.95 million. Since 3 August, excess revenue flows to the City
PFD instead — **$352,764.17 so far**.

**The point.** Three public tax streams — a 2% city lodging tax and two state sales tax credits —
collect over $4 million a year against a repayment obligation capped at $2.25 million and now
largely discharged, and the surplus has built to $19.5 million in escrow against $4.05 million of
required balances. The DRA's 2025 statements record a surplus net fund position of $5.11 million.
On 1 September the DRA moved another $1.17 million of excess cash into debt service. Both January
2027 debt service payments are fully funded.

**Where this does and does not connect to housing.** It is tempting to set $19.5 million against
the city's affordable housing pipeline, and the contrast is real: `context/HOUSING-README.md`
records eight Vancouver projects with over 630 units stalled for want of state gap financing and
**zero** Housing Trust Fund awards to Vancouver projects in 2025. But do not claim this money is
available. These are trustee-held funds under a 2013 bond indenture with defined flow-of-funds
requirements; "Authority Reserve Fund – Excess" is a defined account under that indenture, not
spare cash. The honest claim is narrower and still worth making: **a public authority's dedicated
tax streams are now running at 180% of their capped obligation, the borrowing they were pledged
against is paid off, and the question of what the surplus is for is not being asked in public.**
That question belongs to the City Council, which appoints this board and which amended its bylaws
in March.
