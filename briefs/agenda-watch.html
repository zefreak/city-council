Consent — first reading · public hearing 21 September

Camping banned at all hours within 1,500 feet of the Bridge Shelter

An ordinance amending VMC 8.22.030 and 8.22.040(B) adds a new first entry to the city’s list of “camping and outside habitation impact areas”: within 1,500 feet of the nearest point of the Bridge Shelter property at 5313 NE 94th Avenue. Inside an impact area, camping and outside habitation are unlawful at all times. Everywhere else, VMC 8.22.040(C)–(D) restricts camping in parks, streets and public lots only between 6:30am and 9:30pm — so what this removes, inside the line, is the lawful overnight window.

A 1,500-foot radius is 162 acres, a quarter of a square mile, and the buffer is measured from the parcel boundary rather than from a point, so the real footprint is larger. Staff evaluated 1,000, 1,500 and 2,000 feet and chose 1,500 because 1,000 “would not encompass several nearby locations where significant camping and outside habitation currently occurs.” That sentence is the honest one: the buffer is drawn to reach existing camps, not only to protect the shelter door.

What the staff report concedes, verbatim: the amendment “would reduce the public area available for lawful nighttime camping and will cause people currently camping within the proposed buffer who decline shelter placement to relocate to other locations.” Budget impact is recorded as none, for an ordinance whose own challenge list anticipates signage, mapping, outreach, cleanup and enforcement.

The arithmetic to put on the record. The shelter adds 120 beds, and another roughly 70-bed shelter already operates within 1,000 feet — so that shelter’s surroundings fall inside the new ban too. Staff state the Bridge Shelter “will not provide sufficient capacity to shelter every person experiencing unsheltered homelessness in Vancouver.” Nowhere does the packet say how many people currently camp inside the 1,500-foot line, so the question the ordinance turns on cannot be answered from it.

The strongest version of the city’s case, stated fairly: this is not a bare sweep. The restriction takes effect as 120 beds open; HART and partner outreach continue before and after; the ordinance is site-specific and changes neither the existing 1,000-foot Safe Stay buffers nor the citywide day/night rules. Vancouver built the shelter first, which is not the usual sequence. The criticism belongs on the gap between 120 beds and the displaced population — and on the fact that several of the camps the buffer reaches sit on WSDOT rights-of-way, state land now closed to overnight use with no WSDOT agreement anywhere in the packet.

Two openings, not one. Ask a councilmember to pull it from consent on 14 September; testify at the second reading and public hearing on 21 September.

The good part, plainly. Permanent supportive housing for households exiting homelessness, senior housing with a 20% homelessness set-aside, family housing with a 20% disability set-aside. The problem being solved is not of the city’s making: only one Vancouver project has won a State Housing Trust Fund award in two years, leaving 564 prospective units stuck. State HTF applications close 21 September, a week after this vote, which is why the new Hacienda/YWCA award is conditioned on winning HTF and expires if it does not. The timing is deliberate and the item is not padding.

The thing to look at. The awards are funded with $7.5m of production money plus $2m moved out of the AHF’s rental assistance category. The stated reason is administrative — “the current slow pace of spending observed in the rental assistance category.” Nothing in the packet explains why the 2026 rental assistance money went unspent, and “slow to spend” and “not needed” are different findings. Rental assistance is the only AHF category that reaches a household at the bottom of the market this year; production at 30–60% AMI reaches tenants in 2028 and later.

Smith Tower lost. HDC-NW / Mid-Columbia Manor asked for more to preserve 170 units of occupied downtown senior housing and got nothing, because “its cost per unit is significantly higher… due to the challenges of retrofitting a circular building.” Screening preservation against new production on cost-per-unit is defensible and consequential: those 170 households are already housed at a rent the market will not reproduce.

Two numbers in one packet. The staff report says all projects serve households at 30–60% AMI; the resolution’s own recital says at or below 50% AMI. The resolution is the operative document. And the money concentrates: three of the six projects are in the Heights, carrying $14.1m of the $20.65m committed (68%) and 234 of 403 units — the same district whose infrastructure design contract is consent item 4.

Discussion — no vote scheduled, and the memo understates both

The two November statewide initiatives, and what the attached texts actually say

Under “From the City Manager”, requested by Council on 10 August. The memo notes its summaries come from Ballotpedia; the full initiative texts are attached and were read for this brief. Both contain material the summaries do not. If Council is going to take a position by resolution, this is where it starts, and Community Communication is the public’s only slot on it.

IL26-638 — school sports. Beyond the headline ban, two things. The verification requirement is universal, not targeted: §3(2) requires any student electing to play a female-only sport to submit, with the routine sports physical, a statement from their health care provider verifying biological sex by “reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or normal endogenously produced testosterone levels” — new medical paperwork for every girl who plays a school sport in Washington, heaviest on families without a regular provider. And §4 carves an exception into the state’s anti-sex-discrimination statute itself, amending RCW 28A.640.020(1)(c) — athletics “offered to all students without regard to sex” — to read “except as provided in section 3 of this act.”

IL26-001 — “Parents’ Bill of Rights”. It strikes subsections (1)–(4) of RCW 28A.605.005 in their entirety and substitutes a new list, so anything not restated is gone. Gone: the right to schooling in a setting where discrimination on the basis of sex, race, creed, religion, colour, national origin, veteran status, sexual orientation, gender expression, gender identity or disability is prohibited — the nearest replacement protects only against discrimination “based upon the sincerely held religious beliefs of the child’s family.” Gone: the right to file a harassment, intimidation and bullying complaint on a child’s behalf. Gone: annual notice of the school’s language access services under Title VI.

Two more the memo does not mention. The records shield narrows: current law lets a school withhold “health care, social work, counseling, or disciplinary records” from a parent who is a criminal defendant where the student is the named victim or the target of an abuse investigation — the replacement covers only medical and mental-health counselling records, only during an abuse investigation, and deletes the criminal-defendant protection entirely. And the clause “Nothing in this section creates a private right of action” is struck and not replaced, alongside a new section directing that the act “be liberally construed.” The initiative’s own §1 states that passage “provides two years of constitutional protection from legislative interference.”

Workshop 4:00pm — no vote, and this is where it gets shaped

Development fees: below state average, a $1.6bn capital programme behind them, and a menu that includes “pause”

Vancouver’s current fees, from the city’s own modelled projects: a 198-unit mixed-use residential building pays $14,929 per unit (2.87% of development cost); a 100-unit three-storey multifamily building pays $15,347 per unit (3.65%). Proposed: update traffic impact fees and water/sewer SDCs, add engineering review and inspection fees, index everything to CPI — and add a commercial park impact fee, because commercial and industrial development currently pays none. That last one is the straightforwardly good item here, and it is scheduled last, in 2028.

The capital programme behind it is specific: a $300m PFAS programme plus a $400m 20-year water CIP, a $300m sewer solids renewal plus $250m sewer CIP, a $345m transportation capital budget (32nd Street Bypass $86m, 59th Street $23m), and 800+ acres of park acquisition against a $166m parks plan. Slide 14: “The City risks underfunding necessary infrastructure.”

The framing to push back on. One slide is headed “Any increase in fees will make new development more expensive” and the options menu includes “pause new increases until market conditions improve.” Set that against the city’s own numbers: fees run 2–3% of development cost while total development cost per affordable unit went from $200k to $400k since 2016. A 3% fee is not the binding constraint. Staff are right that flat per-unit fees fall hardest on small middle-housing projects — and their own fix is on the same slide: “expand discounts and exemptions… affordable housing, child-care, transit-oriented development.” That is the sentence to hold them to, and October is when.

The mover nobody voted on. Between 2024 and 2026, fire permit and review fees rose 152% on the mixed-use project and 98% on the multifamily one, against single-digit moves everywhere else. Those are cost-recovery permit fees, not impact fees — but they are why “fees went up” is true, and they are not what this workshop is about. Timeline: traffic impact fee workshop October, Council action December, effective Q3 2027.

W2
Workshop — the ordinance hits consent on 5 October

Parking scofflaw programme: boots and impounds at four unpaid citations

Three things bundled. Degendering and modernising ~46 sections of VMC Title 19 — uncontroversial. Fines rise, first change since 2017: payment/overtime $25/$30 → $40/$40, prohibited parking $40 → $65 (+63%), improper plate $50 → $65. And a scofflaw programme adding immobilisation and impoundment at 4 or more citations unpaid for 45+ days.

The city’s numbers: 48% of fines issued in 2025–26 are still outstanding; over 2022–26, 1,110 vehicles would qualify — under 2% of cited vehicles but 62% of delinquent fines ($707,566); and 47% of unpaid prohibited and plate citations are on out-of-state or no-plate vehicles, the gap the programme exists to close. Process is not thin: a minimum of 15 notices over 62 days before impound, appeals and payment plans at every step, and an amnesty programme before it goes live in Q4.

The problem is who the residual population is. The presentation’s own “Common Issues” slide is three photographs — no licence plate, obscured VIN, defaced VIN. That is also the profile of a vehicle someone is living in. Vancouver already bans habitating a vehicle parked on any street, park or public lot between 6:30am and 9:30pm, so a vehicle resident accrues citations as a condition of existing, cannot pay them, and under this programme is booted and then towed. Losing the vehicle is losing the housing, and storage fees make recovery cost more than the original ticket. Nothing in the presentation mentions vehicle residency and no exemption is proposed.

Three asks, for October: an exemption or HART-referral diversion for vehicles in use as a residence; a genuine ability-to-pay standard on the payment plans rather than a schedule; and a count of how many of the 1,110 scofflaw vehicles are registered to people with no other address. Next steps: consent agenda 5 October, public hearing 12 October.

Quiet — one line at the bottom of a hearing block

A third extension freezes site-specific rezones into late 2027

Resolution 2026-09-01 extends the suspension of the annual site-specific comprehensive plan amendment cycle to 30 October 2026. The chronology, from the resolution's own recitals: the periodic update was statutorily due December 2025 under RCW 36.70A.130(5)(b) and is “now expected to be approved in mid-October 2026”. The freeze has run since 1 October 2023. Pre-applications reopen 31 October 2026 — but a final adopting ordinance can come “no sooner than one year after the adoption of the 2025–2045 comprehensive plan”, so no site-specific rezone can actually be granted until roughly late October 2027, four years after the freeze began.

It points both ways and the criticism belongs on the delay. Four years is a long time to close the only route by which a single parcel's designation can be corrected outside a once-a-decade update; the one alternative opening, the periodic update itself, closed its written record on 3 September. But the annual cycle is also how urban growth boundary expansions get picked off one parcel at a time outside any comprehensive capacity analysis, and RCW 36.70A.130(2)(a) genuinely forbids running it against a pending update. The suspension is legally required. The ten-month overrun is not.

Most lines rise 6%. The big jumps are not padding: the two hearings examiners' rate went from $200 to $275 an hour on 1 May 2026. And cost recovery is the defensible default — the alternative is the General Fund subsidising review of private development applications.

Two places it strains. The subdivision fee is flat up to 16 lots: $1,153 per lot at 16, but $6,151 per lot at three — a fixed cost falling five times harder on small infill than on a large plat. A per-lot band is the fix and this is the hearing to ask for it. And a 2.5% technology fee on top of “100% of current costs of service” is, by construction, 102.5%; the staff report's own worked example is a $687 cost charged at $704.

Buried in the same ordinance, unexplained anywhere in the staff report: the list of building components eligible for deferred submittal is cut hard — fire sprinkler, fire alarm, exit illumination, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, auxiliary power, and floor and roof trusses all come off. Deferred submittal is what lets a builder pull a permit and start while those designs are still being engineered. For multifamily and commercial work that changes the construction timeline.

Both directions, honestly. Urban Forestry Commission — emerald ash borer is here

7a
Watch — nothing needs a vote, one thing is news

“EAB identified in Vancouver area August 2026”

Marine Park Engineering, 4500 Columbia Way; hybrid. The staff report is blunt: emerald ash borer is “fatal to ash trees within approximately five to ten years after detection”, “there has never been a successful effort to eradicate EAB once it becomes established”, and the population will “increase significantly over the next five to ten years”. Vancouver has had a management plan since 2022, so this is a managed problem rather than a surprise.

The distributional edge, which the packet does not flag. The city is about to send “postcards to homeowners with ash street trees explaining management and treatment options” and do “neighborhood outreach regarding EAB and privately owned ash trees.” Treatment is recurring; removal is expensive. A dying ash in the right of way is the city’s cost; on private land it is the owner’s. Where that line falls, and whether there is assistance for owners who cannot pay, is not addressed. Staff are separately chasing a Washington DNR grant for ash trees on school sites — the right instinct, applied to one category of owner.

Also on the agenda, without attachments: the 2027-28 work plan and budget preparation — the shaping stage for the next biennium, two days after the Council adopts the Financial Policies that frame it. And the comprehensive-plan tree code took effect at the end of July: tree preservation, minimum canopy coverage, street trees and parking lot landscaping, all of which interact with infill feasibility on small lots.
