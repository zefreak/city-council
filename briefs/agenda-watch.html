Two things the county's own website will tell you wrong

The comprehensive plan hearing dates have moved and the notice has not caught up. Clark County's published news release still says the Council hearing is 6 October with adoption 13 October, and the Planning Commission's decision table — updated 27 August — still heads its column “Council Decision 10/13/2026.” But the Council itself, at Council Time on 26 August, agreed to start the hearing Thursday 1 October at 4pm, continue Friday 2 October at 10am, and continue again Monday 5 October at 10am if needed. That decision was added to the agenda on the day and exists only in the minutes.

And two meetings on this page are invisible to the watcher. The 3 September Planning Commission hearing and the 14 September manufactured home park session are run by Community Planning, not the Council, so they are not on the meetings listing this project scrapes. Both were found by hand. Anyone relying only on the council calendar would miss the single most consequential deadline of the month.

Bottom line

The written record on Clark County's entire 20-year comprehensive plan closes at the end of Thursday night's hearing. After 3 September the Planning Commission deliberates and votes its recommendation on all 101 decision items — every urban growth area expansion, every code amendment, the manufactured home park overlay protecting 2,033 households — and the next opening is the Council hearing in October. Written comments must be received by the end of the hearing; testimony is by signing up on arrival.

Support it — with two fixes

The manufactured home park overlay: 34 parks, 2,033 homes, and one escape hatch

Clark County has 34 manufactured home parks holding 2,033 homes. Twenty-five parks — 81% of the homes — sit inside urban growth areas, mostly the Vancouver UGA. Fewer than half are in zones that currently permit them. Average density is 6.8 homes per gross acre against a proposed R-zone maximum of 24–36, which is exactly why the land is worth more empty. Residents own the home and rent the ground, and the homes are difficult or impossible to move. A moratorium on redevelopment has held since August 2025 and runs out in February 2027 — this zoning is what has to replace it.

Proposed CCC 40.250.120 permits only parks, plus transitional housing, permanent supportive housing, family day care and adult family homes. An owner wanting to redevelop must win a use exception. Tumwater and Kenmore case law establishes the tool as lawful. This is good policy and worth saying so.

Two things to put on the record. First, subsection C.2 permits base-zone residential “if developed as affordable housing consistent with subsection E” — but subsection E is the use exception and contains no affordability standard at all: no AMI level, no term, no definition. Vancouver's equivalent zone, which this is modelled on, specifies permanently affordable at 60% AMI or below. Ask which it is meant to be, and ask for the number to be written in. Second, the use exception's test — “not economically viable due to the property's location, site conditions, or surrounding development” — is the argument every owner of a 6.8‑unit‑per‑acre park on land zoned for 36 will make. Its relocation clause reads “relocation assistance, right to return policies… or other assistance”, with no floor. Bellingham's comparable overlay carries local provisions for notice, opportunity to purchase and relocation assistance. Clark County's does not.

Rural parks get nothing, deliberately. The county's consultant memo says rural parks “face less redevelopment pressure as nonconforming uses” and should keep their existing zoning. But the same memo notes nonconforming status means no expansion and no modification beyond maintenance and repair — which it says “can limit MHP flexibility and future viability.” The nine rural parks are protected from redevelopment by being frozen. That is not the same as being preserved.

Title 20 update: affordability requirements for HB 1491 compliance

Correction to an earlier version of this page: the packet was described here as unpublished. It has since posted — 62 pages — and has been read. What is still unpublished is the staff report for this item, the one carrying the actual proposed code language. Staff's own memo promised it “10 days in advance of the public hearing” — that was 29 August. The Commission is scheduled to vote a recommendation on code text the public has not seen.

What the law requires. Within a quarter-mile walkshed of Vancouver's 51 Vine BRT stations: no off-street parking requirements, a 50% impact fee cut for projects using the 20-year MFTE, and 50-year affordability — 10% of units at 60% AMI, or 20% at 80% AMI, or 10% at 80% AMI with a tenth of units at two bedrooms or more. It must be met in each individual building; a master planned community cannot concentrate the affordability in one block.

What staff have done with the exemptions. The statute lets cities exempt parcels already zoned at the FAR threshold on 1 January 2025, plus a discretionary 25% of the remaining parcels. Staff exempted the whole City Center Vision subarea and CX Plan District, and then — in their own words — “identified parcels making up to 25% of otherwise non-exempt station area that are anticipated as most likely to redevelop and added them to the exemption list.”

That sentence is the finding. The 25% could have gone anywhere. It was applied to the parcels most likely to actually be built on — so the mandate falls heaviest where development is least likely and lightest where it is most likely. Staff's stated reason is that HB 1491 is “effectively a partially funded inclusionary zoning requirement” — 50 years of affordability against at most 20 years of MFTE — that pushes development away from transit. That argument deserves engaging, but note what it is: an argument about volume, aimed at a statute about depth and tenure. Trading one for the other is a choice, not an arithmetic result.

And the choice lives in a map that may not be law. The memo says staff “does not envision codifying this map, but will post it to the City's website.” The presentation says the mapping is “to be referenced and linked in code.” Those are different things: a map in code changes by ordinance, a map on a website changes by staff decision. Ask which.

Vancouver is non-compliant right now, and the state's rules apply in the gap. The city's deadline was 31 December 2025; it adopted 1 June 2026. Commerce has finalised a model code that preempts local zoning for non-compliant cities, and staff say it “applies to impacted, non-exempt parcels until the City adds the affordability components to its code.” Vancouver is the only city currently subject to HB 1491. So the question is not whether there is an affordability requirement near BRT — there is one today — but whether Vancouver's replaces it with something narrower.

Act — public hearing

Evergreen Public Schools: the school impact fee goes to zero

The agenda title says “capital facilities plan.” The substance is a fee schedule. Table 36 of the Comprehensive Plan is amended so Evergreen's school impact fee drops from $6,432 to $0 on a single-family home and $3,753 to $0 on a multi-family unit, plus $938.25 to $0 on an ADU, for the six years to 31 December 2032.

This is legally compelled, not a giveaway. Impact fees may fund only capital needed for new growth; Evergreen's board found adequate capacity at every grade level and declining enrollment, and its counsel wrote to the City that the district “will not be entitled to collect school impact fees.” Council already imposed a moratorium on collection in June and affirmed it in August.

The question worth asking anyway. The same packet notes the district's upcoming process for consolidating existing facilities, while the district's own letter says the City is planning for “at least 38,000 additional housing units (about 44% more than the City has now)” and enrollment “is likely to gradually increase.” So capacity is surplus now because the district is shrinking and closing buildings, and the growth that would refill them lands inside the 20-year plan but outside the six-year fee window. Whatever capacity is then needed gets paid for by levy or bond — by existing residents — rather than by the development that caused it. The district can amend its plan annually. On what trigger, and will the City ask?

Proportional impact fees — smaller homes pay less, bigger homes pay more

State law now requires residential impact fees to be proportional, so small units pay less. Vancouver's current fees, from a memo that is not in the packet PDF and had to be fetched separately:

FeeSingle familyMulti-family
Park impact fee, three districts$6,130$4,480
School impact fee, Vancouver SD$2,786$2,486
Traffic impact fee, three districts$247–$439 per vehicle trip

Staff want square-footage brackets — illustrated as 0–1,500 / 1,500–2,500 / 2,500+ sq ft — rather than a per-square-foot rate, working with ECOnorthwest. ADUs are statutorily capped at 50% of the main unit's fee. The design constraint to watch is that staff intend it to be revenue neutral: every dollar taken off a small unit is added to a large one. That is a genuinely progressive redistribution and worth supporting — but revenue neutrality is a choice, not a requirement, and it is being settled now, in a workshop, with no vote. Follow-up workshop 13 October; hearings “likely at the end of the year.”

Two City documents in one packet disagree by $90. The Vancouver school impact fee is $2,786 in this memo and $2,876 in the Evergreen staff report. One is a transposition. Not resolved here. Also worth knowing: HB 1491's mandated offsets include a 50% impact fee cut, so a proportional redesign changes what that 50% is calculated against. Items 5 and 9 are not independent.

“Parking Scofflaw Program” — and no document says what it is

The agenda has been read; the packet is not published, so there is no attachment for this item and no way to know what is proposed. Presented by the Parking Project Coordinator and the Lead Parking Officer. Scofflaw programmes elsewhere typically mean escalating enforcement against vehicles with accumulated unpaid citations: boot, tow, impound.

If that is what this is, the reason to care is specific rather than general. Towing turns an unpaid fine into loss of the vehicle and then into storage fees larger than the original debt — falling on people who could not pay the first ticket, and intersecting directly with vehicle residency. Three questions with yes/no answers: is there an ability-to-pay determination, is there a payment plan that halts escalation, and is there a protocol for vehicles someone is living in? Asking before a programme is designed is worth more than objecting after.

Public comment here is open on any issue — the agenda says “the public is invited to speak regarding any issue” — three minutes, but you must pre-register by 5pm Tuesday 8 September: email callie.taylor@cityvancouver.us or phone 360-487-8650. Note the domain has no “of” in it; that is what the agenda prints, three times.

Item 4 is a Parking Code Review & Update, also with no published presentation. It runs the day after the Planning Commission's HB 1491 hearing — a law whose mandated offsets include eliminating off-street parking requirements near all 51 BRT stations. Fire impact fees are new — every one of these districts is at $0 today. Fire District 3 proposes $992 single family / $505 per multi-family unit; District 6, $791 / $563; Clark-Cowlitz Fire Rescue, $654 / $308. Fire District 10 proposes $3,975–$4,714 for a single-family home, five times its neighbours, and its staff report presents two methods rather than one recommendation. That gap is the first question to ask. The Planning Commission recommended all four 5–2.

Both directions, honestly. A $13,665 per-unit multi-family fee in Woodland being higher than the single-family fee is backwards from any density-supporting position, and Washington's new proportional-fee law exists precisely because flat per-unit fees fall hardest on small units — three commissioners voted to deny. But the alternative funding source is a levy or bond, which falls on existing residents including renters, and which rural districts often fail to pass. A fee legally restricted to growth-caused capacity is not the regressive option here.

No fire impact fee may be collected until the district signs an interlocal agreement with the county (CCC 40.630.090), so adoption is not the last step. Whether a regular daytime Council meeting also sits that day — which would be the broad-scope public comment slot — is not established.
