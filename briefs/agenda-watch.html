City Council meets at a school on Fourth Plain, and the whole meeting is a community forum

2pm
Act — written comment by noon Monday; RSVP to speak

Council Community Forum, Rooted School Vancouver, 10401 NE Fourth Plain Blvd

2:00–3:30pm, doors 1:30pm. The published agenda has two items, roll call and the forum. There is no consent agenda and no vote. The event page says it is held earlier than usual so that high school students can come, and that discussion “will focus on issues affecting young people in our community”. The agenda says people are “welcome to talk about other issues” too. Three minutes per speaker, up to 90 minutes in all. C-TRAN serves the site.

How to take part. In writing: email council@cityofvancouver.us by 12:00pm Monday 28 September, and it goes into the record. In person: RSVP on the City's event page. The agenda says in-person participation is for “those that are registered”.

RSVP, but know the law. The event page is headed “Registration Required”. This is a meeting of the City Council, and RCW 42.30.040 says a member of the public “shall not be required, as a condition to attendance … to register his or her name and other information”. Organising who speaks at which table is one thing; turning someone away at the door is another. If it happens, cite the statute.

A good event, with two things to hold it to. Tables run by outside facilitators (PointNorth, per the City's 21 September memo), with a “stay on topic” rule, produce themes rather than positions. Ask when the write-up is published and whether the table notes are kept as a record. The stated outcomes are all process: “increase youth engagement”, “build trust”. Worth raising at a table: the City Manager's recommended 2027-28 budget lists a Youth Coordinator as “Not Recommended”. The budget publishes 1 October, and its hearing is 16 November.

Work sessions — a drinking water ordinance, e-bikes, and the counties' lobby

WS
Watch — no comment at a work session. Hearing 20 Oct

Drinking water ordinance: good public health work, with the text not yet published

It codifies county oversight of Group B water systems and individual wells, adds a long-term sanitary survey programme, and adds a technical manual revised every five years. Wells serve 25% of county water users and Group B systems 15% (presentation, slide 3). Those are rural households with nobody else testing their water. This is worth supporting.

Two things to ask for before 20 October. First, the ordinance itself: only the presentation is attached. Second, the existing fee. “No new fees proposed” is qualified on the same slide by “Existing fee initiated in 2025 … BOH oversight of fee schedule in future”. The amount is not given. The listed reviewers are the Development Engineering Advisory Board, the Planning Commission, an “informal industry advisory review” and neighbourhood associations. No well-user or tenant group is named.

The Council's own minutes had routed this to a Board of Health meeting, which takes public comment; it has landed on a work session, which does not. The solid waste code hearing is tentatively the same evening, 6pm Tuesday 20 October.

Watch — nothing proposed yet

E-bikes: the Sheriff says the problem is a different vehicle

The Sheriff's written answers to the Council say the serious collisions involving pedestrians come mostly from juveniles on e-motorcycles. Those are machines with no pedals, over 750 W or above 28 mph, which are already motorcycles under state law. Enforcement “lean[s] heavily toward education”. There is no draft ordinance and no options list. If one appears — trail speed limits, age limits, a helmet mandate, impound powers — it will land on people who use e-bikes to get to work. The Sheriff's own account of the problem is the answer to it.

02
Watch — the county's letter to the state is the best evidence against its own plan

What Clark County has already conceded to the Department of Commerce

From the Community Planning Director's letter of 17 September 2026: “we acknowledge further de designations of agricultural lands based solely or primarily on proximity to urban growth that otherwise meet the definition of agricultural lands historically has not been found to be defensible by the Growth Management Hearings Board.”

The same letter says the county is “conducting targeted audits of the draft plans and documentation supporting expansion” — the evidentiary record for the expansions was not complete when the Planning Commission voted on them — and that it will “explicitly evaluate the cumulative loss of agricultural and forest economies … rather than evaluating each parcel in isolation.” That evaluation is not in the hearing record.

Five of the nine map items de-designate agricultural resource land, and every one passed on a narrow Planning Commission vote:

03
Watch — a capacity number that is not an affordability number

The 0–80% AMI “surplus” of 49 units is made of zoning, not rent

Exhibit 4, Table 5, is the plan's demonstration that the unincorporated Vancouver UGA has capacity at every income band: 19,419 units of capacity in the 0–80% AMI band against a need of 19,370. Look at what generates it. The row is the sum of the zoning districts R-24, R-36, OR-24, OR-36, R-50, OR-50, mixed use, the Hwy 99 overlay areas, the commercial districts, and an assumed 11% of the R1-5/R1-6/R1-7.5 capacity. Nothing in any of those zones requires a rent. The model treats “multifamily zoning” as a proxy for “affordable below 80% AMI”, and the plan contains no mechanism that makes it one.

The countywide allocation asks for 12,852 non-PSH units plus 7,491 permanent supportive housing units below 30% AMI — 20,343 units, about a fifth of the 103,695 total, plus 3,986 emergency beds. Table 5 does not break that band out at all; 0–30% is folded into the single 0–80% bucket. A zoned-capacity model cannot tell a unit affordable at 25% AMI from one affordable at 75%, and the deepest band is exactly the one zoning cannot deliver — it needs subsidy. There is no funding plan in the record for the 20,343.

Note the direction of that argument. It is a reason to doubt the affordability claim, not a reason to oppose the zoning. The zoning is necessary and insufficient.

04
Watch — capacity managed down by changing assumptions, not code

The Vancouver UGA lost about 24,500 units of planned capacity, and where the cut landed was a choice

Three amendments brought the unincorporated Vancouver UGA back inside its control total: reverse the 179th St/Fairgrounds upzoning to current zoning (~30% of the reduction); cut the assumed Hwy 99 sub-area density from 75 to 40 dwellings/acre (~8%); and cut the assumed density in the R1-5, R1-6 and R1-7.5 zones from a flat 14 to a tiered 12/10/8 (~62%). Those zones currently allow 21.8 dwellings/acre.

Only the first changes the zoning map. The other two change modelling assumptions in the Vacant Buildable Lands Model — the code is untouched and the number on paper moves. The plan's housing capacity is being managed downward by changing what staff assume will get built, not what may be built.

05
Good — and almost all of it is state law

The residential code changes

Off-street parking minimums exempted for passive-house, modular and mass timber residential (RCW 36.70A.817); tandem parking allowed; gravel parking allowed for up to six spaces. Passive-house retrofits may exceed height limits and project into setbacks by eight inches for insulation. Façade-modulation and Highway 99 upper-storey stepback exceptions for affordable housing, passive house, conversions, modular and mass timber. The consultant's memo marks nearly every line “State compliance” — the county has, in her words, “little to no discretion to modify locally.”

One is a local choice and a good one. Park trailers (“park model RVs”) and tagged tiny houses on wheels become permitted within manufactured home parks — recorded as “Implement Planning Commission direction”. It legalises a housing type already there. The manufactured home park overlay passed the Planning Commission 7–0.

TDR
Watch — discussed 23 Sep with no paperwork; nothing recorded yet

The farmland offset: what happened to the enforcement

The Planning Commission rejected, 2–5, a Transfer of Development Rights policy that would have held every expansion on de-designated farmland under an urban holding designation until an acre was conserved for each acre converted. On 17 September the county told Commerce it would pursue a TDR policy “separate from urban growth expansion efforts”. That is the same policy with its enforcement removed. The Council held a “TDR Discussion” at Council Time on 23 September with no staff report of any kind, and no minutes are out yet.

Worth putting in a written comment: if the expansions pass, what replaces the holding designation, and what is the date and the deliverable?

Vancouver Tue 6 Oct · 4:30pm

Transportation and Mobility Commission — three corridor redesigns, and the comment window shuts first

WS
Act — phase 2 engagement “wraps up in early October”

NE 72nd Ave, NE 86th/87th Ave, 97th/98th Ave — at 30% design

Comment to vancouvermoves@cityofvancouver.us before early October. The staff memo says the phase 2 engagement summary will be compiled then and shared with the Commission “prior to the public hearing on these projects”, and that hearing is in November (date not published). Comments arriving after the summary is written land outside the record it is built from.

These are good projects and they are the kind that get killed in November by people who turn up about parking. Two 10-foot drive lanes on NE 72nd with speed cushions and a 25 mph posted limit, past Walnut Grove Elementary; sidewalks on both sides of 97th/98th; lighting in three segments; a funded Safe Routes to School project. The unusual and correct part is that turn lanes, not just kerb space, are being repurposed — it treats vehicle throughput as a thing to trade.

The near-term work rides on an already-scheduled pavement preservation project, so the marginal cost of doing it now is low and the cost of deferring is a repaved road you cannot restripe for another cycle.

Three honest caveats. All on-street parking goes on NE 72nd and on 97th/98th between Burton and Mill Plain, and the memo never says how many spaces or who depends on them — that count is the number to ask for. Segments 1, 6 and 7 of 97th/98th are outside the pavement project entirely, so the corridor improves in the middle and is left as-is at both ends. And most crossings, lighting, sidewalk infill and full roadway sections sit in the long-term column; what the paving money buys is largely paint, tubular markers, signs and speed cushions.

4,800+ mailers went to property owners and occupants — the right call, and not always done. What is absent from the engagement list is any Spanish-language channel named as such, on corridors that include Fourth Plain. A Community Forum sits on this agenda at the 6:00pm break, despite the API reporting public speaker signup as false.

Ahead Oct 2026 – Fall 2027

Dated, published, and worth putting in a diary now

MPD
Watch — a permanent countywide parks tax is being lined up

The Greater Clark Parks District is being prepared for expansion across the county

From the packet for Vancouver's Parks & Recreation Advisory Commission retreat on 23 September. Its slide reads “Focus is on expansion of existing MPD. Public opinion survey after election with results in early '27”. The May 2026 regional task team report behind it ends with “Possible ballot measure in Fall 2027.” At about 13¢ per $1,000 countywide it would raise ~$15M a year.

The need is real, and the numbers are the county's own. County Parks has 0.69 staff per 10,000 residents against a national median of 3.6 for its size. A third of daily maintenance tasks go undone. Regional parks have run on reserves for seven years, and the report warns of closures. Vancouver's parks department has lost 37.5% of its staff since 2007 while the city grew by 47,000.

But look at which vehicle was picked. The report compared two options. A six-year levy needs a 60% vote and comes back to voters for renewal. A Metropolitan Park District needs a simple majority and is permanent, and its rate is set each year by the governing body — for the existing district, the County Council. The report counts the levy's renewal campaign as a cost. That renewal vote is also the only moment voters get to judge how the money was spent. It is a property tax, and renters pay it through rent.

What to ask for before the petition drive: an independent board, a published rate ceiling, and revenue-sharing terms with the cities written down first. Hold them to the “accountability benchmarking” the report itself lists. Vancouver's parks plan update will also add park impact fees on non-residential development. That is a good idea: part of the cost of growth moves off new housing.
