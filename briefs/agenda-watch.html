02a
Watch — the state's final word, 22 September

Commerce: the boundaries are already big enough, and the farmland case is not made

The Department of Commerce's final comment letter, added to the record on 24 September, says:

  • “it appears the current urban growth area boundaries are sufficient for the projected population increases”;
  • it found no analysis showing other zoning strategies were tried before expansion;
  • it found no discussion showing the farmland “no longer meet[s] the designation criteria.”

It recommends removing the de-designated land from the expansions, or justifying it. It asks the county to break housing capacity out by income, “at a minimum … everything above 50% AMI”. That is the same flaw as the single 0–80% bucket in card 03. It also asks for documentation of local funding gaps. Commerce is advisory. A hearings board appeal would start from this letter.

The best case for the expansions is the cities' reply, and it deserves a hearing. Ridgefield says its middle-housing incentives since 2021 produced 6 duplex units, one qualifying ADU and no cottages, while its mixed-use zones produced 495 units. Camas says the Nevin site is already cut into five-acre lots, so the real choice is ranchettes or 1,200+ apartments. Two answers: the “affordable” capacity the cities claim is zoning, not rent; and “it's already parcelised” makes sprawl the justification for more sprawl.

03
Watch — a capacity number that is not an affordability number

The 0–80% AMI “surplus” of 49 units is made of zoning, not rent

Exhibit 4, Table 5, is the plan's demonstration that the unincorporated Vancouver UGA has capacity at every income band: 19,419 units of capacity in the 0–80% AMI band against a need of 19,370. Look at what generates it. The row is the sum of the zoning districts R-24, R-36, OR-24, OR-36, R-50, OR-50, mixed use, the Hwy 99 overlay areas, the commercial districts, and an assumed 11% of the R1-5/R1-6/R1-7.5 capacity. Nothing in any of those zones requires a rent. The model treats “multifamily zoning” as a proxy for “affordable below 80% AMI”, and the plan contains no mechanism that makes it one.

The countywide allocation asks for 12,852 non-PSH units plus 7,491 permanent supportive housing units below 30% AMI — 20,343 units, about a fifth of the 103,695 total, plus 3,986 emergency beds. Table 5 does not break that band out at all; 0–30% is folded into the single 0–80% bucket. A zoned-capacity model cannot tell a unit affordable at 25% AMI from one affordable at 75%, and the deepest band is exactly the one zoning cannot deliver — it needs subsidy. There is no funding plan in the record for the 20,343.

Note the direction of that argument. It is a reason to doubt the affordability claim, not a reason to oppose the zoning. The zoning is necessary and insufficient.

04
Watch — capacity managed down by changing assumptions, not code

The Vancouver UGA lost about 24,500 units of planned capacity, and where the cut landed was a choice

Three amendments brought the unincorporated Vancouver UGA back inside its control total: reverse the 179th St/Fairgrounds upzoning to current zoning (~30% of the reduction); cut the assumed Hwy 99 sub-area density from 75 to 40 dwellings/acre (~8%); and cut the assumed density in the R1-5, R1-6 and R1-7.5 zones from a flat 14 to a tiered 12/10/8 (~62%). Those zones currently allow 21.8 dwellings/acre.

Only the first changes the zoning map. The other two change modelling assumptions in the Vacant Buildable Lands Model — the code is untouched and the number on paper moves. The plan's housing capacity is being managed downward by changing what staff assume will get built, not what may be built.

05
Good — and almost all of it is state law

The residential code changes

Off-street parking minimums exempted for passive-house, modular and mass timber residential (RCW 36.70A.817); tandem parking allowed; gravel parking allowed for up to six spaces. Passive-house retrofits may exceed height limits and project into setbacks by eight inches for insulation. Façade-modulation and Highway 99 upper-storey stepback exceptions for affordable housing, passive house, conversions, modular and mass timber. The consultant's memo marks nearly every line “State compliance” — the county has, in her words, “little to no discretion to modify locally.”

One is a local choice and a good one. Park trailers (“park model RVs”) and tagged tiny houses on wheels become permitted within manufactured home parks — recorded as “Implement Planning Commission direction”. It legalises a housing type already there. The manufactured home park overlay passed the Planning Commission 7–0.

TDR
Watch — a new TDR policy, posted 29 September

Councilor Little's TDR amendment: worth backing, and it is not an offset

It would replace policy 1.1.24 (doc 237651): “Clark County and participating jurisdictions shall work cooperatively to implement a countywide transfer of development rights (TDR) program starting in 2027”. The County would act as the TDR bank, at a “pre-determined price per credit (e.g. $25,000)”, and “payments into the TDR bank may be allowed outside of receiving areas as a mitigation tool.”

What it fixes. The version still printed in Exhibit 2 makes any boundary expansion contingent on TDR. Commerce called that “potentially inconsistent with state statute”. Little's text drops the link and commits to implementing a programme in 2027. On 23 September the only commitment was a round table.

What it does not do. It sets no acre-for-acre ratio, no no-net-loss rule, no date for the programme to be running, and no price floor. The “mitigation” payment is a fee in lieu, so it is only as good as the price and the easements the bank actually buys. It cannot offset the five de-designations voted on the same day, because it is no longer tied to them.

So: support it as a separate good, ask for a credit price floor and a no-net-loss goal to be written in, and keep the main ask as it was. That ask is the state's own remedy: take the de-designated farmland out of the expansions.

Act — public hearing; register by noon online, 6:30pm in person

Resolution opposing IL26-001 and IL26-638

One operative line: the Council “hereby expresses its opposition” to both November initiatives. It is a hearing because RCW 42.17A.555 lets an elected body take a position on a ballot measure only if the public gets “an approximately equal opportunity for the expression of an opposing view.” The initiatives' supporters are guaranteed a slot. If the resolution's supporters stay home, the record will show only the other side.

Worth supporting, so cite the text, not the recitals. IL26-001 strikes the current statute's protected-class anti-discrimination right, the bullying-complaint right, the language-access notice, and the records shield for a child whose parent is a criminal defendant in a case where the child is the victim. The resolution's recitals on abuse victims' records match the text. Two others go further than the text does. “Require schools to ‘out’ LGBTQ+ kids” is an inference from broad records access; no clause says it. “Genital exams” is stronger than IL26-638, which lets a provider verify sex by anatomy, genetics or testosterone levels. The point that cannot be rebutted is that every student entering female-only athletics needs the medical certification.

Written comment to council@cityofvancouver.us by noon Monday. Remote testimony needs online registration by noon. In-person registration is at City Hall until 6:30pm. Three minutes. The API's “no signup” flags are wrong; the agenda's first page sets out these routes.

05
Consent — passes without debate unless pulled. Hearing Mon 12 Oct

HB 1491: 50-year affordability near BRT, with the exemption map left off the ordinance

On “impacted” parcels within a quarter-mile walkshed of the 51 BRT stations, new housing must carry a 50-year covenant: 10% of units at 60% AMI, or 20% at 80%, or 10% at 80% with a tenth of units two-bedroom or larger. MFTE projects that meet it get a 50% traffic impact fee cut. It takes effect on 11 November.

The map decides which parcels are covered, and it is not in the ordinance. The text points to a map “available at the … Permit Center, and the Department website.” Staff put the statutory 25% exemption on the parcels “most likely to redevelop” (14 August memo). As drafted, staff can change that choice without a vote. The ask for 12 October is to adopt the parcel list as an exhibit.

Staff's objection deserves a fair hearing. They call it a partially funded inclusionary mandate that “will likely result in less housing production”. In a market where the City completed under half its 2025 target, that can be true at the margin. But the law is untested here: since June there have been “no local development proposals or inquiries” on the affected parcels. Nobody has testified on it at any stage; the Planning Commission vote was 4–0 “with no comments received”.

Drafting errors to point out before second reading: the hearing is recited as “October 12, 2025”; the RCW citation is blank; the title promises severability, but there is no severability clause and no Section 3; and the compliance deadline is given as 30 June 2026 here but as 31 December 2025 in the August memo.

WS
Watch — on consent 9 Nov, hearing 16 Nov

Scofflaw programme: four $25 tickets become $643.52 and a towed car

Four or more parking tickets unpaid after 45 days puts a vehicle on a list. It can then be booted, and impounded after two business days. Staff's own example: $100 of fines becomes $643.52. The “17.5%” collections fee is charged on a total that includes itself, which makes it 21.2% of the debt.

Nothing in the draft deals with ability to pay or with people living in their vehicles. Payment plans exist before listing, which is a real safeguard. After a boot, release requires payment in full. Staff's case: only 52% of citations are paid within 45 days. Ask for an ability-to-pay reduction, payment plans that survive the boot, and a diversion route for vehicles used as homes.

Council — a five-year rewrite of the county's housing surcharge money, with no dollar figure

SB1
Act — comment on any county business, 3 minutes, early in the meeting

HB 2060 interlocal: from bonds and capital to shelter operations and housing

The agreement with all eight cities and towns governs the document-recording surcharge for households at or below 50% AMI (RCW 36.22.178). Per the staff report, it “supersedes earlier agreements … by removing outdated capital and bond-related allocations and prioritizing funds for shelter operations, transitional housing, and permanent housing”, with the Community Action Advisory Board recommending awards each year. It runs 1 January 2026 to 31 January 2031, nine months retroactive.

Probably good. Operating money for shelters is the hardest money in the system to find, and the 50% AMI floor stays. But the agreement is not attached, and the dollar amount on the staff report is blank. “Removing bond-related allocations” could mean debt that now needs another payer, or construction money that is gone. The comp plan already has no funding plan for its 7,491 permanent supportive housing units.

Ask: how much the surcharge raises each year; what the removed allocations were; publish the agreement; and set a floor for permanent housing, so shelter operating costs cannot absorb the whole stream.

Written comment at clark.wa.gov/councilors/public-comment. WebEx 2498 195 2199, password BOCC. This is the county's broad-scope comment slot: any county business, not just the agenda.

Clark County Wed 7 Oct · 9:00am work session · 1:00pm Council Time

A mobile home rent loophole, and a budget built around two November levies

8.1
Act — Council Time comment, agenda items only: say “item 8.1”

Mobile home parks: the uncapped rent reset on sale

State law caps manufactured home lot rent increases at 5% a year (RCW 59.20.370). But when a home is sold and the lease passes to the buyer, the landlord may take one increase “not limited by” the cap at the first renewal (RCW 59.20.380(3)). Buyers price that in, so every sale knocks value off the seller's home. Their equity goes to the park owner.

Councilor Yung raised it on 30 September as a 2027 legislative ask. The minutes record his concern that the reset “causes a decrease in the mobile homes value”. That is right, and closing it is good for park residents. There is no paper attached, though, and the title says “Rent Cap Laws”. Ask that the request be written as removing the one-time increase on sale, not as a general review of the cap. A park resident who has sold, or tried to, under this rule is the best possible witness.

Ahead Oct 2026 – Fall 2027

Dated, published, and worth putting in a diary now

MPD
Watch — a permanent countywide parks tax is being lined up

The Greater Clark Parks District is being prepared for expansion across the county

From the packet for Vancouver's Parks & Recreation Advisory Commission retreat on 23 September. Its slide reads “Focus is on expansion of existing MPD. Public opinion survey after election with results in early '27”. The May 2026 regional task team report behind it ends with “Possible ballot measure in Fall 2027.” At about 13¢ per $1,000 countywide it would raise ~$15M a year.

The need is real, and the numbers are the county's own. County Parks has 0.69 staff per 10,000 residents against a national median of 3.6 for its size. A third of daily maintenance tasks go undone. Regional parks have run on reserves for seven years, and the report warns of closures. Vancouver's parks department has lost 37.5% of its staff since 2007 while the city grew by 47,000.

But look at which vehicle was picked. The report compared two options. A six-year levy needs a 60% vote and comes back to voters for renewal. A Metropolitan Park District needs a simple majority and is permanent, and its rate is set each year by the governing body — for the existing district, the County Council. The report counts the levy's renewal campaign as a cost. That renewal vote is also the only moment voters get to judge how the money was spent. It is a property tax, and renters pay it through rent.

What to ask for before the petition drive: an independent board, a published rate ceiling, and revenue-sharing terms with the cities written down first. Hold them to the “accountability benchmarking” the report itself lists. Vancouver's parks plan update will also add park impact fees on non-residential development. That is a good idea: part of the cost of growth moves off new housing.
