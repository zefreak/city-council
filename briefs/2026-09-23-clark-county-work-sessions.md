## Work session 1 — Solid Waste Code Updates

Presented by the Solid Waste and Recycling Division (moved from Public Health to Public Works in
March 2025). Draft ordinance amends CCC 2.37.030–2.37.040 and CCC 24.12.130, and rescinds CCC
24.12.370 and 24.12.380 in their entirety. Effective 1 January 2027.

### What it actually does

**1. Rescinds the $1.43/ton tipping fee** (CCC 24.12.130). The ordinance recital: Ordinance
2017-09-10 "established a temporary $1.43 per-ton solid waste tipping fee to support essential solid
and hazardous waste **assessment, response, and mitigation** services, and this fee is no longer
necessary under the revised administrative structure mutually established by Public Health and Public
Works following the 2025 transfer of solid waste functions." The deck adds that the 2017 fee covered
"a pause in state funding" and that "state funding has been restored."

The effect: disposal costs fall very slightly, and a dedicated, ring-fenced revenue stream for
hazardous-waste response disappears into general appropriation. **$1.43/ton is a small per-household
sum and a real annual total** — and the total is nowhere in the packet. A fee whose removal is
justified by "state funding has been restored" is only as safe as the state budget; the fee was
created in 2017 precisely because state funding paused once before.

To be fair to the proposal: the underlying permit-fee authority in CCC 24.12.130 survives untouched —
the jurisdictional board of health may still set application and operations fees "sufficient to cover
the estimated costs" of monitoring compliance. What is deleted is the 2017 *addition* of the flat
tipping fee, not the board's fee-setting power.

**2. Rescinds the compost education and outreach duty** (CCC 24.12.370), which currently requires
Public Health's solid waste programme to provide technical assistance on compost to county
departments, conduct "educational outreach to inform residents about the value of compost", and
report tons of organic material diverted. **3. Rescinds the compost reporting section**
(CCC 24.12.380), which requires a biennial report to Ecology by 31 December of even-numbered years.

The stated basis: "Ecology has confirmed that our Comprehensive Solid Waste Management Plan already
meets the intent of the RCW requirements." That may well be right. The practical difference is that
a **code obligation** becomes a **plan commitment** — a plan the Council can amend without an
ordinance, and which no member of the public can enforce the way they can enforce code. Ask whether
Ecology's confirmation is in writing and whether it can be published.

**4. Updates the compost procurement policy** (CCC 2.37.030–.040) to shift reporting from Public
Health to Solid Waste and Recycling, move the annual reporting date from 15 December to
**1 March**, and rewrite the exemption for delay. The old text let a department skip compost if
"products are not available within a reasonable time or distance from the project." The new text:
if "availability or delivery timelines would unreasonably delay the project schedule, **including
delays affecting critical path work**." That is a materially wider escape hatch — "critical path"
is a term of art in construction scheduling that almost any contractor can invoke. The other
exemptions (purchasing standards, health and safety standards, price not "reasonable or
competitive", conflict with state guidance) are unchanged.

Compost procurement is a climate measure: it is how counties create demand for diverted organics
under ESSHB 1799 (2022), ch. 43.19A RCW. **Widening the delay exemption weakens it.** That is a
one-line change in an ordinance presented as housekeeping.

### Verdict

The organisational parts are genuinely housekeeping — the programme moved departments in March 2025
and the code should say so. Three substantive things ride along with it: a dedicated hazardous-waste
revenue stream is removed without a number, a public education mandate is deleted, and a compost
procurement exemption is widened. None of the three is discussed as a policy choice in the deck.

## Work session 2 — Fireworks, annual update

Presented by Community Development, with handouts from Clark County Fire District 3, Camas-Washougal
Fire Department and Vancouver Fire Department.

**The legal constraint is the whole story, and it is on slide 3.** RCW 70.77.250(4): *"Any ordinances
adopted by a county or city that are more restrictive than state law shall have an effective date no
sooner than one year after their adoption."* Clark County cannot restrict fireworks for the 2027
season; anything adopted now bites in **July 2028** at the earliest. If the Council wants a ban or a
tightening, the decision is effectively already a year late for 2027, and it needs to be made this
year for 2028.

**The data, from the Fireworks Call Center, 3–5 July 2026:**

| | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fireworks Call Center calls | 1,013 | 1,079 | **1,309** |
| 9-1-1 calls for the day | 848 | 2,272 | **2,269** |
| Police-escalated noise complaints (VPD) | 105 | 29 | 41 |
| Police-escalated noise complaints (CCSO) | 35 | 14 | 4 |

Fire calls in the same window totalled **255** across all agencies, 140 of them Vancouver Fire.
Vancouver Fire's own 28 June – 5 July series: 1,172 calls in 2026 against 1,011 in 2025, 21 fire
calls, **3 injuries** (up from 1), estimated loss $9,000. The Fire Marshal's Office logged 3
fireworks-caused fires in 2026 — "no structure fires — portable toilets and two larger grass fires."

**Read the deck's own caveat before using its numbers.** Under the 255-call fire table it says:
*"It's impossible for us to know how many fires were the direct result of fireworks but it's safe to
assume most of them were."* That is an assumption doing a lot of work — the table's own categories
include "burning complaint" and "unknown/investigation" (84 of the 255), and every outside fire in a
dry first week of July gets counted. The honest version of this slide is 3 confirmed fireworks fires
from the Fire Marshal and 255 fire calls over a holiday weekend, with the causal link unestablished.
A tightening of the ordinance can be argued for on injuries, call volume, wildfire risk and noise
without needing the 255 to be fireworks.

The 9-1-1 series has a break in it too: 848 in 2024 against ~2,270 in both 2025 and 2026. A near
tripling in one year and then flat suggests a change in how calls were counted, not a change in
behaviour. Nothing in the deck explains it.

**Council Options** is a slide title with nothing under it in the text extraction — whatever options
staff will put up, they are not in the published deck. That is the substance of the session and it
is not public in advance.
