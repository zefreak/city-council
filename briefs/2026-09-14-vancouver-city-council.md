### 1. Consent item 11 — camping banned within 1,500 feet of the Bridge Shelter

Ordinance amending **VMC 8.22.030 and 8.22.040(B)**. Staff Report 179-26, packet pp. 509–518.
Staff contact Jamie Spinelli, Homeless Response Manager.

**What it does.** Adds a new first entry to the list of "camping and outside habitation impact
areas": *"Within 1,500 feet of the nearest point of the City of Vancouver Bridge Shelter
Property"* (5313 NE 94th Avenue). Inside an impact area, camping and outside habitation are
unlawful **at all times**. Everywhere else in the city, VMC 8.22.040(C)–(D) bans camping and
vehicle habitation in parks, streets and public lots only between **6:30am and 9:30pm** — so
outside the impact areas, overnight camping remains lawful. This ordinance removes the overnight
window inside the buffer.

**The size.** A 1,500-foot radius is **162 acres, a quarter of a square mile**, and the buffer is
measured from the nearest point of the *parcel*, not from a point — so the real footprint is that
plus the parcel. Staff considered 1,000, 1,500 and 2,000 feet and chose 1,500 because 1,000 *"would
not encompass several nearby locations where significant camping and outside habitation currently
occurs"* (p. 510). That sentence is the honest one: the buffer is drawn to reach existing camps,
not merely to protect the shelter's front door.

**What the staff report concedes, in its own words** (p. 511, "Challenge(s)"):

> "The amendment would reduce the public area available for lawful nighttime camping and will
> cause people currently camping within the proposed buffer who decline shelter placement to
> relocate to other locations."

**The arithmetic that matters.** The shelter adds **120 beds**. Another roughly **70-bed shelter
already operates within 1,000 feet** of the site (p. 510) — so that shelter's own surroundings fall
inside the new ban too. Staff state plainly that the Bridge Shelter *"will not provide sufficient
capacity to shelter every person experiencing unsheltered homelessness in Vancouver."* The
report does not say how many people currently camp inside the 1,500-foot line, so the question
"are there enough beds for the people this displaces" is not answered anywhere in the packet.
**That is the question to put on the record.**

**The strongest version of the city's case**, stated fairly: this is not a bare sweep. The
restriction takes effect as 120 beds open, HART and partner outreach continue before and after,
and the ordinance is site-specific — it does not touch the existing 1,000-foot Safe Stay buffers,
does not create a general buffer for other land uses, and does not change the citywide day/night
rules. Vancouver has, unusually, built the shelter first. The criticism belongs on the gap between
120 beds and the displaced population, and on the fact that several of the camps the buffer
reaches sit on **WSDOT rights-of-way** — state land the city is now closing to overnight use
without, in this packet, any accompanying WSDOT agreement.

**Where it is going.** First reading 14 September on **consent**. Second reading and **public
hearing 21 September**. So there are two openings: Community Communication on the 14th (to ask a
councilmember to pull it), and the hearing on the 21st.

*"Budget Impact: No budget impact is anticipated"* (p. 511) — for an ordinance whose own challenge
list anticipates *"additional signage or mapping, as well as outreach, cleanup and enforcement
activity."*

Staff Report 178-26, packet pp. 498–508. Staff contact Ian Alger, Housing Project Planner.

| Applicant | Project | AHF units | Total units | New award | Existing award | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Community Roots | Mill Plain Project | 17 | 24 | $1,500,000 | $1,050,000 | $2,550,000 |
| Housing Initiative / VHA | The Claudia | 20 | 60 | $1,000,000 | $2,000,000 | $3,000,000 |
| Palindrome | Heights Site C Artifact | ~40 | 100 | $2,000,000 | $4,100,000 | $6,100,000 |
| Related NW / VHA | Heights Site P Family | ~26 | 83 | $1,000,000 | $3,000,000 | $4,000,000 |
| Related NW / VHA | Heights Site P Senior | ~26 | 51 | $3,000,000 | $1,000,000 | $4,000,000 |
| Hacienda CDC / YWCA | Main Street Project | ~6 | 85 | $1,000,000 | $0 | $1,000,000 |
| **Totals** | | **~135** | **403** | **$9,500,000** | **$11,150,000** | **$20,650,000** |

**The good part, said plainly.** Seven applications sought $20.6M; six are funded. These are real
projects — permanent supportive housing for households exiting homelessness, senior housing with
a 20% homelessness set-aside, family housing with a 20% disability set-aside. The problem being
solved is real and is not of the city's making: **only one project in Vancouver has received a
State Housing Trust Fund award in the past two years, leaving 564 prospective units stuck**
(p. 498). State HTF applications are due **21 September**, one week after this vote — which is why
the Hacienda/YWCA award is conditioned on winning HTF and expires if they do not. The timing is
not an accident and the item is not padding.

**The thing to look at: $2M moves out of rental assistance.** The AHF Administrative Plan
allocates $6M/yr to production and preservation and $2M/yr to rental assistance. This resolution
funds the awards with *"$7.5 million in housing production and preservation funds through 2028,
plus $2 million in funds that would be moved over from the AHF rental assistance category"*
(p. 499). The stated reason is administrative — *"the current slow pace of spending observed in
the rental assistance category."* **Nothing in the packet explains why the 2026 rental assistance
money went unspent**, and "slow to spend" and "not needed" are different findings. Rental
assistance is the only AHF category that reaches a household at the bottom of the market this
year; production at 30–60% AMI reaches tenants in 2028 and later. Staff say the transfer *"will be
evaluated annually"* — the ask is that the evaluation be public, and that the underspend be
diagnosed rather than absorbed.

**A discrepancy inside the item.** The staff report says *"All projects serve households earning
30–60% of area median income"* (p. 499); the resolution's own recital says the awards *"promote
housing opportunities for households earning very low income, at or below 50% of the area median
income"* (p. 505). Both are in the same packet. 60% AMI and 50% AMI are not the same commitment,
and the resolution is the operative document.

**Smith Tower is the one that lost.** HDC-NW / Mid-Columbia Manor asked for more money to preserve
**170 units of downtown senior housing** and got nothing, because *"its cost per unit is
significantly higher than other applications due to the challenges of retrofitting a circular
building"* (p. 500). Preservation of occupied deep-affordable stock competing against new
production on cost-per-unit is a defensible screen and a consequential one: the 170 households in
Smith Tower are already housed at a rent the market will not reproduce. Its existing $3M award
stands; what it needed was more.

**Where the money is going geographically.** Three of the six projects are in the Heights, and
they carry **$14.1M of the $20.65M total city commitment (68%)** and **234 of 403 units (58%)**.
That is a deliberate concentration in one redevelopment district, and it is the same district
whose public infrastructure design contract is consent item 4 below.

**Scale, from the background analysis in `context/` (§7 and the 10 Aug 2026 AHF deck).** Vancouver's
own comprehensive plan prices the annual below-market subsidy gap at **$236.9M/yr**. $9.5M is about
4% of one year of that. This is not an argument against the awards — the same analysis records
$67.6M of AHF awards leveraging $588.3M from other sources, an **8.7× multiple**, which is what
these awards are doing again. It is an argument against treating the AHF as the answer. The
binding constraint here is state gap financing, and the 564 stalled units are the evidence.

### 3. From the City Manager — the two November statewide initiatives

Memo from Assistant City Manager Aaron Lande and Policy and Program Manager Katherine Kelly,
packet pp. 562–563; full initiative texts attached at pp. 564–578. Requested at the **10 August**
Council meeting. **No action is listed — this is a discussion item.** If Council is going to take a
position by resolution, this is where that starts, and Community Communication is the public's
only slot on it.

The memo notes its summaries come from **Ballotpedia**. The initiative texts are attached in full
and were read for this brief. Both contain material the summaries do not.

**IL26-638 — school sports.** Adds a new section to chapter 28A.600 RCW requiring school districts
and the WIAA to *"prohibit biologically male students from competing with and against female
students"* in female-only competition. Two things beyond the summary:

- **The verification requirement is universal, not targeted.** §3(2): *any* student who elects to
  participate in a female-only activity must provide, with the routine sports physical, *"a health
  examination and consent form or other statement signed by the student's personal health care
  provider that verifies the student's biological sex, relying only on … reproductive anatomy,
  genetic makeup, or normal endogenously produced testosterone levels."* That is a new paperwork
  and medical-attestation burden on **every girl who plays a school sport in Washington**, not only
  on trans students. Families without a regular provider carry it hardest.
- **It carves an exception into the state's anti-sex-discrimination statute.** §4 amends
  **RCW 28A.640.020(1)(c)** — the provision requiring athletics be *"offered to all students without
  regard to sex"* — to read *"except as provided in section 3 of this act."* This is not a
  standalone rule bolted on; it is an amendment to the chapter that prohibits sex discrimination in
  Washington schools.

**IL26-001 — "Washington Parents' Bill of Rights."** Amends RCW 28A.605.005 as amended by
**HB 1296 (2025 c 369 s 501)**. Mechanically, it **strikes subsections (1)–(4) of current law in
their entirety** and replaces them with a new list, so anything not restated is gone. Reading the
struck text against the replacement:

- **Gone:** the right to a public education *"in a setting in which discrimination on the basis
  of sex, race, creed, religion, color, national origin, honorably discharged veteran or military
  status, sexual orientation, gender expression, gender identity, [or] the presence of any sensory,
  mental, or physical disability … is prohibited."* The replacement's nearest provision, new (i),
  is a right against discrimination *"based upon the sincerely held religious beliefs of the child's
  family."* A protected-class list is replaced by a religious-belief clause.
- **Gone:** the right to file a harassment, intimidation and bullying complaint on a child's behalf
  under RCW 28A.600.477.
- **Gone:** the right to annual notice of the school's language access policies and Title VI free
  language-access services under RCW 28A.183.040.
- **Narrowed, and the memo does not mention it:** the records shield. Current law lets a school
  withhold *"health care, social work, counseling, or disciplinary records"* from a parent who is a
  **criminal defendant where the student is the named victim** *or* the target of a child-abuse
  investigation. The replacement (4) covers only *"medical or health records or mental health
  counseling records"*, and only during a child-abuse or neglect investigation. **The
  criminal-defendant-where-the-child-is-the-victim protection is deleted**, as are social work and
  disciplinary records from the shield.
- **Also deleted, also unmentioned:** *"Nothing in this section creates a private right of action."*
  Removing that bar does not itself create a cause of action, but combined with the new §2
  (*"provisions of this act are to be liberally construed"*) it is a change in enforceability worth
  asking the City Attorney about.
- **And the lock-in:** the initiative's own §1(1) states that voter approval *"provides two years
  of constitutional protection from legislative interference."* Under Washington Const. art. II
  §41, the legislature cannot amend a voter-approved initiative for two years except by two-thirds
  of both houses.

*I read the operative sections of both initiatives in the packet, not the whole 15 pages. The
struck/inserted direction is encoded here as `((deleted))` — the standard RCW bill convention —
so unlike a drawn redline it is unambiguous, and I checked it: the deletion opens at
`(((1) The legislature finds` and closes at `private right of action.))`.*

### Development Fee Updates — 4:00pm, ~45 min (packet pp. 7–21)

Chad Eiken (Community Development), Steven Wall (Public Works), Patrick Quinton (Economic
Development).

Where Vancouver's fees stand today, from the city's own modelled projects (pp. 10–11):

| | 198-unit mixed-use residential | 100-unit 3-storey multifamily |
|---|---|---|
| Total fees, 2024 | $2,583,207 | $1,397,197 |
| Total fees, 2026 | $2,956,035 | $1,534,735 |
| **Per unit, 2026** | **$14,929** | **$15,347** |
| **% of development cost** | **2.87%** | **3.65%** |

**The mover nobody voted on:** Fire permit and review fees rose **+152.4%** on the mixed-use
project and **+98.4%** on the multifamily one between 2024 and 2026, and **+116.3%** on a 40,000 sq
ft commercial project. Every other line moved single digits. Those are cost-recovery permit fees,
not impact fees — but they are the reason "fees went up" is true, and they are not what the
workshop is about.

**What is proposed** (p. 13): update traffic impact fees; update water and sewer SDCs; **add a
commercial park impact fee**; add engineering review and inspection fees; and index everything to
CPI annually. The capital programme behind it (p. 15) is large and specific — **$300M PFAS
programme** plus a **$400M 20-year water CIP**, a **$300M sewer solids renewal programme** plus
$250M sewer CIP, a **$345M transportation capital budget** (32nd Street Bypass $86M, 59th Street
$23M, 18th Street $14M, 104th Avenue $11M, Mill Plain $11M), and 800+ acres of park acquisition
against a $166M parks plan.

**The commercial park impact fee is the progressive item here.** Parks impact fees are currently
**N/A on commercial and industrial development** (p. 11) — the city's own framing is that
*"commercial and industrial development does not share burden of new open space investments."*
Making them share it is straightforwardly good, and it is scheduled last, in **2028** (p. 20),
after everything else.

**The framing to push back on.** Slide 12 is headed *"Impacts on New Development — Any increase in
fees will make new development more expensive,"* and slide 19's menu includes *"Pause new increases
until market conditions improve."* Set that against the city's own numbers: impact fees and SDCs
run **2–3% of development cost**, while total development cost per affordable unit went from
**$200k to $400k since 2016** (AHF deck, 10 Aug 2026, in `context/`). A 3% fee is not the binding
constraint, and the background analysis in `context/` §3 finds zoned capacity is not either. The
constraint is capital cost and gap financing. Slide 14 states the other half honestly: *"The City
risks underfunding necessary infrastructure."*

**The genuine complication, which cuts the other way.** Staff are right that *"fees typically make
up a larger percentage of development costs in smaller projects (middle housing)"* — a flat
per-unit fee is regressive across project size. The fix named on slide 19 is *"expand discounts and
exemptions — as allowed by statute — to cover more types of development: affordable housing,
child-care, transit-oriented development."* **That is the sentence to hold them to**, and October
is when to do it.

**Timeline** (pp. 20–21): traffic impact fees — workshop **October**, Council action **December**,
effective Q3 2027. Park impact fees, water/sewer SDCs, engineering and inspection fees — Q3 2027.
Commercial park impact fee — 2028.

### VMC Title 19 parking clean-up and scofflaw programme — ~4:45pm (packet pp. 22–36)

Gabriel Montez, Parking District Manager. **This one has the shortest fuse on the page: the
ordinance is scheduled for the consent agenda on 5 October and a public hearing on 12 October**
(p. 36).

Three things bundled together. The first is uncontroversial: degendering and modernising the code
across ~46 sections.

**Second, fines rise** (p. 26), first change since 2017:

| | 2017 | Proposed | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment / Overtime | $25 / $30 | $40 / $40 | +60% / +33% |
| Improper parking | $35 | $40 | +14% |
| **Prohibited parking** | **$40** | **$65** | **+63%** |
| Improper license plate | $50 | $65 | +30% |

The comparison table puts the proposed Vancouver rates **above Tacoma and Spokane** on most lines
and below Seattle and Portland.

**Third, and this is the one:** a scofflaw programme adding **immobilisation (boots) and
impoundment** as enforcement tools for **4 or more citations unpaid for 45+ days** (p. 28).

The city's own numbers (pp. 30–32): **48% of fines issued in 2025 and 2026 are still outstanding**
— $272,000 of $560,000 in 2025, $337,000 of $702,000 estimated in 2026. Over 2022–2026, **1,110
vehicles** would qualify as scofflaw — under 2% of cited vehicles, 15% of vehicles with delinquent
citations, but **62% of delinquent fines** ($707,566 of ~$1.15M). And **47% of unpaid prohibited
and license-plate citations are on out-of-state or no-plate vehicles**, which is the gap the
programme exists to close.

**The left read.** A collection mechanism aimed at repeat offenders who can pay is legitimate, and
the process is not thin: minimum **15 notices and 62 days** before impound, with appeal and payment
plans available at every step, plus an amnesty programme in Q3 before the programme goes live in
Q4 (pp. 35–36). Vancouver's threshold matches Seattle's and Spokane's and is more generous than
Olympia's.

**The problem is who the residual population is.** The presentation's own "Common Issues" slide
(p. 33) is three photographs: **no license plate, obscured VIN, defaced VIN**. That is also the
profile of a vehicle someone is living in. Vancouver already bans habitating a vehicle parked on
any street, park or public lot between 6:30am and 9:30pm (VMC 8.22.040(D)) — so a vehicle resident
accrues parking citations as a condition of existing, cannot pay them, and under this programme
gets booted and then towed. **Losing the vehicle is losing the housing**, and impound and storage
fees make recovery harder than the original fine. Nothing in the presentation addresses vehicle
residency, and there is no stated exemption or diversion path for it.

**The concrete asks, for October:** an exemption or HART-referral diversion for vehicles in use as
a residence; a genuine ability-to-pay standard on the payment plans rather than a schedule; and a
count of how many of the 1,110 scofflaw vehicles are registered to people with no other address —
which the city can produce and has not.

## What I could not check

- **How many people currently camp inside the 1,500-foot buffer.** The staff report says
  *"significant camping … at several nearby locations"* and does not count them. Without that
  number, "120 beds against the displaced population" cannot be evaluated, and the packet offers no
  way to derive it.
- **What the buffer map actually covers.** Attachment B (fileId 4971) is a three-page graphic; a
  `pdftotext` pass returns only the labels "1000 Feet / 1500 Feet / 2000 Feet". Which streets,
  parcels and WSDOT rights-of-way fall inside the line has to be read off the map by eye, and I
  could not do that here. **If one thing gets checked before the 21 September hearing, it is this.**
- **Whether WSDOT has agreed.** Several of the camps the buffer is drawn to reach are on WSDOT
  rights-of-way. No interagency agreement or WSDOT correspondence is in the packet.
- **What period the $3,588,391.51 Do Good payment covers.** Neither the staff report nor
  Amendment No. 1 states a period for the Phase 2 amount, and the amendment does not change the
  agreement's October 2029 end date. The per-bed figure above is arithmetic on an unstated
  denominator and should be treated as such.
- **The Washington case law on impounding a vehicle used as a residence.** There is a Washington
  Supreme Court decision (*City of Seattle v. Long*, 2018/2019) holding the state homestead act
  applies to a vehicle used as a principal residence and analysing impound-related penalties under
  the excessive fines clause. **I could not retrieve the opinion** — courts.wa.gov returned an HTML
  interstitial and Justia is behind a JS challenge — so the citation and its scope are **unverified
  here**. It is the right question to put to the City Attorney before the 12 October hearing, not a
  finding to quote.
- **The full text of both ballot initiatives.** I read the operative sections — IL26-001 §1 and
  IL26-638 §§1–4 — not all fifteen attached pages. The struck-text direction is encoded and was
  verified, but I did not compare every subsection of the new list against every subsection of the
  old.
- **Why the AHF's 2026 rental assistance allocation went unspent.** The packet asserts a *"slow
  pace of spending"* and nothing more.
- **The 2027-2028 Financial Policies in full** — a 20-page attachment. The staff report, the
  Designated Reserve table, the debt-service cap and the equity/asset-management sections were
  read; the rest was scanned.
- **The First Forty Feet amendment history.** Attachments B–H are the six prior executed
  amendments and the original contract, roughly 7MB. I read Amendment No. 7 and the staff report;
  I did not reconstruct how the contract went from its 2022 value to $10.05M amendment by
  amendment.
