What we explored
Over the past year, staff conducted research and community conversations to better
understand the landscape for climate-friendly home improvements in Vancouver. The
work focused on answering three questions:
   1. What is the local opportunity? Housing stock, household characteristics, and
       climate vulnerability.
   2. What support already exists? Existing programs, resources, and opportunities for
       coordination.
   3. What do residents need and want? Priorities, barriers, and desired types of
       support.

Local Housing Context
Staff reviewed housing characteristics such as ownership status and age of the housing
stock as well as existing climate vulnerability data to better understand where need and
opportunities may be concentrated. Older homes, for example, may have greater
opportunities for energy efficiency, weatherization, and other improvements.

A notable housing characteristic is that Vancouver’s housing tenure is nearly evenly split
between owners and renters. Homeowners generally have greater control over
improvements, whereas renters depend on property owners to make most significant
changes. These different circumstances require different approaches to supporting
improvements. Additionally, larger multifamily buildings are required by the WA Clean
Buildings Performance Standard to make energy efficiency improvements, while smaller
rental properties have few, if any, requirements and available resources. Therefore,
these smaller properties may represent an opportunity to reach homes where
improvements are mainly voluntary and often depend on available support.

The full background report is available in Attachment A.

Earlier this year, staff convened program administrators to better understand the
existing program landscape, identify gaps, and explore opportunities for coordination.
The discussion highlighted differences in eligibility, reach, capacity, and services offered.
A key gap is residents who exceed income limits for assistance but may still face barriers
to affording improvements. Existing programs also have limited capacity. For example,
the City’s Housing Rehabilitation Program has a waitlist of approximately 80–100
applicants, with wait times of up to two years.

Programs also tend to address specific improvements rather than the whole home which
may require residents to navigate multiple programs to address different needs. For
example, the HEAR program provides high-efficiency electric equipment and appliances
but does not cover weatherization, which can limit the benefits of HVAC improvements.
Better coordination could help residents identify and sequence improvements and make
better use of available resources. Finally, gaps in existing support for fuel-switching
improvements may create an opportunity for additional City involvement to reduce
building-related emissions.

Community Engagement
Staff engaged homeowners, renters, small property owners, and residents who
participated in the HEAR program. Several themes emerged:

    •   Residents generally prioritize affordable operating costs, healthy indoor air, and
        functional homes. Residents focus on the day-to-day experience of living in their
        home and the ability to remain safely and comfortably in their home over time,
        including as they grow older.
    •   Homeowners want practical support to figure out what to do and how to do it.
        Participants expressed strong interest in support to understand their home’s
        needs, access programs and trusted providers, and navigate the process. They
        also shared concerns about finding reliable contractors and understanding how
        to use new technologies.
    •   Rental housing requires approaches that address both tenant and property-
        owner needs. Renters were interested in energy-efficiency education and low-
        cost energy efficiency improvements they can make themselves. Property owners



         P.O. Box 1995 | Vancouver, WA 98668-1995 | 360-487-8000 | cityofvancouver.us

       prioritized tenant experience and property maintenance but were more receptive
       to climate-friendly improvements when there was a financial incentive or clear
       benefit. Participants specifically asked for information about available rebates
       and incentives and some mentioned that rebates influenced their decision to
       install a heat pump.

Potential approaches for City support
The research suggests several potential levels of City involvement that could build on
existing programs and partnerships. These approaches are not mutually exclusive and
can be tailored to different audiences and desired program outcomes.

   •   Inform. Provide practical education about energy efficiency, technology options,
       and available programs and incentives. Community feedback favored live,
       interactive workshops over static online tools as residents indicated that
       opportunities to ask questions and receive information directly were more useful
       for this topic. For renters, this could include information about improvements
       within their control. The rental registry could also provide a channel for reaching
       property owners and renters. This approach could reach a broad audience at
       relatively low cost but would be less likely to enable substantial improvements or
       overcome barriers such as upfront costs.
   •   Navigate. Provide personalized assistance to help residents understand their
       home's needs, connect with existing programs and incentives, and find trusted
       providers. Community feedback showed that residents request support not only
       identifying available resources, but also determining what steps to take, in what
       order, and how different programs or improvements fit together. This type of
       support could also help residents and property owners make better use of
       existing resources as well as coordinate multiple programs to maximize benefits.
       However, it would reach fewer residents than a broad information approach,
       require greater staff capacity, and would not address limited availability or
       waitlists for programs that are already at capacity.
   •   Fund. Provide financial assistance to address upfront costs, complement existing
       rebates and programs, or fill gaps for residents who exceed the income limits for
       current assistance. Upfront cost was a commonly cited barrier to larger home
       improvements, suggesting that financial assistance could enable improvements
       residents may otherwise be unable to afford. This could support larger-scale
       changes (such as replacing appliances or heating systems) that can achieve
       greater GHG reductions. However, this approach would likely reach fewer homes
       than lower-cost options and require greater City investment.

                                                  Introduction
                                                  Imagine you live in a single-family home built in
                                                  the 1950s. In July, the first heat wave of the
                                                  summer rolls in with temperatures much warmer
                                                  than you are used to in past years. By mid-
                                                  afternoon, the sun shines in and your house traps
                                                  the heat. With no air conditioning and little
                                                  insulation, as is common in older Vancouver
                                                  homes, finding relief is difficult. Fast forward to
                                                  February, you crank up your old furnace one cold
                                                  morning to keep the chill away. It runs
                                                  constantly to make up for the drafts slipping
                                                  through the walls and windows. You find comfort
                                                  in the moment, but only to face disappointment
                                                  later when the utility bill arrives.

                                                  Now imagine a different experience. A trained
                                                  contractor helps you through a simple process to
                                                  upgrade your home, including adding insulation,

                                                  safer and more comfortable homes that cost less

                                                  why this type of program is needed, highlights

                                                  key local context to guide its design.

Key Points
Problem Statement

Vancouver’s homes are increasingly vulnerable to climate and energy resource pressures. Extreme
weather events, rising temperatures, wildfire smoke, and high energy burdens create unsafe and
costly living conditions. At the same time, buildings account for 37% of the City’s communitywide
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and residential energy drives the largest share. Without action, the
most vulnerable residents will continue to face these hazards, while ongoing reliance on fossil fuels
locks in emissions that intensify climate impacts citywide.

Proposed Solution

Residential decarbonization reduces greenhouse gas emissions from homes by using less energy,
improving energy efficiency, and cutting reliance on fossil fuels. Interventions not only help
Vancouver meet its climate goals, but also importantly deliver direct economic and health benefits for
residents and the community. Interventions can make homes healthier, safer, and more comfortable
during extreme heat and wildfire smoke events by improving indoor air quality and temperature
control, while also lowering energy bills and supporting a more stable electrical grid. Further,
investments in electrification and renewable energy can create local jobs, build skills in the regional
workforce, and expand opportunities for workers and contractors from historically underrepresented
communities.

Opportunities for City Action

While strong local and state programs exist, their scale and scope must be expanded to meet
community needs and climate goals. Key opportunities for City action and considerations for program
design focused on the decarbonization of residential buildings are described below. Considered
actions target existing residential buildings, which offer the greatest potential for impact, as new
single-family and middle housing must already meet the Washington State Residential Energy Code,
and new multifamily development must comply with the City’s Green Building Program.

   1. Explore opportunities to scale or complement existing weatherization and electrification
      programs.
   2. Add or enhance programs to support whole-home, correctly sequenced upgrades (e.g., insulation
      before efficient appliances) to maximize benefits.
   3. Support residential electrification efforts as they offer some of the largest emissions-reduction
      potential.
   4. Create pathways for renters and small multifamily buildings with tenant protections.
   5. Improve outreach, coordination, and program navigation of existing and new residential
      decarbonization programs.
   6. Target program efforts towards residents who experience combined risks from climate impacts,
      social inequities, and health vulnerabilities. This may include residents in areas with poor air
      quality or high heat exposure, low-income households with high energy burdens, and the “near-
      eligible” residents who often exceed income limits for federal and state programs.

What is Decarbonization?
Decarbonization is one of the most important ways communities can respond to climate change.
There are two main parts to decarbonization. First, it involves reducing greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions that come from burning fossil fuels like gas, oil, and coal. This can be done by switching to
renewable energy sources such as wind, solar, hydropower, and geothermal as well as using
electricity instead of fossil fuels to power buildings and appliances. Improving energy efficiency is also
a key part of this work as homes that use less energy put less strain on energy systems and produce
fewer emissions overall. The second part of decarbonization focuses on removing carbon already in
the atmosphere through approaches which capture emissions or increase the carbon stored in natural
systems, like forests and soil (Cho, 2022). This report focuses on residential decarbonization, a subset
of decarbonization which addresses emissions from existing residential buildings. These emissions
primarily come from the energy used to heat and cool buildings, produce hot water, cook food, and
power household appliances.



Problem Overview
This section outlines the intersecting climate, health, energy, and housing challenges affecting
Vancouver’s homes as well as how homes, in turn, affect the climate. Understanding these issues
provides the foundation for why residential decarbonization is needed and what it must address.


Climate and Housing Challenges
Vancouver faces longstanding housing challenges that affect residents’ ability to stay safe and
healthy. Housing affordability is a persistent issue, with median home prices nearly tripling and
average rents increasing by 60 percent from 2012 to 2023 (Bump et al., 2024). Many households spend
a large share of their income on rent or mortgage payments, and over one-quarter of renters pay
more than half of their income on housing. The City also faces a shortage of safe and affordable
housing, with current estimates indicating that 38,129 additional housing units will be needed in
Vancouver by 2045 to meet the demands of a growing population (Bump et al., 2024). These pressures
leave families with limited resources to maintain their homes, invest in energy efficiency, or respond
to emergency repairs, making them more vulnerable to both financial hardship and displacement. In a
recent Community Needs Assessment Survey conducted by Clark County, low-income residents
reported food, housing, and utility assistance within their top five needs (Clark County Community
Services, 2024).

These social and economic pressures create a foundation of vulnerability that climate change is now
intensifying. Annual air temperatures in the region have risen by almost 2 degrees Fahrenheit since
1900, and more frequent heat events now push indoor temperatures to dangerous levels for residents
living in older, poorly insulated homes or without air-conditioning (Michael Chang et al., 2023).
Prolonged exposure to high heat can place significant strain on the heart and other vital organs. This
was shown during the 2021 Heat Dome, which caused record breaking temperatures that killed 126
Washingtonians and contributed to the deadliest weather-related disaster in Washington state history
(Vogel et al., 2023). These same homes that overheat in the summer often struggle to retain heat in
the winter, leaving residents vulnerable during cold periods. Homes with inadequate insulation or
persistent dampness can contribute to respiratory and cardiovascular issues (World Health

CITY OF VANCOUVER | RESIDENTIAL DECARBONIZATION BACKGROUND REPORT | MARCH 5, 2026              Page 305 of 126
Organization, 2018). Together, these conditions show that inadequate housing performance can turn
extreme weather into serious public health risk within homes.

In addition to temperature extremes, indoor air quality is also increasingly affected by climate change
and local pollution sources. Vancouver is identified by the Washington Department of Ecology as one
of the 16 communities in the state most impacted by air pollution due to the cumulative impacts of
recurring wildfire risks, resident proximity to highways, and exposure to industrial activities
(Washington State Department of Ecology, 2023). As a result, residents in these communities die an
average of 2.4 years earlier than those in the rest of the state, and are twice as likely to have poor
health (Washington State Department of Ecology, 2023). Wildfire events throughout the West bring
high concentrations of particulate matter to Vancouver, specifically tiny particles known as PM2.5,
which can cause adverse respiratory health effects (BERK Consulting, 2025). Local industrial emissions
also contribute to air pollution exposure, particularly for residents living near major facilities such as
Frito Lay, Great Western Malt, SEH America, and the River Road Generating Plant. In addition to these
outdoor sources, indoor air quality can be further degraded by fossil-fuel appliances within homes.
Gas stoves and other gas appliances produce nitrogen dioxide and release PM2.5 which increases the
risk of respiratory diseases such as asthma, especially in children (Seals, 2020).

Energy affordability – or lack of affordability – compounds these risks. When families struggle to
afford the electricity needed for cooling, heating, or ventilation, their health and safety suffer.
Households may be forced to choose between essential needs such as electricity, water, medications,
groceries, and other basic expenses. Missed utility bills can trigger debt collection and utility shutoffs
which creates financial hardships that are difficult to recover from and may lead to cascading impacts
over time. Research also shows a higher energy burden is associated with increased rates of mental
distress and physician-diagnosed depression (Han et al., 2024). In Washington, households that spend
more than 6% of their income on energy are considered energy-burdened, and roughly 9% of very low-
income households statewide experience this burden (Rubin et al., 2023). While county-specific data
are unavailable, Clark Public Utilities reports that more than 4,000 families in Clark County receive
federal Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) grants each year to help eligible
residents pay to heat their homes, regardless of heating type (Clark Public Utilities, n.d.). This
highlights the level of need in the community, although it reflects only those who access the program
and may therefore underrepresent the true level of need. These pressures limit residents’ ability to
take other actions to adapt to extreme temperatures and maintain healthy homes.

These housing-related climate impacts are also connected to pressures on the region’s energy system.
Rising temperatures increase the need for home cooling, which places additional stress on the
electrical grid, especially because electricity flows less efficiently during extreme heat (Michael
Chang et al., 2023). This combination heightens the risk of outages that directly affect household
safety and comfort, for instance by disrupting home cooling during extreme heat or interrupting the
operation of essential in-home medical and assistive devices that rely on electricity. Additionally, the
resulting system strain can translate into higher energy rates for residents down the line. At the same
time, earlier snowmelt and more frequent droughts are expected to limit the capacity of hydropower
generation and further strain the region’s hydro-dependent grid (Michael Chang et al., 2023).


Building Sector Emissions
As climate impacts intensify, it is important to recognize that buildings themselves are a major source
of emissions driving climate change. In Vancouver, the Buildings and Energy sector is the second
largest source of communitywide emissions, accounting for 37% of total GHG emissions. Residential
energy use – energy used where people live, eat and sleep – makes up the largest share within this


CITY OF VANCOUVER | RESIDENTIAL DECARBONIZATION BACKGROUND REPORT | MARCH 5, 2026               Page 316 of 126
sector at 15%, although residential buildings have lower emissions intensity compared to commercial
and industrial buildings (City of Vancouver Climate Program, 2024).

From 2019-2023, total energy use increased in Vancouver’s residential building sector. However, GHG
emissions remained steady overall and even decreased for some electric customers (City of
Vancouver Climate Program, 2024). This is due to the increasing share of clean energy in the Clark
Public Utilities (CPU) resource mix. In 2024, CPU sourced approximately 48% of its electricity from
Bonneville Power Administration hydropower, 29% from the River Road natural gas–fired plant, 6%
from nuclear power, 5% from wind, and 12% from market purchases of unspecified power sources
(Clark Public Utilities, 2024). In contrast, natural gas use and resulting emissions both increased by 5%
over the same period (City of Vancouver Climate Program, 2024).

This pattern reflects current household fuel use: approximately 67% of residential heating relies on
electricity, while 31% relies on utility gas, with small shares from wood (0.5%), bottled or tank gas
(0.4%), and fuel oil (0.2%) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024). Because electricity and gas are on different
decarbonization trajectories, this split has important emissions implications. In Washington, the Clean
Energy Transformation Act (CETA), requires an electricity supply free of GHG emissions by 2045
(Washington State Department of Commerce, 2024). As a result, homes heated with electricity will
increasingly benefit from a cleaner grid over time. In contrast, continued reliance on natural gas will
constrain emissions reductions. NW Natural has adopted a voluntary decarbonization target of 30%
carbon savings by 2035, but has so far been unsuccessful in meeting its self-imposed targets and had
only ~1% renewable natural gas production in 2024 (NW Natural, 2021) (Samayoa, 2026).


Importance of Residential Decarbonization
Given the intersecting climate, health, and energy challenges, residential decarbonization offers a
critical pathway to improve household financial, physical, and emotional well-being and meet the
City’s climate goals. Residential decarbonization refers to the suite of methods to reduce GHG
emissions from homes (ASHRAE, n.d.). This includes strategies to reduce energy demand through
weatherization, efficiency upgrades, and everyday energy-saving behaviors, alongside electrification,
fuel switching, and the use of clean energy to reduce emissions.

This work is central to meeting the emissions and equity goals outlined in Vancouver’s Climate Action
Framework (CAF). The CAF calls for a transition to 100% clean energy and a significant reduction in
per capita energy use to reach carbon neutrality by 2040 (City of Vancouver, 2022). Because
investments in homes last for many decades, any choices made today, whether through new
construction or retrofit projects, will have long-term implications. Without intentional action, the
community risks locking in carbon-intensive systems that will be difficult and expensive to replace
later.

Residential decarbonization also delivers everyday benefits for residents and the community. Better
insulation, efficient heat pumps and household appliances, and improved ventilation make homes
safer and more comfortable during extreme heat, cold, and wildfire smoke events. Reduced energy
use helps stabilize the grid and avoid cost spikes. Building upgrades may also lower utility bills,
improve air quality, and support long-term housing stability. For example, a recent study of the
Cascadia region found that 76% of low-income households, including both homeowners and renters,
could save on average $500 annually on energy bills by upgrading to a medium-efficiency heat pump,
assuming they can overcome the upfront costs (Pelai & Moore, 2025). At the same time, investments in
electrification and renewable energy can create local jobs, build skills in the regional workforce, and
expand opportunities for workers and contractors from historically underrepresented communities.
Together, these interventions help build a safe and more resilient community as climate risks grow.

CITY OF VANCOUVER | RESIDENTIAL DECARBONIZATION BACKGROUND REPORT | MARCH 5, 2026              Page 327 of 126
However, the residential sector is complex. It involves a wide range of decision-makers, including
homeowners, renters, landlords, property managers, and contractors who each bring different
priorities, resources, and access to information. High upfront costs and the time investment needed
for upgrades can deter investment for many households. For instance, to purchase and install an air
source heat pump in Vancouver costs approximately $13,0001, and is generally more expensive
upfront than fossil-fuel heating due to expensive manufacturing and labor as well as potential
electrical upgrades needed to support the system (Pelai & Moore, 2025). Renters often lack authority
to make major improvements, while property owners may not be incentivized to make investments
when utility costs are typically paid by residents. There is also a need to ensure that any
improvements landlords make to properties do not unintentionally lead to rent increases or
displacement (Walker et al., 2023).

Given both the urgency and complexity of this work, the following sections provide an overview of
existing initiatives and local contextual factors to inform the development of an effective residential
decarbonization program for Vancouver.


Review of Existing Initiatives
The City, the local utilities, County, and state agencies collectively administer a variety of programs
locally that together support residential decarbonization goals. While these efforts provide a strong
foundation, they will need to be expanded and scaled to achieve the City’s ambitious GHG reduction
goals. This section summarizes existing programs and their current limitations, based on
conversations with program staff, to highlight opportunities for City intervention.

City of Vancouver Housing Rehabilitation Program: The City provides affordable home repair
financing for income-eligible homeowners to help preserve existing housing. Participants can receive
up to $35,000 in low-interest (2%) loans with no monthly payments, repaid only when the home is sold,
refinanced, or no longer owner-occupied. Eligible repairs include structural and safety issues,
weatherization, and major systems such as roofing, plumbing, and electrical. The program is funded
through U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) Community Development Block
Grant (CDBG) and HOME Investment Partnership.

Additionally, this program received funding from the Washington State Home Electrification and
Appliance Rebates Program (HEAR) to provide rebates and incentives for high-efficiency electric
equipment and appliances in 2024 and 2025. The City partnered with Northwest Native Chamber and
the Hispanic Metropolitan Chamber to deliver these incentives to residents. During the first round of
HEAR funding, the City and its partners received 219 applications and were able to serve 55
households and 4 small businesses. Of these households, 94% had incomes below 80% of area median
income, 45% identified as BIPOC, 30% included residents over age 65, and the average household
health disparity score was 8.4. Among contractors, 43% were BIPOC or women-owned, and all
businesses served were BIPOC-owned.

Limitations:

      •    The Home Rehabilitation Program faces a wait list of approximately 80–100 applicants,
           resulting in wait times of up to two years.
      •    Weatherization projects are often not pursued because urgent structural repairs, such as a roof
           replacement, typically consume the full loan amount.

Climate Vulnerability
Not all residents are equally affected by climate change. Vulnerability depends on three factors:
exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. Exposure refers to the presence of hazards such as
extreme heat, flooding, or poor air quality. Sensitivity reflects how much a system or population may
be affected by hazards and depends on factors such as age and pre-existing health conditions.
Adaptive capacity describes the ability of a person, asset, or system to cope or adjust to a hazard,
which is often influenced by housing quality, income, and access to resources (U.S. Climate Resilience
Toolkit, 2021). Buildings themselves can also be vulnerable when their design, condition, or systems
make them more susceptible to climate hazards, which in turn affects the people living in them.

As mentioned above, a recent Community Needs Assessment Survey conducted by Clark County found
low-income residents reported housing and utility assistance within their top five needs, highlighting
the extent to which financial and housing instability reduce adaptive capacity in this area (Clark
County Community Services, 2024). These same constraints also make it harder for these households
to participate in residential decarbonization efforts, even though they are among those who would
benefit most. Recognizing these barriers provides essential context for designing programs that
improve both climate resilience and energy outcomes.

Green Workforce
A strong workforce is essential to advancing residential decarbonization efforts. At the same time,
these programs inherently generate demand for this work. Therefore, residential decarbonization
programs have the potential to intentionally build and support career pathways for workers and
contractors from underrepresented communities. Just as we aim for program participation to reach
those most harmed by climate change and the health and economic inequities described above, we
also want to ensure the economic and professional benefits of this work reach the same communities.
Program goals should be structured to advance workforce development while ensuring equitable
opportunities for underrepresented workers and contractors.

Within the program design process, it will be important to clearly define the specific aspect of the
workforce we are aiming to advance or address. Preliminary conversations suggest several areas to
explore:

   1.   Skills and Training

        •   Addressing workforce gaps and building skills: While the region has robust training
            programs through traditional trade apprenticeships, registered apprenticeships can
            sometimes present barriers for underrepresented groups, such as women or tribal members.
            Residential decarbonization programs can intentionally support the expansion of skill-
            building opportunities for those historically excluded from trades. A key way to do this is
            through collaborations with community partners who can help ensure that training
            programs and workforce opportunities reach such groups. For example, the NW Native
            Chamber has dedicated initiatives to equip Native and frontline communities with
            knowledge and skills in renewable energy industries.
        •   Inclusive workplace culture: Job site culture trainings, similar to those often required in the
            commercial sector, can be applied to residential work to ensure all workers operate in a
            positive and supportive environment. This can also benefit business owners by helping to
            retain their workforce. Programs like Greendot or RISE Up can be used to facilitate these
            trainings.

        2. Connection to Opportunities

        •   On-the-job experience: Building skills is important, but providing access to paid work
            opportunities is equally critical. Program design can incorporate requirements to ensure
            underrepresented workers gain practical experience. For example, the City partnered with
            the NW Native Chamber and the Hispanic Metropolitan Chamber to administer the HEAR
            grant, reaching a workforce in which 43% of contractors were BIPOC or women-owned.
        •   Partnering with workforce organizations: Workforce Southwest Washington, which focuses
            on placing people into quality jobs, has a growing clean energy focus that aligns with
            building decarbonization and retrofitting efforts. The city could consider a partnership with
            this organization to train and place residents in residential decarbonization projects.
        •   Leveraging contractor networks: CPU and ETO maintain networks of vetted contractors.
            However, conversations with ETO suggest local coverage in Southwest Washington is
            limited. The skills building opportunities above can support expansion of these contractor
            networks to ensure residents in Southwest Washington have access to high-quality services,
            while also connecting trained workers and underrepresented contractors to projects in their
            own communities.

Policy Landscape
Washington has made meaningful progress on building decarbonization, but significant gaps remain
for existing residential buildings. The Washington 2021 State Energy Strategy provides a road map for
the state’s clean energy transition and highlights building decarbonization as not only an essential
part of meeting the state’s GHG emission limits and climate goals, but also the opportunity for
decarbonization actions to address inequities through enhancing resilience, growing the clean energy
workforce, and improving health outcomes. Existing state policy primarily targets large commercial
and multifamily properties through the Clean Buildings Performance Standard, which requires energy-
efficiency upgrades for buildings above specific size thresholds. New development is addressed
through the Washington State Energy Code and, locally, Vancouver’s forthcoming Green Building
Policy will strengthen standards for new construction. However, no comparable policy framework
exists for existing single-family homes or smaller residential buildings. As a result, upgrades are
entirely voluntary and depend on individual home or property owner choices and financial capacity.

Additionally, state incentive programs that support residential decarbonization can vary in availability
depending on funding cycles. Some programs are short-term or time-limited and may not always be
accompanied by robust outreach to cities which makes it difficult to assess how effectively they are
reaching local households.


Recommended Areas for City Action
Based on the findings of this report, the following are preliminary opportunities where City
intervention may be considered in the next phase of program design. These recommendations will
continue to be revised and refined as the team undertakes a detailed review of program models and
lessons from other cities as well as engages the community to learn about their needs.

   1. Explore opportunities to scale or complement existing weatherization and electrification
      programs

Existing weatherization and electrification programs are primarily funded through federal or state
grants, which have finite resources and specifications on eligible households and allowable services.
As a result, these programs are not able to meet the scale or breadth of need across Vancouver’s
housing stock. For example, both the City’s Housing Rehabilitation Program and the County’s
Weatherization Program currently have waitlists. Further, many older homes, particularly those built
before 1980, prior to major energy code updates, require insulation and building envelope
improvements that the Home Rehabilitation program often cannot address due to competing repair
CITY OF VANCOUVER | RESIDENTIAL DECARBONIZATION BACKGROUND REPORT | MARCH 5, 2026             Page 431 8of 126
needs and funding shortfalls. There are also opportunities to expand access to high-efficiency electric
equipment and appliances by building on the existing HEAR grant program, which currently has a
waitlist, and by increasing utilization of CPU and ETO rebates. These rebates may be underutilized
due to limited awareness of available programs or because the incentive amounts are not large
enough to motivate participation.

Given these dynamics, the City should weigh the benefits and tradeoffs of scaling existing programs,
which have established infrastructure and expertise but stricter eligibility and funding constraints,
versus developing a complementary local program that operates with greater flexibility while
connecting residents to these existing services.

   2. Support whole-home, sequenced retrofits

Program design should prioritize comprehensive, whole-home retrofits rather than isolated upgrades
to achieve maximum benefits for residents and the climate. For example, the current HEAR program
funds high-efficiency electric appliances but does not cover insulation or air sealing prior to
installation. This can limit the effectiveness of HVAC upgrades which deliver maximum energy savings
and resident benefits, such as reduced utility bills, when paired with weatherization measures.
Conversely, certain appliances, like water heaters or dishwashers, can deliver full benefits without
additional interventions. Programs should therefore consider both the appropriate sequencing and
combination of upgrades.

A comprehensive retrofit package may also include resident education to ensure households can use
new systems effectively and adopt energy-saving behaviors (e.g., turning off lights) to fully realize
program benefits. Finally, depending on program goals, whole-home retrofits may prioritize cooling
measures to enhance resilience during extreme heat while also incorporating efficiency upgrades to
minimize overall energy demand.

   3. Lead on residential electrification
The City is in a unique position to incentivize high-efficiency electric systems given that CPU is legally
restricted from supporting these upgrades and other programs have not focused on this area. This is
especially important as roughly 31% of all residential buildings in Vancouver rely on natural gas (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2024), and electrification offers some of the largest emissions-reduction potential.

   4. Create dedicated pathways for renters and multifamily buildings
With nearly half of Vancouver residents living in rental housing and 18% residing in plexes (duplexes,
triplexes, and quadplexes), decarbonization efforts must intentionally include programs tailored to
these groups. This recommendation focuses on smaller existing multifamily buildings that will not be
legally required to comply with Washington’s Clean Buildings Performance Standard which is aimed
at buildings over 20,000 square feet. Targeting these buildings offers an opportunity to maximize
impact as investments in these units may stretch public dollars further.

However, since 57% of renters are cost-burdened in Vancouver, programs must protect tenants from
added financial strain or displacement by prioritizing strong tenant protections (such as drawing from
the Anti-Displacement Strategy designed for Central Vancouver) and ensure benefits flow to residents.
At the same time, programs should address split incentives by creating appealing opportunities for
property owners—such as financial incentives for upgrades or integrating improvements into
affordable housing programs—so that investments support vulnerable populations while encouraging
broad participation. Over the longer term, the City could also explore linking the Rental Registry to
basic energy performance or upgrade requirements. Finally, since renters have limited ability to make


CITY OF VANCOUVER | RESIDENTIAL DECARBONIZATION BACKGROUND REPORT | MARCH 5, 2026               Page 441 9of 126
building-level improvements themselves, complementary resident education can help maximize
benefits while empowering households to participate meaningfully in climate action.

   5. Improve coordination, navigation, and information sharing across programs
To make existing programs more accessible and effective, the City should centralize program
information, such as through a one-stop resource center, and conduct targeted dissemination to
groups that may not have previously received information. Current programs report challenges in
reaching segments of the population outside existing City networks.

Depending on the mix of existing and upcoming programs, the City could also play a more active role
in coordinating or referring participants to appropriate pathways to ensure interventions are
completed in the most effective sequence and all existing incentives (from CPU, city, state, and
federal programs) are fully utilized. This coordination could also include connecting participants to
workforce opportunities.

Achieving this role will require intentional partnerships with community-based organizations and
trusted community messengers, along with new models of funded collaboration. These partnerships
should be built into program design from the outset and prioritize co-creation with communities to
reach residents where they are.

   6. Ensure Programs Serve Residents with the Greatest Climate and Social Vulnerabilities
While any program expansion or creation will benefit all Vancouver residents by reducing greenhouse
gas emissions and improving air quality, programs should intentionally center residents who
experience combined risks from climate impacts, social inequities, and health vulnerabilities, as
highlighted in the maps above. These maps can help identify the target populations such as areas
with poor air quality or high heat exposure. Additional populations to consider include low-income
households with high energy burdens, residents of manufactured homes, and the near-eligible
households who exceed income limits for federal and state programs. Determining the program focus
will require combining data from this report with program goals and community input to identify
where interventions can have the greatest impact while advancing equity .


Next Steps
Building on the findings from this report, the next phase will involve reviewing successful program
designs from other cities to identify approaches that best fit Vancouver’s context. In parallel, the team
will evaluate climate funding options to support implementation. Input from City staff, local
organizations, and community members will be incorporated to ensure the program is well-aligned
with local needs. These insights, along with a recommended funding source, will be compiled into a
final proposal for City leadership to consider a pilot program.

Present Situation
The Multifamily Tax Exemption (MFTE) program is an incentive tool used by Washington jurisdictions
to incentivize the construction of new multifamily housing and affordable units in designated
Residential Target Areas (RTAs). The Vancouver MFTE program was initially adopted in 1997 and
last updated in 2025.


In 2025 the Washington State Legislature passed two house bills, HB 1491 and HB 1494, related to
the MFTE program, which substantially alter and expand the MFTE program under Chapter 84.14
RCW.

House Bill 1491 is a Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) integration initiative related to development
standards within defined transit "station areas", which include parcels within ¼ of the C-Tran bus
rapid transit stops. Municipalities are required to adopt the HB 1491 requirements at the time of their
community comprehensive plan updates. Vancouver is the first jurisdiction in the State required to
comply with the new requirements. As the first jurisdiction, Vancouver is collaborating with the
Department of Commerce to pilot and navigate through the complexities of new regulations. This
ordinance amendment covers only the MFTE program related changes of House Bill 1491. Currently,


                                                                                             Page 49 of 126
the Vancouver’s MFTE program is superseded in station areas under a state preemptive TOD model
development code until the local ordinance amendments are adopted. No MFTE development
application proposals or inquiries have been submitted within the station area boundary under the
pre-emptive TOD model code.

House Bill 1491: Mandated MFTE program changes:

The MFTE program component of HB 1491 mandates that cities create and offer a 20-year TOD
MFTE program option in designated "station areas" (parcels within a ¼ mile radius from bus rapid
transit lines). Station areas are not considered Residential Target Areas under VMC 3.22. The station
area boundaries and development standards will be located and administered in the Vancouver
Municipal Code under section 20.530. While not identical to existing MFTE target areas, the station
areas overlap substantially with existing areas along the Mill Plain and Fourth Plain corridors. The
map below shows the relationship between the station area and MFTE residential target area
boundaries. While the City is mandated to offer a 20-year TOD option, participation in the program by
applicants is optional. Where overlap occurs between the two boundaries, the applicant may choose
the MFTE option they prefer.

The new 20-year TOD exemption will function essentially in the same manner as the existing 8 and
12-year options in terms of process and timing. There are still an application and fee, a 3-year
conditional certificate construction window and final certificate application process. The primary
differences for the 20-year TOD option include: a longer 50-year affordability compliance period with
a recorded deed covenant, specific affordability criteria options, a mandatory 50% traffic impact fee
reduction, and a direct administrative approval.


The TOD option exemption term is 20 years, and the affordability maintenance period is 50 years.
MFTE projects constructed within a station area must, by recorded covenant, agree to at least one of
the following affordability and income eligibility options for fifty years:

    • At least 10 percent of all units are restricted to households earning at or below 60% AMI; or
    • At least 10% of all units are restricted to households earning at or below 80% AMI, where at
      least 10% of the restricted units are family-sized units with more than two bedrooms; or
    • At least 20% of all units are restricted to households earning at or below 80% AMI.



Due to the extended length of affordability, a deed restrictive covenant is required to be recorded on
the property to ensure long-term tracking and monitoring of the exemptions.The conditional certificate
process for approval of the 20-year TOD option is now administrative, without public hearing,
provided that the TOD affordability criteria above are met. Lastly, cities are mandated to provide an
automatic 50% reduction to traffic impact fees for eligible projects.

House Bill 1494: Administrative Reforms and Flexibility:

In addition to the TOD affordability requirements and incentives, House Bill 1494 includes a series of
policy-level reforms intended to make the MFTE program more versatile for local governments by
addressing known loopholes or unclear code provisions within the current Revised Code of
Washington (RCW) code language.

To ensure continued tracking and monitoring for 12-year ownership projects, a recorded deed
restriction covenant will now be an added requirement. For exemptions with potential for changes in
ownership, the covenant requirement will help maintain a clear chain of title providing potential buyers

                                                                                              Page 50 of 126
with notice of the MFTE program restrictions.

To address unnecessary cancelations of multi-owner MFTE exemptions when a single unit owner
becomes non-compliant, HB 1494 now provides that only the non-compliant owner will lose the
exemption status. The MFTE exemption for the remaining compliant owners will not be canceled.
Previously, the RCW language was written such that the overall MFTE exemption must be canceled if
a single owner in a multi-owner project was non-compliant.

Another area of concern addressed by HB 1494 is that MFTE-eligible cities are allowed to create
MFTE target areas independently and introduce program policies without providing notice to other
taxing districts without any recourse. The expansion of eligibility and changes to MFTE program
regulations make financial and strategic planning difficult for smaller taxing districts. In an effort to
better inform taxing districts and help them anticipate impacts on their ability to collect tax revenues,
cities must now provide notice to affected taxing districts when adding new residential target areas or
changing MFTE program requirements.

The impact of introducing MFTE developments to new areas is also a growing concern. The
establishment of new MFTE target areas can create a substantial impact on the composition and
existing neighborhoods. Introducing new development can generate unintended displacement
through the effects of higher land values, rising housing costs, and tax burden, especially for renters,
low-income households, and communities of color. Moving forward, when creating new residential
target areas under MFTE programs, cities are now required to determine if newly proposed
Residential Target Areas are compliant with anti-displacement requirements in RCW 36.70A.070(2)
and establish clear policies to keep current residents and communities in their homes.

Another displacement issue addressed under HB 1494 is for apartment lease renewal for tenants in
income-restricted units. If a household receives an increase in income during their lease, they may
become ineligible and may be subject to immediate displacement at the end of the lease. HB 1494
now allows tenants in MFTE income-restricted units to remain eligible to stay in the unit even if their
annual income grows. The MFTE household will now remain eligible until their adjusted household
income exceeds 150% of the area median income provided they remain in the unit. If an MFTE tenant
voluntarily leaves with an income above 80% area median income, then they become ineligible for
MFTE units.


Advantage(s)

    1. Brings the City MFTE code into compliance with mandated legislative changes.
    2. Increases lands eligible for MFTE projects, potentially creating more income restricted housing
       units.
    3. Supports development along high-capacity transit corridors.
    4. Provides updated flexibility to address some longstanding program issues.


Challenge(s)

    1. The mandatory affordability requirement in defined station areas will limit new development in
       those areas due to the mismatch in the cost of providing the affordable units for 50 years and
       the length of time and amount of the financial incentives. Local developers will likely avoid
       parcels in station areas until the incentives are improved.
    2. The mandatory 50% reduction in traffic impact fees reduces access to capital project funding
       for City infrastructure surrounding transit nodes.


                                                                                               Page 51 of 126
    3. Removes the “new construction” tax base increase value for 20 years.
    4. Affordability monitoring will be required for 50 years.
    5. Removes City Council review and approval process.


Budget Impact
The new MFTE program option will defer new residential structural property tax collections for 20
years on any eligible project. As with the current MFTE program, the City will still receive its annual
allocated taxes from the County tax assessor but growth in the overall tax base will be constrained
without the new construction increase. In addition, there will be less local control over the potential
financial impacts of the TOD option projects because the statute requires station area exemptions to
be granted if affordability criteria are met. As highlighted above, the underfunded nature of the
affordability requirement is likely to result in few developments, if any, taking advantage of this option
so the impact to the local tax base could be minimal.

3.22.010         Purpose.

The purpose of this chapter is to encourage new private multi-housing development and
redevelopment within designated urban centers to accommodate future population growth,
provide places to live close to employment, shopping, entertainment, and transit services and
encourage affordable housing where appropriate. (Ord. M-4416 § 3, 2023; Ord. M-3987 § 1, 2011; Ord.
M-3314, 1997)


3.22.020         Definitions.

“Annual income” means gross annual income adjusted for household size and calculated based
on verifiable income documentation provided by the housing applicant/tenant.

”Station area” means all lots that are (a) Fully within an urban growth area; and fully or partially
within one-quarter mile walking distance of a stop on a fixed route bus system that is
designated as a bus rapid transit stop in the transit development plan as required in RCW
35.58.2795, for which an environmental determination has been issued as required under
chapter 43.21C RCW, and that features fixed transit assets that indicate permanent, high
capacity service including, but not limited to, elevated platforms or enhanced stations, off-
board fare collection, dedicated lanes, busways, or transit signal priority.

“Director” means the director of the city’s economic prosperity and housing department or
authorized designee.

“Growth Management Act” means Chapter 36.70A RCW.

“Household” means a single person, family or unrelated persons living together.

“Income-based housing” means residential housing that is rented by a person or household
whose monthly housing costs, including utilities other than telephone and any mandatory
recurring fees required as a condition of tenancy for the unit, do not exceed 30 percent of the
household’s monthly income. For the purposes of homeownership, the income limits are listed
in VMC 3.22.040(D)(9), Options Under the Program.

“Market-rate exemption option” means an eight-year property tax exemption with no income or
rent requirements.

“Median family income” means the median income calculated annually by the United States
Department of Housing and Urban Development for the Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro
metropolitan statistical area. Median family income is adjusted based on household size.

“Multifamily housing” means building(s) having four or more dwelling units designed for
permanent residential occupancy resulting from new construction or rehabilitation or
conversion of vacant, underutilized, or substandard buildings.

“Owner” means the property owner of record.

“Permanent residential occupancy” means multifamily housing that provides either rental or
owner occupancy for a period of at least one month. This excludes hotels and motels that
predominately offer rental accommodation on a daily or weekly basis.

“Primary residence” means a residence occupied for more than six months each calendar year.

“Rehabilitation improvements” means modifications to existing structures that are vacant for 12
months or longer, or modification to existing occupied structures which convert nonresidential
space to residential space and/or increase the number of multifamily housing units.

“Residential target area” means an area within an urban center that has been designated by the
city council as lacking sufficient, available, desirable, and convenient residential housing to
meet the needs of the public.

“Urban center” means a compact, identifiable district containing several business
establishments, adequate public facilities, and a mixture of uses and activities, where residents
may obtain a variety of products and services.

"Workforce housing" means rental housing with monthly costs that do not exceed 30 percent of
the monthly income of a household whose income is at or below 80 percent of the median
household income adjusted for household size, for the county where the household is located,
as reported by the United States department of housing and urban development.

3.22.030         Residential target area designation and standards.

A. Criteria. Following a public hearing, the city council may, in its sole discretion, designate one
or more residential target areas. Each designated target area must meet the following criteria,
as determined by the city council:

    1. The target area is located within an urban center;

    2. The target area lacks sufficient available, desirable, affordable, and convenient
    residential housing to meet the needs of the public who would likely live in the urban
    center if desirable, affordable, attractive, and livable places were available; and

    3. The providing of additional housing opportunities in the target area will assist in
    achieving the following purposes:

         a. Encourage increased residential opportunities within the target area, including
         income-based housing opportunities; or

         b. Stimulate the construction of new multifamily housing and/or the rehabilitation of
         existing vacant and underutilized buildings for multifamily housing; or

         c. Where appropriate, stimulate the construction, rehabilitation or conversion of
         existing vacant and underutilized multifamily rental units to owner-occupied
         multifamily housing as such property redevelops.

    4. In designating a residential target area, the city council may also consider other factors,
    including, but not limited to: whether additional housing in the target area will attract and
    maintain an increase in the number of permanent residents; whether an increased
    residential population will help alleviate detrimental conditions in the target area; and
    whether an increased residential population in the target area will help to achieve the
    planning goals mandated by the Growth Management Act under RCW 36.70A.020. The city
    council may, by ordinance, amend or rescind the designation of a residential target area at
    any time pursuant to the same procedure as set forth in this chapter for original
    designation.

    5. When designating a residential target area, the city council shall give notice of a hearing
    to be held on the matter and that notice shall be published once each week for two

3.22.040          Tax exemptions for multifamily housing in residential
                  target areas.

A. Intent. Limited exemptions from ad valorem property taxation for multifamily housing in
urban centers are intended to:

    1. Encourage increased residential opportunities within urban centers designated by the
    city council as residential target areas;

    2. Stimulate new construction or rehabilitation of existing vacant and underutilized
    buildings for multifamily housing in residential target areas to increase and improve
    housing opportunities;

    3. Assist in directing future population growth to designated urban center target areas
    and thereby reducing development pressure on single-family residential neighborhoods;

    4. Achieve development densities which are more conducive to transit use in designated
    urban centers;

    5. Encourage new construction or rehabilitation of owner-occupied multifamily housing
    where identified as desirable; and

    6. Encourage income-based housing opportunities.

B. Duration of Exemption. The value of residential improvements qualifying under this chapter
will be exempt from ad valorem property taxation for:



ORDINANCE - 17

    1. Eight successive years where all applicable criteria herein are met under subsection
    (D)(9)(a) of this section; or

    2. Twelve successive years if all applicable criteria herein are met under subsection
    (D)(9)(b) or (c) of this section. In all cases the duration of exemption shall be measured
    beginning January 1st of the year immediately following the calendar year after issuance of
    the final certificate of tax exemption.

C. Limits on Exemption. The exemption does not apply to the value of land or the value of
improvements not qualifying under this chapter, nor does the exemption apply to increases in
assessed valuation of land and nonqualifying improvements. In the case of rehabilitation of
existing buildings, the exemption does not include the value of improvements constructed prior
to submission of the completed application required under this chapter. The exemption does
not apply during the construction phase of the project prior to issuance of the final MFTE
certificate.

D. Project Eligibility. A proposed project must meet the following requirements for
consideration for a property tax exemption:

    1. Location. The project must be located within a residential target area, as designated in
    VMC 3.22.030.

    2. Application Date. A complete MFTE application must be received prior to receiving a
    construction occupancy permit.

    3. Tenant Displacement Prohibited. Property proposed to be rehabilitated must be vacant at
    least 12 months before submitting an application and fail to comply with one or more
    standards of the applicable state or local building or housing codes on or after July 23,
    1995.

    4. Size. The project must include at least four units of multifamily housing within a
    residential structure or as part of a mixed-use development. A minimum of four new units
    must be constructed or at least four additional multifamily units must be added to existing
    occupied multifamily housing. Existing multifamily housing that has been vacant for 12
    months or more does not have to provide additional units so long as the project provides
    at least four units of new, converted, or rehabilitated multifamily housing.

3.22.050         Tax exemptions for multifamily housing in transit-oriented
                 development areas.
A. Applicability. RCW 36.70A.840 requires a 20-year MFTE exemption option within transit-
   oriented development “station areas”, which are designated separately from the MFTE
   residential targeted areas defined in VMC 3.22.030. A map of the applicable station area
   locations is provided in VMC Figure 20.530.020-1. Station area boundaries include parcels
   fully or partially within one-quarter mile walking distance of a station area. In circumstances
   where interpretation of eligible parcels is required due to uncertainty, contradiction, or
   conflict on the station area boundary map, the director shall provide an interpretation of
   eligible station area parcels upon request.

B. Affordability. MFTE projects constructed within a station area must, by recorded covenant,
   agree to at least one of the following affordability and income eligibility options for fifty
   years:

   1. At least 10 percent of all units are restricted to households earning at or below to 60%
      AMI; or

   2. At least 10% of units are restricted to households earning at or below 80% AMI, where at
      least 10% of the restricted units are family sized units with more than two bedrooms; or

   3. At least 20% of units are restricted to households earning at or below 80% AMI.

C. Development Standards. In addition to the station area development standards detailed in
   VMC 20.530.040, 20-year option MFTE developments also shall meet the following
   requirements:


  1. Traffic Impact fee. A 50 percent reduction of transportation impact fees shall be
      provided for projects receiving a 20-year multiple-unit housing property tax exemption
      providing 50 years of affordability.

3.22.060          MFTE program procedures

A.   Application Procedure. A property owner who wishes to propose a project for a multifamily
     tax exemption shall complete the following procedures:

     1. File with the city the required application and the required fees. The initial application
          fee to the city shall consist of a base fee of $5,000 plus $75.00 per multifamily unit. If the
          city denies the application, the city will retain that portion of the fee attributable to its
          own administrative costs and refund the balance to the applicant.

     2.   A complete application shall include:

          a.    A completed city of Vancouver multifamily limited tax exemption application form;

          b.    A description of the project including the type of project and exemption requested;

          c.    Preliminary floor and site plans of the proposed project;

          d.    Detailed list of unit size, number of bedrooms and bathrooms and projected rent
                or sales price;

          e.    A detailed project budget, financing plan and operating projection;

          f.    A detailed description of green building construction practices proposed for the
                development;

          g.    A detailed advertising plan describing outreach methods for income-based
                tenants;

          h.    For rehabilitation projects, the applicant shall also submit an affidavit that existing
                dwelling units have been unoccupied for a period of 12 months prior to filing the
                application and shall secure from the city verification of property noncompliance
                with the city’s minimum housing code;

          i.    A statement acknowledging the potential tax liability when the project ceases to be
                eligible or is canceled under this chapter;

          j.    Verification by oath or affirmation of the accuracy of the submitted information;

   9. Post-Exemption Rent Restriction. At the expiration of the 12-year tax exemption period,

      return to market rate status.

             AN ORDINANCE amending the Vancouver Cultural Access Program.

                                        Staff Report: 198-26

To:       Mayor and City Council

Subject
Ordinance Relating to the Vancouver Cultural Access Sales Tax, Amending Vancouver Municipal
Code 3.45

Key Points

    • The Vancouver Cultural Access sales tax, now branded as Inspire Vancouver, was approved
      on December 2. 2024 by City Council.
    • This program provides grants to qualified non-profit organizations in Vancouver who are
      focused on arts, culture, heritage and science.
    • The Culture, Arts & Heritage Commission was identified as the “designated entity” to guide the
      program and act as the approver of grants.
    • To ensure integrity of the grant process, clarification in the Cultural Access ordinance is
      necessary.


Strategic Plan Alignment
Culture and Heritage – celebrating culture and heritage

High Performing Government – a government that is reliable, fiscally responsible, equitable, and
open to compromise

Present Situation
The Vancouver City Council approved an ordinance on December 2, 2024, to establish the
Vancouver Cultural Access sales tax in Vancouver Municipal Code (VMC) 3.45. State law allows
cities to impose a special one-tenth of one-cent sales tax that must be dedicated to supporting access
to non-profit organizations focused on arts, culture, heritage and science. The Culture, Arts &
Heritage Commission (CAHC) was identified as the “designated entity” to guide the program and act
as the approver of grants, while City staff serve in an administrative capacity.

The ordinance also called for a program policy to be developed to guide the specific management of
funds. In the last year plus, program staff have worked closely with the commission to build out the
overall program – including significant community engagement, developing criteria and an equitable
review process, and branding the program Inspire Vancouver. The program policy was adopted by
the City Council on September 15, 2025.

The City Council amended the policy on June 25, 2026, to temporarily adjust some of the policy
guidelines to allow for an expedited year one grant cycle. There has been substantial community
interest in this year-one program with over 100 grants received by the deadline of September 9,
2026.

                                                                                            Page 92 of 126
Working with the Commission, staff have developed a robust review process for this program. This
includes detailed grant review and scoring by a Grant Review Committee comprised of 8 community
members that was created by the Commission. The process envisions grant review being completed
in mid-October for consideration and approval by the CAHC.

Three currently serving CAHC commissioners have applied for grants under the program. To ensure
the program’s integrity, accountability and fairness, staff believes it is in the best interest of the City to
confirm commissioners may not receive grant funds or be directly or indirectly involved in a grant
application while serving as a commissioner.

Commissioners have been advised of this amendment. They can choose to either continue serving
on the CAHC, knowing that any non-profit organization in which they would directly or indirectly
benefit will not be eligible to receive a grant, or they may resign from the CAHC and any grant
applications in which they would directly or indirectly benefit will remain in theapplicant pool.

Advantage(s)

    1. This amendment demonstrates transparency and integrity in the review and approval process
       for Inspire Vancouver grants.
    2. Grant awards will be issued on or close to the schedule communicated to applicants.
    3. Provides greater clarity to Culture, Arts & Heritage Commissioners on how they can engage in
       their personal capacity as it relates to the grant program.


Challenge(s)
The potential for a commissioner's resignation may result in the need to appoint new commissioners
to ensure the year-one grant cycle moves ahead on the intended schedule.

Budget Impact
There is no budget impact. Funds for Inspire Vancouver year one grants have been appropriated in
prior budget action.

AN ORDINANCE relating to Comprehensive Plan and Zoning for the City of Vancouver and the
   Vancouver Municipal Code (VMC) Title 20 in matter related to statutory requirements to
   facilitate housing near transit; amending VMC 20.450.030 and 20.530.040; providing for
                        severability; and establishing an effective date.

                                       Staff Report: 195-26

To:       Mayor and City Council
From:     City Manager
Date:     October 12, 2026

Subject
Ordinance Amending Vancouver Municipal Code Title 20 pursuant to House Bill 1491

Key Points

    • House Bill (HB) 1491 includes a range of requirements and incentives intended to facilitate
      affordable housing near transit, including requirements to adopt minimum housing affordability
      thresholds and reduce transportation impact fees near transit.
    • HB 1491 establishes a state model ordinance which effectively imposes its requirements and
      incentives if cities do not adopt local implementing standards.
    • The Planning Commission voted 4-0 to adopt Title 20 changes regarding minimum affordability
      thresholds and transportation impact fees at a September 8, 2026, public hearing with no
      comments received.


Strategic Plan Alignment
Housing and Human Needs – meeting basic needs and partnering with organizations to support the
community

Present Situation
HB 1491 applies in Vancouver’s case to properties within a ¼ mile walking distance of all 51 existing
Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) stations. Within these areas, exemptions from the affordability requirements
are allowed for properties that had previously been zoned to allow Floor Area Ratios (FARs) that
meet or exceed the new HB 1491 FAR requirements of 2.5. Cities may also exempt an additional
25% of otherwise non-exempt properties within each station area if they allow for an additional 0.5
FAR average across station areas. As a result, staff proposes that downtown Vancouver properties
be exempted, along with 25% of properties near other BRT stations. Non-exempt properties subject
to HB 1491 must include one of the following, to be retained for 50 years and enforced by deed or
covenant:

    • At least 10% of the housing units must be affordable for households earning 60% of Area
      Median Income (AMI) for 50 years, or
    • At least 20% of the units affordable at 80% AMI for 50 years, or
    • At least 10% of the units affordable at 80% AMI for 50 years, provided at least 10% of the units
      have two or more bedrooms




                                                                                           Page 109 of 126
Development meeting one of the above may reduce transportation impact fees by 50%.

The proposed changes updating affordability requirements and providing for the impact fee reduction
represent the final components of City compliance with HB 1491. Documentation of compliance with
the other components of 1491 was included in 2026-2045 Our Vancouver Comprehensive Plan,
Appendix V, HB 1491 Compliance Memo.


Vancouver’s deadline for compliance was June 30, 2026, six months after the date of its required
Comprehensive Plan update adoption. Until the City adopts its own standards, it is subject to the
state pre-emptive model code which implements HB 1491 and was adopted by the Department of
Commerce this past June. No local development proposals or inquiries have been submitted in the
impacted properties since that time. No other Washington city has yet been subject to HB 1491,
which applies after a jurisdiction’s first periodic update following the bill’s adoption in 2025. Puget
Sound cities must comply in 2029, and the City of Spokane must comply in 2027.

The City of Vancouver has lobbied for changes to HB 1491 and will continue to do so as the
affordability requirements constitute inclusionary zoning mandates which are not offset by the
incentives, many of which are already included in the newly adopted zoning code. The affordability
requirement will likely discourage development in the areas near transit where it is desired.


Advantage(s)
Adoption of the minimum affordability thresholds and related transportation impact fee reductions is
required by state law.

Challenge(s)
Adopts local standards which, although already effectively in place through the state’s pre-emptive
model ordinance, will likely disincentivize housing in areas where it is desired.

Budget Impact
No significant direct impacts are anticipated

Prior Council Review

     • The Council reviewed the full scope of HB 1491 requirements, including Multi-family Tax
       Exemption incentives at an August 10, 2026, workshop.
     • Council reviewed the item on the Consent Agenda on October 5, 2026.


Action Requested
On Monday, October 12, 2026, upon second reading and a public hearing, finalize and approve the
ordinance.
