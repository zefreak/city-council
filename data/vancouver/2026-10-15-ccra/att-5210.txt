EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Project Overview
This following report identifies strategies and recommendations for the City of Vancouver to
support an economically diverse and vibrant Downtown neighborhood that balances ground
floor active uses, economic development goals, and essential services for residents and
employees. Both the City of Vancouver and the Downtown area have experienced significant
growth in the last 15 years, with the Downtown population and employment base increasing
significantly since 2020. In addition, the City plans for continued growth and densification
within Downtown through the recently adopted Comprehensive Plan. With this growth, the
demand for new jobs and services in Downtown will continue to grow. To help the City
maintain active storefronts, while also incorporating living wage jobs and essential services
downtown, this report analyzes national and local ground floor trends and identifies case
studies to determine practices that support ground floor activation. The report also identifies
growing industries within the region that support living wages, and specifically highlights
those industries that fit within downtown. Based on these industries, the report then
summarizes typical space requirements to inform the City on mechanisms to support these
target industries within Downtown. Lastly, this report identifies specific actions and strategies
the City can take to support active uses, meet economic development goals, and provide
services within Downtown.

Downtown Market Conditions
As noted above, Downtown Vancouver experienced significant growth over the last decade,
including the large-scale development of the Vancouver Waterfront. Along with the
Vancouver Waterfront, various multifamily and mixed-use developments delivered that also
increased the downtown population and employment base. In terms of retail space, Main
Street serves as the primary concentration of retail and restaurant space Downtown. Areas
outside of Main Street primarily consist of residential and office space, which support
downtown retailers. While the retail market in Downtown appears fairly healthy, with modest
rental rates and limited vacancies, the shifting demand away from traditional brick-and-
mortar retail space will continue to impact the success of Downtown businesses.

Many cities across the country are facing similar challenges in traditional retail nodes and
corridors, driven by decreasing foot traffic and declining spending at traditional retail
businesses. According to Placer.ai, downtown visitation across the nation is still below pre-
pandemic levels and the increasing presence of online shopping has further exacerbated
retail corridor vacancies. To revitalize key retail corridors, cities across the country have
implemented strategies to maintain downtown vibrancy, including concentrating retail to
active ground floor corridors, increasing flexibility and allowed uses in ground floor spaces,
partnering with private entities to create innovative ground floor spaces and occupants, and
assisting the local businesses through grants, loans, and business support. These strategies

Downtown Vancouver Land Use Requirements
Recently, the City adopted the Comprehensive Plan and the Downtown Design Guidelines.
The Downtown Design Guidelines shape the ground floor environment by encouraging
developers to consider ground floor space ceiling heights, transparency, and overall
storefront character. While these guidelines add to the character of Downtown and ground
floor activation, this report recommends potential flexibility with the guidelines and potentially
exempting target industries from certain design guidelines to support more diversity of jobs
within Downtown.

The City Comprehensive Plan implements many zoning overlays including the Active Ground
Floor Required and Active Ground Floor Ready overlays. Both of these overlays focus on
concentrating active ground floor uses to specific nodes and corridors in Downtown. This
report identifies the types of businesses that fit into the overlays and also identifies industries
that may require variances from the Active Ground Floor overlays in order to locate in
Downtown. For example, office and retail uses are most likely to fit into the Active Ground
Floor Required overlay due to their space preferences aligning with the overlay requirements.
With City support or assistance, other uses like childcare, public services, art galleries,
museums, and recreational uses could be included in the Active Ground Floor Required
overlay to provide a mix of business types along the corridor.

The Active Ground Floor Ready overlay is meant to allow for flexibility with ground floor uses
so that these spaces can convert to active uses in the future. Light food and beverage
manufacturing, advanced manufacturing, ground floor residential, and education uses are
industries that align with the areas included in the Active Ground Floor Ready overlay. While
giving future development in these areas flexibility with ground floor design and uses, this
report identifies upfront investments needed to maximize long-term flexibility of these
spaces. More specifically, this report identifies a tiered system that outlines the upfront
design and investments necessary to increase the flexibility when converting between
residential and commercial uses. Using this tier system, the City can identify areas
throughout Downtown to support higher design and infrastructure investments that align with
needs of specific industries.

Strategies and Recommendations
The report concludes with a set of specific strategies and actions the City can take to support
the target industries. The recommendations are broken down into five categories, including

continued vibrancy while also meeting economic development goals in Downtown:

   trends and how those businesses can fit in Downtown. Similarly, contacting real estate
   brokers to highlight Downtown Vancouver as an attractive area for businesses looking for
   space throughout the region will further promote business attraction to Downtown, while
   also helping City staff understand what businesses are actively interested in Downtown
   Vancouver and how the City can help support those business types. Lastly, reaching out
   to downtown property owners helps the City understand the unique needs and future

Leverage Publicly-Owned Sites for Development: The City owns various parcels
throughout Downtown that can be used to support new development for industry targets.
The City can support the feasibility of development by offering the land at discounted

Public Private Partnerships: Another near-term strategy the City can implement is

INTRODUCTION
The City of Vancouver contracted with BAE Urban Economics (“BAE”) to understand the
opportunities, constraints, and potential strategies to support continued active ground floor
uses while also expanding access to living wage jobs in Downtown Vancouver. Building upon
the Downtown Redevelopment Assessment conducted by BAE, which identified a significant
inventory of underutilized land with future redevelopment potential in Downtown Vancouver,
this analysis seeks to identify actions the City can take to strengthen existing businesses and
support future development in the Downtown area by creating active ground floor nodes and
bringing higher wage jobs to Downtown. The goal of this study is to support the City in the
following areas:
     • Increase the access to living wage jobs in Downtown
     • Support small and local businesses in Downtown
     • Activate the streets of Downtown
     • Create an economically sustainable and resilient Downtown
     • Increase the access to essential services for Downtown Residents

BAE, in coordination with First Forty Feet (“FFF”) and Bricks Need Mortar (“BNM”), grounds the
critical findings in Vancouver’s market, while also incorporating other studies and actions
that influence ground floor active uses in Downtown, including the Comprehensive Plan and
Downtown Design Guidelines.

The following report analyzes and recommends ground floor activation strategies within
Downtown Vancouver. This report begins with a summary of local and national market
conditions, particularly with ground floor retail spaces. These market conditions discuss the
economic and real estate market trends in Downtown Vancouver, the City of Vancouver, and
the metro region. The team then reviewed national trends and identified actions taken by
similar cities to support active ground floor uses. Building on the case studies, BAE conducted
an industry analysis to identify growing industries and industries that offer high wages in the
Vancouver region that present opportunities to locate in Downtown Vancouver, with a focus
on potential ground floor users. This report concludes with strategies and recommendations
for City policy and actions to support continued ground floor activation and help attract
target industries to Downtown Vancouver.

NATIONAL GROUND FLOOR TRENDS
According to Placer AI, visits to retail corridors across the Country are 11.7 percent below pre-
pandemic levels. Lower foot traffic in downtowns across the United States has led to
challenges in maintaining vibrancy and supporting the success of downtown businesses.
Some cities have started requiring businesses to bring employees back in the office, which
has increased foot traffic, but most visitation to downtown areas is still concentrated to
weekends and other special events. In addition, the continued prominence of online
shopping has put pressure on local retailers, leading to changes in demand for retail space
and impacts on the vibrancy of core retail nodes. In an attempt to create active downtowns,
cities often require ground floor retail, even in areas with limited demand for retail. Often
these ground floor requirements create feasibility challenges to developers, due to lower
rents and high turnover rates of ground floor tenants. These requirements can stall
residential development, which further exacerbates the lack of foot traffic and vibrancy in
downtown areas.

To address the high vacancies in downtown areas many cities have implemented retail
strategies to increase foot traffic and create vibrancy. BAE identified a range of comparable
cities that have implemented strategies to combat vacancies in retail corridors. Among
some of the strategies implemented by cities, discussed in more detail below, are adjusting
the geographic scope of ground floor retail requirements to focus on creating retail nodes,
broadening ground floor uses to include uses such as light industrial, and partnering with
public partners to identify public uses that can occupy ground floor spaces to maintain the
ground floor activation, expanding access to public services, and support project feasibility.
While many cities have implemented ground floor activation strategies, few have specifically
incorporated access to living wage jobs as a goal to create a more economically diverse and
inclusive downtown, as being envisioned by the City. The sections below group the key
findings of the case study research into themes to identify actions taken by similar cities to
support successful ground floor uses.


Concentration of Active Ground Floor
Concentrating active ground floor requirements into retail nodes creates vibrant
neighborhoods and limits the amount of challenging ground floor spaces in low foot traffic
areas. Many cities previously introduced blanket ground floor requirements to create vibrant
neighborhoods and have since rolled back those requirements to only specific retail-oriented
corridors. Milwaukee, Wisconsin through the 2040 Downtown Plan is creating downtown retail
districts for entertainment and active ground floor uses. Milwaukee is complementing this
concentration of retail by upgrading the pedestrian infrastructure within these nodes where
they expect increasing activation. The City of Spokane, WA implemented new mixed-use
zoning with different intensities of ground floor retail in their Centers and Corridors plan.
Many other jurisdictions such as Grand Rapids, Michigan and Champaigne, Illinois have



                         Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                              9
implemented similar strategies to focus ground floor active use requirements to key nodes
and corridors rather than having blanket requirements.


Land Use Changes
In response to the increasing amount of ground floor vacancies jurisdictions like Oakland,
California and Arlington County, Virginia have recently changed land use policy to expand
the options of potential tenants in ground floor spaces. For example, Oakland has included
artists and maker spaces as allowable uses in ground floors, and has also allowed these
establishments to manufacture and sell items within the same storefront. Arlington County,
Virginia increased the amount of uses allowed in their ground floors to increase the flexibility
and desirability for developers to build in the Columbia Pike corridor. Arlington County
included the following uses as acceptable uses in the ground floor: food and beverage
manufacturing, art manufacturing, museums, shared commercial kitchens, animal boarding,
and urban agriculture. The flexibility of potential uses has led to an increase in the number of
development projects in the area, leading to the desired growth of the Columbia Pike
corridor. In other cities looking to increase job growth, Chicago recently adopted land use
revisions to allow biotech labs in their ground floors, and Tucson, Arizona has expanded their
ground floor uses to include other job-generating uses in addition to retail and restaurants.


Public Partnerships
Partnering with public and non-profit organization to fill vacant spaces leads to improved
vibrancy, expanded access to services, and also increases the foot traffic to support
surrounding retail. As an example, Brooklyn, New York recently completed a 174-unit
affordable housing project with 10 ground-floor light industrial and manufacturing spaces.
The affordable housing was financially supported by national, state, and local tax credits,
while the developer and City of New York supported the development of the ground floor light
industrial spaces. Recently Tacoma, Washington prioritized partnerships with local
universities and hospitality groups to increase ground floor occupancy in their Economic
Development Strategy Plan. Tucson, Arizona has also prioritized education uses in ground
floor space, and have included other potential public and non-profit uses within their Infill
Incentive District. Included in partnering with public entities, cities like Denver and
Alburquerque have started filling empty land or ground floor spaces with temporary users, or
pop-ups. Pop-ups are typically local businesses that temporarily occupy a ground floor
space to test their success without the obligation of a full lease. Pairing pop-ups with local
events helps to foster local business growth and maintain active ground floor corridors.


City Assistance and Business Support
City assistance and business support programs help small businesses succeed by providing
business planning support, rent assistance, and grants. Less ground floor spaces become
vacant when small businesses are supported by the City, which ultimately creates active
storefronts. The City of Oakland created land trusts to reduce rents in ground floor spaces.
This reduction in rent directly supports artists and makers looking for affordable spaces. The



                         Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                              10
City of Cleveland focused on increasing street art by assisting artists and makers with
reduced rent in the North Collinwood area. Alburquerque and Denver used grant programs
to assist businesses with tenant improvement and moving expenses.


Other Strategies
While most strategies focus on policy changes, some cities have focused more on the built
environment and investing in pedestrian and multimodal infrastructure. A recent example of
this includes the City of Ogden, Utah, where the City is currently developing a downtown
parking garage off of the historic main street to expand and concentrate access to parking
that then includes a pedestrian-focused connection to the main street. Included in the Make
Ogden Plan, the City of Ogden is also looking to relocate their express rail train station to the
historic train station on the main street to bring more foot traffic from rail users to the
downtown area. These types of major infrastructure investments change corridors and how
people access retail nodes and downtown areas. However, infrastructure investments
typically have high costs and long timelines, meaning these types of strategies are more
long-term solutions than short-term strategies.

LOCAL LAND USE REQUIREMENTS
Along with this study, the City of Vancouver has recently adopted the Downtown Design
Guidelines and the City Comprehensive plan. Both projects play a major role in determining
the future environment of active ground floor spaces within Downtown. This section reviews
the regulations and implications of the Downtown Design Guidelines and Comprehensive
Plan Overlays on the future of Downtown Vancouver.


Downtown Design Guidelines Summary
The Vancouver Downtown Design Guidelines are the City’s primary design review tool for
projects within the Downtown Area. They are intended to be used together with the zoning
ordinance, Comprehensive Plan, and design review procedures. The guidelines focus on
compatibility with downtown’s historic character, high-quality materials, active ground floors,
pedestrian-oriented design, and a cohesive sense of place. The plan area includes several
downtown subareas, including Uptown Village, the Mill Plain Couplet, West-side Government,
Esther Short, and Central Downtown, including the Main Street Heritage Overlay District #2.

For active ground floors, the guidelines are organized by frontage type: retail/commercial,
office/professional services, live/work, residential, residential accessory spaces, and weather
protection. Retail-ready ground floors are expected to provide tenant flexibility through a
minimum 25-foot depth, frequent entries, potential restaurant venting, high transparency,
and taller ground-floor dimensions. Office ground floors require visibility, appropriate floor
height, privacy transitions, setbacks, and landscape buffering. Live/work units must provide
storefront character, while ground-floor residential is expected to contribute to the public
realm through stoops, balconies, raised entries, and privacy transitions.

The guidelines also include broader design requirements that affect active storefronts,
including facade modulation, avoidance of blank walls, pedestrian-scaled lighting, signage
integrated with the building, weather protection, utility and service area screening, and limits
on parking frontage. The Main Street Heritage Overlay District #2 adds a more specific
historic character framework, including a 25- to 30-foot column rhythm, vertical window
proportions, recessed windows, base-middle-top facade organization, and durable materials
such as brick, terracotta, ceramic tile, or masonry.


Comprehensive Plan / Overlay Summary
The City’s Comprehensive Plan provides the long-term policy framework for growth, land use,
housing, employment, public facilities, and livability. The City’s adopted Our Vancouver
update is paired with a zoning code update, which determines what can be built where. The

INDUSTRY ANALYSIS
To support the City’s economic development goals in Downtown, BAE conducted an industry
analysis of economic characteristics and trends in Downtown Vancouver, Clark County, and
Southwest Washington. This analysis seeks to identify industries that are most likely to
support living wage jobs in Downtown and explores the compatibility of industries that could
be located in Downtown Vancouver. Using this information, the consultant team compiled an
industry matrix that summarizes the historical and projected growth of industries in the area,
education and wage requirements, space requirements, current land use compatibility in
Downtown, and recommended City assistance for each industry. Each section of the report
will add relevant information to the matrix that will ultimately inform the City of the types of
target industries and tenant types that present opportunities for Downtown growth.


Methodology
BAE analyzed historical and projected employment by industry data, at the two-and three-
digit NAICs level, from the State of Washington Employment Security Department for Clark
County, Southwest Washington, and the State of Washington. BAE also analyzed business
data for Downtown which included NAICs categories. BAE analyzed industries that
experienced growth between 2014 to 2024 in Clark County, and the current concentration of
jobs by industries located in Downtown to understand current economic drivers. To
understand future growth, BAE identified the industries projected to grow in Southwest
Washington through 2033. Building upon the analysis of industry growth and concentration,
BAE selected two-digit NAICs code industries to create an opportunity matrix for Downtown.
BAE used the following criteria to select industries that represent opportunities for growth in
Downtown to be analyzed in the target industry matrix, including:
     • Industries with historical growth in Clark County
     • Industries with projected growth in Clark County
     • Industries with concentration in Downtown
     • Industries with high average wages

While some industries met all the criteria, BAE also included two-digit NAICs industries that
partially met the criteria. To further understand specific industries that could fit in Downtown,
BAE also reviewed more detailed three-digit NAICS industries, using the same criteria as the
two-digit NAICs industries, to include in the opportunities matrix.

industry grew the most, which is typical among growing geographies and reflects growth in

CASE STUDY PROJECTS
In addition to the analysis of national trends and case studies, BAE also researched specific
ground floor examples for uses that fit within the industries selected in the opportunity matrix.
BAE reviewed ground floor use types such as light industrial, retail, office, and specialty uses
like education, urgent care, public administration, and daycares. While there are limited
examples of new and innovative uses in downtown areas, BAE also focused on non-typical
mixed-use projects outside of core downtowns but within urban areas. To understand how
these projects were delivered, BAE also researched project financing and the role of the city in
the development for each project example.


Light Industrial
Throughout the Country, various cities have encouraged mixed-use developments to include
ground floor light industrial space. The 805 Rockaway Ave project was recently completed in
Brooklyn and was the first ground floor industrial or manufacturing space mixed with a
mixed-use multifamily residential in New York. The Rockaway Ave project is supported by
multiple government entities and non-profit agencies. The developer is a non-profit
industrial development organization and financed the majority of the ground floor industrial
space with City support. The City of San Francisco has also supported manufacturing mixed
use developments like 100 Hooper Street and One De Haro. For these projects, a zoning
variance was approved to allow mixed-use office and light industrial projects in Production,
Distribution, and Repair zones. For both projects, a local non-profit advocacy organization, SF
Made, coordinated with the developer to advocate and manage the ground floor
manufacturing space. Since 2010, SF Made has advocated for local manufacturers and
coordinated efforts for leasing industrial space to local manufacturers, highlighting the
importance of local partnerships to support the expansion of light industrial and
manufacturing spaces in growing urban areas.

Denver created the River North District (“Rino”) to revitalize a historic industrial area of
Downtown Denver, this district allows the existing industrial uses mixed with other
complimentary businesses, including breweries, retail, and entertainment uses. While in
theory the combination of uses creates a more active corridor, the Rino district has struggled
with vacant ground floors due to rising rents, and a lack of nearby residents to support the
ground floor businesses. In efforts to increase business activity the City of Denver recently
created a grant program for businesses locating to the Rino District. Lastly, a former factory
in Poughkeepsie, NY was transformed into a mixed-use housing and artist studio space. This
project maintains an industrial character by reserving flex spaces for artists, while also
converting other portions of the building into housing.

Retail
While there are many examples of mixed-use retail buildings throughout the nation, BAE
focused on less typical mixed-use retail uses. For example, the tenant mix at the Goat Blocks
in Portland has a nursery, grocery store, restaurants, and an interactive art museum.
Furthermore, the strategic placement of the tenants at Goat Blocks, with the restaurants and
grocery store on the corner of the building allow for optimal pedestrian traffic and visibility.
The nursery and the museum are located mid-block, given they serve as more of a
destination rather than solely relying on foot traffic. Slabtown in Portland and the Works in
Atlanta are historic industrial areas that have been redeveloped to residential and retail uses.
Both projects have included some ground floor residential uses in spaces where demand for
commercial uses is limited, leading to a concentration of ground floor retail uses connected
by clear pedestrian infrastructure. Slabtown has also included medical office spaces and
other non-traditional retail uses to support a more broad tenant base. In Atlanta, the Works
has followed with national trends by including more entertainment and dining but limiting the
amount of traditional retail spaces in an effort to concentrate the vibrancy and shopping
opportunities into high foot traffic corridors. Other examples include Ponce City market,

CONSTRAINTS TO ACTIVE
STOREFRONTS IN DOWNTOWN
Market Conditions
Demand for retail in cities across the country declined during the COVID-19 pandemic and
many cities are still recovering from increased retail vacancies. A primary reason for the
decline in retail demand for many downtown areas was the lack of visitation from
surrounding residents and visitors. Downtown areas with a limited residential base struggled
to support ground floor retail, while Cities with aggressive ground floor requirements resulted
in an oversupply of retail space. Ultimately, downtown districts require smart planning and
programming, support from the City, and innovative approaches to expand the economic
base while maintaining activity and vibrancy within ground floor spaces.


Local Context
Vancouver's proximity to Oregon creates a structural disadvantage that shapes the retail
environment in ways few other Pacific Northwest cities face. For decades, Jantzen Beach on
Hayden Island served as a major regional retail destination, a place where Oregon's absence
of sales tax drew cross-river shoppers seeking meaningful savings on everyday consumer
goods to larger purchases. Though consumer traffic to Jantzen Beach has been declining for
some time, the underlying behavior it represented has not disappeared. Shoppers who once
drove to Jantzen Beach now shop online, where Oregon-registered retailers and national
platforms alike offer the same tax-free or tax-minimized advantage from the convenience of
home. The result is that Vancouver retailers competing in traditional retail categories face a
persistent, structural price disadvantage that no amount of local marketing can fully
overcome.

Coupled with the convenience of online shopping and delivery, few categories of ground floor
small businesses remain strong prospects. To compete in this environment, ground floor
concepts must offer something that cannot be replicated on a screen or purchased cheaper
across the river. Retailers must provide highly in-demand goods with immediate need,
deeply experiential offerings, multi-channel operations that use the physical location as one
part of a larger business model, or highly specialized products that serve a defined and loyal
customer base.


National Trends
Downtown Vancouver's challenges are not unique. Across the country, downtowns anchored
by traditional office inventory are struggling to sustain the ground floor commercial activity
that once defined them. Prior to the COVID pandemic, downtown storefronts were limited,
sought-after, and supported by the steady foot traffic of office workers, visitors, and residents




                         Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                              31
moving through dense urban cores. That finite supply justified high lease rates, and landlords
and lenders priced accordingly.

Those conditions no longer exist, but the pricing largely does. Office vacancy has reduced
downtown foot traffic significantly, yet commercial lease rates have been slow to adjust. This
is not simply a matter of landlord reluctance. Banks are resistant to reassessing the value of
commercial properties downward, because doing so would impair loan collateral and force
difficult write-downs. Municipalities face their own disincentive: lower assessed property
values translate directly into a reduced property tax base, creating fiscal pressure that cities
are understandably reluctant to absorb. The result is a structural constraint where brick-
and-mortar small businesses, particularly single-location operators with a single revenue
channel, are expected to pay yesterday's rents for today's foot traffic. For many, this is simply
not survivable.

Cities across the country have turned to events and programming as a strategy for
reanimating downtown streets. Events can generate energy and visibility, and in the right
circumstances they build habits that eventually benefit existing businesses. However, the
evidence increasingly shows that one-off, monthly, or even weekly events do not reliably
convert crowds into customers for established ground floor commercial tenants. Temporary
vendors and food carts, with low overhead, no lease obligations, and no sunk costs, are
typically the primary beneficiaries of event-driven foot traffic. Existing tenants may find that
an event outside their door brings crowds that browse but don't buy, or that street closures
and vendor competition actively impede their normal operations. Events are a tool worth
using, but they are not a substitute for the sustained, daily foot traffic that healthy downtowns
require.

That sustained foot traffic depends primarily on workers, and getting workers back downtown
is necessary but not sufficient. Unless Downtown can offer the wrap-around services that
working families rely on: childcare, after-school care, petcare, gyms, and everyday personal
services, workers will leave as soon as the workday ends, or sooner. Affordable options for
these services are abundant outside the Downtown core, where rents are lower and supply is
higher. Without competitive offerings closer to where people work, Downtown will continue to
function as a place people pass through rather than a place they stay.

Local Business Perspective
Against this backdrop, the typical downtown small business faces a structural mismatch
between its cost model and the commercial environment it now operates in. Most small,
independent ground floor businesses were built around a single location, a single revenue
channel, and a customer base sustained by foot traffic levels that no longer exist. Key
overhead costs, namely rent and payroll, are increasingly less manageable in post-
pandemic downtowns.




                         Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                              32
This mismatch is particularly acute for businesses that lack the capital, capacity, or
operational flexibility to adapt quickly. Adding revenue channels requires investment.
Rightsizing operations by adjusting hours, reducing payroll, and/or renegotiating space
carries its own costs and risks. Relocating back-office or production functions to lower-cost
locations outside the Downtown core may make financial sense but requires planning,
resources, and technical assistance that many small operators do not have on hand. The
challenge facing Downtown Vancouver's small businesses is a structural problem that will
require structural responses.


Land Use Challenges
Downtown Design Guidelines
The Downtown Design Guidelines establish a strong foundation for active ground floors by
emphasizing pedestrian-oriented frontages, transparency, weather protection, high-quality
materials, human-scaled design, and the careful placement of service, utility, parking, and
loading areas. The guidelines also recognize that active ground floors may include not only
retail and restaurants, but also office, live/work, residential accessory spaces, and ground-
floor residential when designed to contribute to the pedestrian realm. However, the current
typology-based structure can create constraints for long-term adaptability because each
frontage type has different physical assumptions. Retail-ready ground floors are expected to
provide greater depth, transparency, frequent entries, venting potential, and taller floor-to-
floor heights, while office, live/work, and residential ground floors have different height,
setback, privacy, and entry conditions. These differences may support good initial design
outcomes, but they can make future conversion more difficult if ground-floor residential is
not designed from the beginning with sufficient height, depth, access, transparency, and
utility flexibility.


Comprehensive Plan Land Use Overlays
Land use overlays may create similar constraints if active ground floor requirements are
applied too broadly or without a clear distinction between areas where active uses should be
required at initial occupancy and areas where future conversion potential should be
preserved. A tiered Active Ground Floor Ready approach can help resolve these issues by
clarifying which shell-building decisions must be made at initial permit, while allowing final
tenant improvements to occur later as market demand emerges. The Main Street Heritage
Overlay reinforces historic rhythm, vertical proportions, durable materials, base-middle-top
composition, and traditional storefront patterns, which are important to downtown character
but may require careful coordination with modern tenant needs, larger openings, mechanical
ventilation, signage, accessibility, and flexible interior layouts.

INDUSTRY TARGETS AND SPACE NEEDS
Using the prior analysis that identified target industries, the consultant team further analyzed
the target industry’s space requirements and overall fit in Downtown by creating the matrix
shown in Table 10 below. The purpose of the space needs matrix is to recommend industries
that can easily fit into the existing downtown environment, and industries that may need
additional support from the City to locate in Downtown. The space needs matrix outlines the
specific space requirements that the target industries typically need when moving in to a
new space like total square footage, floor-to-floor heights, parking requirements, curb
management, visibility, utility needs, and tenant improvements. To summarize the overall fit
in Downtown for each industry, the compatibility of each target industry within downtown was
scored from low to high, with “high” symbolizing a good fit for the existing built environment in
Downtown.


 HOW TO USE THE SPACE NEEDS MATRIX:
  • Identify tenants for vacant spaces in Downtown. The City can support private
    businesses relocating to privately owned spaces or coordinate with tenants to
    relocate into a City owned space.

  • Pinpoint target industries and tenants for new public development or public-private
    partnership project. Through acquisition and development of land in Downtown, the
    City can find industries that meet economic development goals and build space
    based on the target industry’s space requirements.

  • Determine land use changes, exemptions and infrastructure upgrades for target
    industries that meet economic development goals.


Square Foot Range
Downtown Vancouver has a wide range of users from industrial to ground floor retail and
multifamily residential. The square foot range row allows the City to identify industries with
square foot needs that fit in vacant spaces or publicly-owned ground floor spaces. For
example, if a 10,000 square foot space becomes vacant in Downtown, the City can search for
target industries like manufacturing, health care, and education users that may fit the square
foot requirements of the 10,000 square foot building.


Floor-to-Floor Heights
Floor-to-floor heights are particularly important for industrial users that require loading docks
or garages for truck use. Floor-to-floor heights inform the City on the requirement by industry
to identify potential investments that can be made to encourage the industrial target
industries to relocate or expand into Downtown. For example, food and beverage
manufacturing requires a higher ceiling height to accommodate machinery and deliveries.
This may limit the ability for food and beverage manufacturers to locate in existing



                         Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                              35
warehouses in Downtown unless new development specific to industries requiring higher
floor-to-floor heights is provided.


Parking Requirements
The parking requirements row describes the typical parking needs of the target industries. As
downtown focuses on densification, the amount of parking will become a consideration in
supporting business and residential growth. The city could focus on target industries that
require lower parking requirements in certain areas, while also accommodating industries
with higher parking requirements by coordinating shared parking agreements, allowing
exemptions for target industries that help support economic development goals, or creating
a transit incentive plan for employees and visitors of target industries to use public
transportation. Certain uses, like education, require a significant amount of parking, but
alleviating this with public transportation and shared parking agreements can lower the
parking requirements and help attract certain high-parking industries to Downtown.


Curb Management
Curb management is the management of sidewalks, parking, loading and unloading for
street facing businesses. The matrix ranks the target industries curb management level from
low to high. Industries with high curb management, like beverage and food manufacturing,
require space to be available at the curb for frequent truck deliveries, loading, and unloading.
Businesses with high curb management needs often prefer an alleyway or a side street for
loading and unloading. Utilizing the curb management row can help the City concentrate
industries with high curb management needs to low pedestrian areas and help plan for
future infrastructure upgrades to attract target industries with loading requirements.


Visibility
Visibility for businesses is key to attracting customers and includes pedestrian visibility and
vehicle visibility. Pedestrian visibility focuses on pedestrian walkability and appeal from the
sidewalk, while vehicle visibility focuses more on signage and direction-based visibility. In the
matrix below, light industrial users require lower pedestrian visibility due to small amount of
pedestrian visitors, and high vehicle visibility for truck deliveries and employee or customer
visitation. Retail and specialty uses require high levels of pedestrian visibility and still require
a moderate level of vehicle visibility to attract customers. The City can utilize the matrix
below to concentrate target industries to areas with specific visibility requirements and
support visibility with infrastructure upgrades like the Main Street Promise Project.


Utilities and Tenant Improvements
The utilities and tenant improvements row describes power requirements, and specific tenant
improvements necessary to support each target industries. Light industrial users have higher
requirements for power and utility capacity, while food service users require ventilation and
grease traps. The City can use this information to inform several actions. First, the City can
coordinate tenanting efforts for vacant spaces that already have existing tenant upgrades



                          Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                               36
that align with that target industry. Secondly, the City can identify vacant spaces that require
certain upgrades and tenant improvements with an effort to make those investments in
order to attract the target users. For example, if a vacant ground floor space along a key
corridor becomes available and food service users are looking to move in, the City can
provide financial assistance to upgrade the space for ventilation, grease traps, and other
needs.


Fit in Downtown
The “Fit in Downtown” section gathers the information from the above sections and assigns a
level of fit for the industry within the Downtown environment. The uses that are labeled “high”
are the uses that easily fit in with the built environment and are typically found in downtown
areas. As seen below, these uses are retail and restaurant, specialty, and residential uses.
The light industrial uses range from high level of fit to a moderate level of fit in Downtown. For
industries with a moderate or low level of fit, the City should take specific action to create an
attractive and welcoming environment that addresses key needs of the industry.


Downtown Subarea
The Downtown subarea row describes which subarea the industry would most likely fit into
based on its space requirements and the current conditions within Downtown subareas. For
example, the light industrial uses will most likely locate in the civic center or west industrial
subareas, while the retail and office industries fit into the east side of Downtown in the Esther
Short Park, Main Street, and Uptown subareas.

The active ground floor overlays matrix considers the land use controls included in the
Comprehensive Plan Update and their relationship with the target industries. The matrix
includes the target industries compatibility with mixed-uses, the Active Ground Floor Required
Overlay, the Active Ground Floor Ready Overlay, and a classification system based on

 HOW TO USE THE ACTIVE GROUND FLOOR OVERLAYS MATRIX
  • Determine what industries are likely to fit into the Active Ground Floor Required and
    Ready Overlays.

  • Help create use diversity in downtown by supporting tenant improvement and
    infrastructure upgrades for industries that may not be typical Downtown tenants.

  • Identify infrastructure upgrades for existing spaces and new developments



Mixed-Use Compatibility
The mixed-use compatibility row can help the City select industries other than the typical
retail and office uses and target them in existing vacant ground floor spaces or new public
development projects. While light industrial users may not be the most compatible for
mixed-uses, the City can help support these industries by concentrating these uses to areas
in Downtown with more limited mixed-use developments or work to ensure specific design
elements within a mixed-use building are met to balance the success of each building
occupant.


Target for Active Ground Floor Required
Within the target industries, there are uses that are compatible with the Active Ground Floor
Required Overlay like office, retail, and a few specialty uses like childcare. The light industrial
uses, public administration, and residential uses do not present a great fit with the Active
Ground Floor Required Overlay due to the nature of their operations, lack of actual ground
floor activity, and the lack of cohesion with the high-pedestrian traffic areas.


Target for Active Ground Floor Ready
While all of the target industries are allowed as uses within the Active Ground Floor Ready
Overlay, some may be less successful under current market conditions. For example, the
Ground Floor Ready Overlay areas are typically representative of areas with lower foot traffic
and visibility under current conditions. Uses like light industrial, museums, public


                          Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                               39
administration, and offices will be more successful in the Ready overlay as they are not reliant
on foot traffic and the overall conditions in the more pedestrian and retail-oriented corridors.
This section of the matrix can help the City determine which uses can easily fit into the
overlay area and which industries will need extra support and retrofitting in the existing
building inventory. To support economic development goals, the City can identify vacant
spaces in the overlay area and introduce target industries to those spaces by supporting
tenant improvements and infrastructure upgrades. While this section applies to the existing

discusses how new development can be flexible to allow a range of near-term and long-ter

Design and Infrastructure Tier Level for Future Conversion
First Forty Feet evaluated the application of land use controls by comparing Downtown
Vancouver’s policy and design framework with the physical, code, and infrastructure
conditions needed to support active ground floor uses over time. This included reviewing the
Downtown Design Guidelines, proposed land use overlays, market findings, industry targets,
case study precedents, and the shell-building decisions that affect future conversion from
ground-floor residential to commercial, civic, service, or employment-generating uses.

The recommended approach is to avoid applying a single active ground floor standard
across all downtown frontages. Instead, land use controls should be calibrated by frontage
priority, market demand, public realm value, and the likely intensity of future use. Priority
pedestrian streets and nodes should be considered for Active Ground Floor Required controls,
where active uses are expected at initial occupancy. Other strategic frontages should be
regulated through an Active Ground Floor Ready framework, where ground-floor residential
may be allowed initially but the building shell must preserve a realistic path to future active
use.

The Active Ground Floor Ready framework could be organized around three tiers of increasing
conversion complexity.

   Tier 1 represents the easiest conversion pathway from residential to small-scale active
   use, such as small retail, gallery, studio, personal service, or small office uses with limited
   infrastructure demands.
    Tier 2 represents conversion to more typical commercial and active main street uses,
   including larger retail, office and professional services, restaurants and cafes, childcare,

CITY ACTIONS AND STRATEGIES
The analysis above identified potential business types that will support continued ground
floor active uses while also incorporating the City’s economic development goals to create a
more economically diverse Downtown. During this analysis, the consultant team identified
potential challenges to supporting the identified industries. To help address those
challenges, the following section summarizes key actions and strategies the City of
Vancouver can take to support existing and future businesses in Downtown. The section first
summarizes all of the potential actions and strategies, and then concludes by identifying the
actions that are most helpful for each industry identifies in the target industry matrix.


City Actions and Strategies
The following section identifies specific strategies and recommendations the City can take to
support ground floor activation and economic development goals in Downtown Vancouver.
The recommendations are broken down into four categories, including (1) Land Use Policies,
(2) City Process, (3) Financial Incentives, and (4) Direct and Indirect City Investment. For each
recommendation, this section summarizes the general strategy, identifies the key City
department or external partner to implement the strategy, and then summarizes the
application of that strategy specifically within Downtown Vancouver. Following the actions
and strategies in the four categories listed above, this section also identifies methods for
funding and raising financial resources to support these efforts. Table 12 at the end of this
section summarizes each recommendation and ranks the potential impact on existing
downtown businesses, ground floor activation, economic development goals, as well as the
policy implementation time horizon and level of required City effort.


Land Use Policies
The City of Vancouver has land use authority over sites throughout the City. Through the
ongoing Comprehensive Planning process, the City can take steps to support the industries
identified above by increasing the flexibility of ground floor spaces and addressing any major
land use impediments that disincentivize business location and investment in Downtown.

This section highlights several policies that will be addressed as part of the ongoing
Comprehensive Plan Update. Currently, the land use constraints within Downtown are fairly
limited, with only minor requirements on future development and ground floor spaces.
However, this section aims to highlight the importance of each land use policy to ensure the
City understands how each factor into the activation of ground floor spaces and industry
growth within Downtown.

Allowing diverse uses and limiting strict requirements for ground floor uses will help decrease

corridors while allowing flexibility outside of those nodes. This flexibility will better facilitate a
mix of uses, allow new redevelopment projects to deliver ground floor spaces that align with
market demand, and will allow the City to support uses that align with economic
development goals.

constraints on new projects by decreasing costs and timelines that will increase the inventory
of space in Downtown that can accommodate a range of users.

by having an approval process that is transparent and reduces complexity and costs for new
development projects. The City can also coordinate with external partners to further industry
growth and decrease Downtown ground floor vacancies.

Curb Utilization
Curb utilization is the management of vehicle parking, loading and unloading, and pedestrian
walkways. Uses with loading and unloading needs require limited vehicle parking in certain
areas to allow trucks to utilize the space. In corridors with heavy pedestrian traffic the curb
should be utilized as wide sidewalks with little loading and unloading zones for trucks.

Permit Streamlining

new development projects. To support the feasibility of new development or business
moving to Downtown, the City could consider streamlining permits for projects that meet
specific criteria. Similar to other efforts, this streamlining will benefit feasibility in multiple
ways, including reduced financing and holding costs, and it will also signal to the
development community that Vancouver is committed to supporting projects aligned with its
goals, which can attract investment to the City and even reduce required returns through a
perception of reduced project risk. It should be noted, however, that the City of Vancouver
already has a fairly efficient approval process relative to nearby jurisdictions. Maintaining

Methods of Funding for Public Investment
In order to provide direct financial incentives, the City needs to identify mechanisms to
increase local capital. There are various methods that the City can explore, all with varying
implications on Downtown businesses and broader tax-receiving entities throughout the City
and Downtown area.

Tax Increment Financing District
Recently approved by the Washington State legislature, local jurisdictions are now eligible to
set up tax increment financing (TIF) districts to fund public improvements. In order to
establish a TIF district, a city must find that:
     • Public improvements are expected to encourage private development that will
        increase assessed values in the area
     • The private development would not be reasonably expected without the public
        investments
     • The expected assessed value within the area would be less without the public
        investment
     • The projected future development is allowed under local zoning and development
        standards

Downtown Vancouver meets these conditions and serves as a valuable opportunity to create
a Downtown TIF district. Potential public improvements that could spur development include
open space, bike and pedestrian infrastructure, and internal roadway improvements for
larger redevelopment sites. To capitalize on future development, a TIF district is most
impactful when it is created around a large amount of redevelopment potential but prior to
the development efforts in order to keep the baseline assessed values relatively low. The City
could consider a full downtown TIF district, or more defined districts to fund specific
infrastructure investments. A separate study conducted by the City concluded that a
Downtown TIF district is challenging under existing State of Washington TIF legislation due to
tax exemption policies and the limitations on the scope of potential TIF investments. If State
laws and regulations change, a TIF district for Downtown could serve as an important tool to
support downtown development and infrastructure upgrades.

Increase Sales Tax in Downtown to Support Downtown Investment

Business Improvement Areas (BIAs) are entities that collect special assessments imposed on

support shared investment, management, services, and programs within the defined area.

Local Improvement Districts
Under Washington State Law, local governments can form Local Improvement Districts (LIDs)
to fund specific infrastructure projects that will lead to improved feasibility of development.
Similar to the TIF, the LID would require a specific geography centered around an
infrastructure investment like a park, transit node, or major utility upgrade. The LID would be
an additional assessment on existing property owners that will benefit from the infrastructure
investment. These funds could be used to fund projects as a “pay-as-you-go” model or by
bonding against future revenues.

Near-Term Recommendations and Strategies
Given the current challenges to development feasibility largely driven by factors outside of
the City’s control, the City should prioritize certain strategies in the near-term in order to
support ground floor activation, new development, and introduction of industry targets in
Downtown, while also investing in certain strategies to pave the way for future development.
These include:

Allow ground floor use flexibility: The City is actively adjusting ground floor use requirements
through the Comprehensive Plan and should continue to support these efforts. In addition to
adjusting the requirements through the Ground Floor Overlays, the City should consider
allowing further flexibility for target industries by allowing waivers and variances from the
Downtown Design Guidelines and Active Use Overlay requirements.

Conduct outreach to target industry businesses, representatives, and downtown property
owners: This study identifies a need to support living wage jobs in Downtown, especially in
industries that are not heavily concentrated in Downtown today. Through contacting industry
representatives and target industry businesses, the City can create an ongoing list of
potential businesses interested in Downtown, coordinate future infrastructure investments to
meet their needs, and understand growth trends and how those businesses can fit in
Downtown. Similarly, contacting real estate brokers to highlight Downtown Vancouver as an
attractive area for businesses looking for space throughout the region will further promote
business attraction to downtown, while also helping City staff understand what businesses
are actively interested in Downtown Vancouver and how the City can help support those
business types. Lastly, reaching out to downtown property owners helps the City understand
the unique needs and future plans that the property owner have for privately-owned sites
throughout Downtown. The City or City partners can act as a liaison between industry target
businesses and property owners to fill vacant spaces in Downtown.

Leverage Publicly-Owned Sites for Development: The City owns various parcels throughout
Downtown that can be used to support new development for industry targets. The City can
support the feasibility of development by offering the land at discounted rates, either through
a low-cost ground lease or fee simple sale. In addition, the City can collaborate with other
public agencies to identify surplus sites that could support new development for industry
targets and ground floor activation. These agencies primarily include Clark County and the
Vancouver Housing Authority. The City would not necessarily have to purchase these sites,
but rather provide resources and support for future development projects on these publicly-
owned sites.

Acquire Privately-Owned Sites and Buildings: The Downtown Redevelopment Assessment
identified a range of privately-owned sites that are currently vacant or underutilized. The City
can support redevelopment of these sites through strategic acquisition. Through purchasing
privately owned sites, the City can catalyze development for surrounding land owners and


                         Vancouver Active Ground Floor Study | July 2026
                                              68
incorporate industry target users to occupy the development on the purchased property. The
City should also seek to purchase sites that disrupt the ground floor active corridors to create
a cohesive active environment in Downtown while also being able to determine the tenant
that occupies the ground floor once the property is developed.
Public Private Partnerships Another near-term strategy the City can implement is continuing
to identify and participate in public-private partnership opportunities. In addition to entering
into development agreements for development on City-owned land, identified above, the
City can also consider other forms of partnerships. This could include locating City services in
the ground floor of private development, or purchasing the ground floor of future
developments to allow public ownership and management of Downtown spaces that can
then be used to support local businesses. In addition, the City could consider partnering with
local childcare providers to attract those uses to Downtown through subsidies or affordable
rent to enhance the available services for Downtown residents and employees. Lastly, the
City can partner with local organizations to fill vacant storefronts with local businesses.

Study Potential Revenue Generation Mechanisms: The City should review the potential
revenue generation tools to create funding mechanisms to support downtown investments
necessary to accommodate additional development and the inclusion of space designed to
meet the needs of the target industries.
