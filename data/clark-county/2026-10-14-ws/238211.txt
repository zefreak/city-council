Introduction
Parks and Nature Division

Parks and open spaces are essential community infrastructure, providing benefits that reach far
beyond recreation. They offer places for people to connect with nature, support physical and mental
well-being, and strengthen community identity. These spaces also play a critical environmental role
by protecting wildlife habitat, improving air and water quality, and helping manage stormwater. As
Clark County continues to grow, parks and open spaces ensure that residents maintain access to
nature, safe gathering places, and opportunities for outdoor activity—key ingredients for a healthy,
resilient, and vibrant community.

The Public Works Parks and Nature Division manages and maintains 97 properties across Clark
County, including regional parks, community parks, neighborhood parks, natural areas, and specialty
sites. These properties are distributed throughout both unincorporated Clark County and the
Vancouver Urban Undeveloped Area (VUUA).

Within the VUUA, parks and undeveloped properties are part of the Greater Clark Park District
(GCPD), a Metropolitan Park District established under Washington State Statute RCW 36.61. Since
the GCPD’s formation in 2005, Clark County has developed 28 neighborhood parks and 5
community parks within the district boundary.

State statute requires that each Metropolitan Park District adopt a Parks System Plan, Capital
Facilities Plan (CFP), and Capital Improvement Plan (CIP). Clark County’s Parks System Plan—known
as the Parks, Recreation, and Open Space (PROS) Plan—was originally developed in 2014–2015 and
updated in 2020–2021, with the revised plan adopted by the County Council in April 2022.

During the PROS Plan’s public outreach process, the community expressed a preference for Level of
Service (LOS) goals for each park classification: regional parks, community parks, neighborhood
parks, and urban natural areas. In the current adopted PROS Plan, LOS is measured as the number of
developed or undeveloped park acres per 1,000 residents, calculated separately for each park type.
Using this criteria for LOS, the current PROS Plan identifies deficiencies across all categories.
Elements of this plan seek to address those deficiencies through acquisition and development. While
community parks, neighborhood parks, and urban natural areas can be acquired and developed using
Park Impact Fee (PIF) funds, addressing deficiencies in regional parkland is more challenging because
the county currently lacks a dedicated funding source for regional acquisitions and development.

To address LOS gaps, Parks and Nature prepares a Capital Improvement Plan (CIP) that includes
funding for both property acquisition and development. The division updates the CIP annually
through a public process that concludes with a hearing before the Clark County Council and
adoption of the six-year CIP. This review and adoption typically occurs each October.

Lands Management Division
The Public Works, Lands Management Division manages thousands of acres of unique properties
and administers several programs including Legacy Lands and the Conservation Futures fund, the
former Camp Bonneville Military Reservation, the Chelatchie Prairie Railroad, the county’s Noxious

    CIP Development
    Recognizing the reality of funding and staffing limitations, available opportunities, and overall
    capacity for the parks system each fiscal year, the Parks and Nature Division utilizes a transparent
    and systematic methodology for the prioritization of capital expenses. This methodology balances
    the needs and objectives of the parks system, the obligations of the county to the public, the impact
    to the Parks and Nature Division and county budgets, and the operational impact of adding to the
    parks portfolio. The Parks and Nature Division aims to be agile, flexible, and adaptable in the
    implementation of a CIP to pursue opportunities that meet the broader goals of the division, county,
    and community as they arise.

    The Parks and Nature Division describes capital plan improvements in the following terms:
          Major Maintenance: Project renovating, restoring, or replacing an existing asset, usually with
           a contractor performing the work rather than park operations staff. Major maintenance
           projects may include (but are not limited to) shelter replacement, playground fall surfacing
           replacement, parking lot or trail resurfacing, restroom replacement, and sports turf
           replacement.
          Emergency Repair Project: Project addressing an emergency involving a Parks and Nature
           Division asset which creates risk of death, injury, significant loss or damage to property
           (public or private) or the environment. Emergency repair projects are limited in scope to
           mitigation of risk. It is contrary to this policy to use emergency situations to elevate capital
           projects.
          Park Enhancements: Projects that focus on capital improvements or additions to existing
           neighborhood, community and regional parks.
          New Park Development: Project creating a new park or park amenity that adds to existing
           park inventory. Projects may include construction of new or additional sport courts,
           playgrounds, shelters, restrooms or new parks in their entirety.
          Land Acquisition: Project acquiring new lands, rights-of-way or property rights to preserve
           land or enable future recreation improvements. Rights-of-way may be acquired to improve
           park access, and property rights can be acquired for habitat or agriculture preservation.




4
CIP Development Process
The CIP development process includes four stages: Identification, Prioritization, Scoping, & Budgeting



       Identification
       Identification of projects by:
           o   Aggregating asset condition assessments, public comments, development needs
               identified in the PROS Plan, prior obligations and staff observations. The county is in
               the process of implementing an electronic asset management system which will serve
               as the method for developing formal lists of items to address.
           o   Staff sorting each proposed project into one of the following categories, as described
               in the previous section: preventative maintenance, major maintenance, development,
               land acquisition.
           o   Adding projects and project details to the list by project type.
           o   Adding notes from plans, community input, government input, etc. to the project
               spreadsheet to assist with prioritization.

       Prioritization
       Prioritization of projects by:
           o   Performing an initial prioritization through the scoring of projects using the rubric
               applicable by project type, located in the appendices of this policy. Prioritization will
               be incorporated into a draft CIP.
           o   Parks and Nature Division staff presenting the draft CIP to the Parks Advisory Board
               (PAB) annually in the second quarter of the preceding year.
           o   Parks and Nature Division staff working with the Clark County Public Works Public
               Information Office to post the Draft CIP for public comment in May.
           o   The PAB reviewing and providing input on the draft CIP. Staff will review comments
               and adjust the CIP as necessary based on the input received. At a subsequent
               meeting the PAB shall consider making a recommendation for adoption of the CIP by
               the Clark County Council. The recommendation and any comments from the PAB will
               be distributed to county administration as part of the budgeting process.
       Note: Projects that are funded and currently in development will not be subject to
       reprioritization, ensuring continual progress on projects already in process. This is consistent
       with a fiscally constrained capital program, as described in the budgeting section of this
       policy.




                                                                                                           5
    Scoping
    Scoping of projects by:
       o   Developing proposals to accomplish the projects in the prioritized list. This may
           include bundling projects that are similarly prioritized to take advantage of economic
           scaling (e.g., parking lot restoration projects in the west park zone that score within
           ten places of each other may be combined into a single, larger project).
       o   Estimating costs for proposed projects. Projects ranking higher on the prioritized list
           will have more accurate estimates completed based on current and projected
           economic conditions and available Parks and Nature Division resources.
       o   Evaluating the prioritized list considering current Parks and Nature Division
           budgetary and staffing capacities, obligatory requirements, etc. When opportunities
           or challenges are identified, reorganization of the list may occur.
       o   Using the list of projects, including costs and any PAB comments, in developing the
           Parks and Nature Division capital budget for presentation to the public works director
           and county administration.



    Budgeting
    Budgeting for the CIP will occur by:
       o   Forecasting each fund available to pay for capital improvements in the Parks and
           Nature Division portfolio annually as part of the Public Works Business Services and
           Financial Controls Division’s budgetary process, forming the basis of fiscal constraint
           considerations. Fiscal constraint for the six-year period is a requirement of the CFP.
           This is different from the CIP, and while CIP projects may move around in response to
           conditions, the cumulative costs and impacts to the parks portfolio would need to be
           consistent with the constraints in the CFP.
       o   The Parks and Nature Division pursuing funding from outside sources, such as grants,
           for projects on CIP as available and where staff capacity exists. Successful
           competition for outside funding may result in adjustments to the CIP, as described
           below.
    The adopted CIP will be posted on the Parks and Nature Division website for public review
    throughout the following budget cycle.

Refinement
The CIP development process will occur annually as part of the budgetary process. Projects that
have been completed will be reported to the Clark County Auditor’s office for inclusion in the
Comprehensive Annual Financial Report (CAFR), as well as to the PAB as part of the annual CIP
update process. New projects will be added to the list per the process described above.
Reprioritization may result in changes from year to year and should not be construed as being
contrary to this policy.
The fiscal constraints mentioned above shall be applied to the overall project budget, subject to the
limitations and restrictions upon the different funding sources or appropriations, except when new
outside funding becomes available for a specific project(s).
The CIP is intended to be a guiding document for repair, acquisition and development of Parks and
Nature Division assets. It is intended to provide a realistic, achievable set of goals. However,
circumstantial changes may result in the need to amend the CIP. It will be the policy of the Parks and
Nature Division to make these amendments during the annual update, as described above. If such a
change occurs within a fiscal year, the Parks and Nature Division shall share this information with
the appropriate budgetary authority to request a change to the CIP.
If authorized and consistent with any requirements regarding such changes, the Parks and Nature
Division manager will prepare a report for the PAB and present it at the next scheduled meeting. The
change will be noted on the Parks and Nature Division website after presentation to the PAB.



Guiding Principles
In support of county policies and goals, the parks capital planning process strives to:
     Prioritize projects with the greatest potential to support multiple county programs and goals.
      Consider the impact of the project on people with barriers to recreational opportunities.
      Ensure reliable technical assessments of projects.
      Ensure each project in the plan is feasible and the most cost-effective way to meet an
       identified need.
      Focus limited resources on cost-effective solutions to the most pressing concerns.
      Incorporate environmental benefits into infrastructure repair projects.
      Maintain an inventory and condition assessment of existing assessments to ensure timely
       maintenance of facilities.

    Project Classifications
    Major Maintenance
    Description
    Major maintenance projects increase the life of an existing asset such as a park shelter and in most
    cases cost over $25,000. Some projects may still meet the major maintenance definition and cost
    below $25,000. These smaller projects frequently are not outlined in the CIP and would be funded
    by the emergent project category established in this plan.
    Strategy
    The Major Maintenance project schedule is currently based on an outdated prioritization list that is
    based on inventory and assessment work done over a several year period. Parks and Nature is
    developing a Facility Condition Assessment (FCA) through 2026 that will identify all assets in the
    system and their condition. The FCA will also develop a new prioritization matrix that will become
    the initial list of projects for the CIP. The total number of projects completed in each calendar year
    will be dependent on a number of factors including staffing and funding.
    Funding
    Funding for major maintenance projects primarily comes from Real Estate Excise Tax (REET2) and is
    currently limited to a 7-year average of $3.5 million per year. The amount of REET2 funds available
    for major maintenance projects is subject to change annually based on the REET2 projection
    performed by the Finance and Audit Team.

Capital Development
Funding
Funding for capital development primarily comes from Park Impact Fees (PIF) and is limited to the
balance of the PIF account for a given PIF district. The Greater Clark Park District is currently
divided into 10 PIF districts. The funds available in an individual district can only be used to make
recreation improvements or acquisitions that serve the district where the funds are available.
Community Parks within three miles of a PIF district, neighborhood parks within the district, trails
serving the district and a portion of regional parks serving the individual districts are all eligible for
PIF fund usage.
Parks and Nature staff also pursue grant funding to further our recreation enhancement projects.
Grant funds primarily come from the Washington State Recreation and Conservation Office (RCO)
or the Washington State Department of Commerce.

Park Operations and Maintenance

Adding additional recreation amenities and parks to our system comes with additional maintenance
requirements. Parks and Nature has added over a dozen new parks to the system over the last 10
years without adding operations personnel. While it is important for the Parks and Nature Division
to respond to the community’s desire for additional recreation opportunities and keep up with the
fast-paced growth of the county, the division needs to be mindful of our responsibility to build only
what we can maintain. While the Parks and Nature CIP project list is extensive, it does not represent
a development schedule that will keep up with the Level of Service (LOS) standard set out in the
current Clark County PROS plan to avoid adding to the workload of an overburdened operations
team. This development versus maintenance balance necessitates a shift away from new park
development toward maintenance and improvements at existing recreation sites. The Park
Enhancements category in the Capital Development section represents the focus on improvements
at existing sites.


        Parks and Nature leadership will pair each Capital Development project with an operations
        position request to cover the additional maintenance needs created by the addition of the
        new recreation amenities whether it be a new park or a park enhancement.

     Park Enhancements
     Description
     Park Enhancements (formerly referred to as Stewardship Capital) are improvements to existing
     parks. These projects focus on enhancing recreational opportunities in the Greater Clark Park
     District while minimizing new park development to reduce operations and maintenance staff drive
     time.
     Strategy
     Parks and Nature staff rank and prioritize projects consistent with the recreation needs, level of
     service requirements and strategies to meet current and future recreational demands identified in
     the PROS plan. Staff also monitors several grant funding programs to identify opportunities to
     secure additional funds for park enhancements.
     While the Park Enhancement Projects have a smaller impact on operations and maintenance needs
     given the efficiencies realized by a reduction in drivetime, there is still additional burden placed on
     our operations teams any time additional recreation amenities are added to the system. When new
     recreation amenities are added to an existing park the maintenance needs estimate for the site will
     be updated to approximate the additional FTE needs in our operations team at the park site.

     Property Acquisition
     Description
     Acquisitions add property to the county real estate portfolio whether they be park or Legacy Lands
     properties. Property acquired using Park Impact Fee (PIF) funds generally will be added to the list of
     properties that will be developed for recreation purposes while property acquired using
     Conservation Futures Funds are parcels planned for natural areas, farm, or forest preservation.


     Strategy
     The County seeks to acquire properties from willing sellers to meet both Level of Service (LOS) and
     conservation goals identified in the PROS Plan and the Natural Areas Acquisition Plan. The Capital
     Improvement Plan (CIP) outlines the annual and six-year acquisition strategy, organized by Park
     Impact Fee (PIF) district for park lands and by watershed for Legacy Lands. Prioritization of parkland
     acquisitions is driven by the specific LOS needs within each PIF district and the availability of PIF
     funds dedicated to property acquisition.

     Acquiring property outside the Greater Clark Park District (GCPD) boundary presents additional
     challenges due to the lack of a dedicated funding source for regional park development. While PIF
     funds may be used to purchase land outside the GCPD, those acquisitions must directly benefit the
     PIF district that generated the funds. As a result, such purchases must be paired with other county
     funding sources—primarily the General Fund—because once developed, these lands would serve
     both county residents and GCPD residents.

     As noted previously, Parks and Nature currently lacks sufficient staffing and operational funding to
     maintain all active recreation sites within the system. Despite these constraints, the division
     continues to pursue strategic property acquisitions as opportunities arise, ensuring the County is
     well positioned to develop future parks and address LOS deficiencies as resources become available.

Funding
This capital plan includes projects and ongoing programs totaling approximately $65,971,082 in
Parks and Nature Division funding over the next six years. The capital plan also represents legacy
lands acquisition funding totaling $23,800,000 for a total capital plan of $89,771,082. The first
year’s budget is an assessment, while the forecasts for the subsequent five years are subject to
change. Actual annual spending will be dependent on Parks and Nature Division resources
(personnel & funding), future budget approval and direction from council. The capital plan is
dependent on the funding programs outlined below.

Park Impact Fees Program (PIF)
PIF fees are collected within the GCPD when building permits are issued for single family homes,
apartments and other residential projects. The funds collected can be used for park development or
land acquisitions. The GCPD is divided into 10 impact fee districts. Park Impact Fees must be spent
on development or acquisition of property that serves the respective districts from which they are
collected. The funds must be spent within 10 years of the date of collection.

Real Estate Excise Tax Program (REET)
An excise tax is collected on the sale of real estate in the county. This program was developed in
1996 and readopted in 2002. The funds may be spent on parks and economic development. Funding
major maintenance and park development with excise tax is allowed. However, other Public Works
Divisions also rely on excise tax dollars to fund projects in their individual Capital Improvement
Plans. Parks currently uses available excise tax only for major maintenance.

Greater Clark Parks District Program (GCPD)
The GCPD was created via voter approved levy in 2005. The levy included the condition that 35
new parks would be built and maintained, and new sports fields and trails would be developed
within the new district. A metropolitan park district taxing authority was authorized within the
unincorporated urban area of the city of Vancouver (VUUA). The tax rate was set at a maximum of
$0.27 per $1,000 of assessed property value. The fund is used primarily for the operation and
maintenance of parks within the district boundary.

Conservation Futures Fund (CF)
Clark County created the Conservation Futures fund in 1985. The primary revenue source for the
fund is the Conservation Futures property tax levy, a county-wide levy that cannot exceed $0.625
per $1,000 valuation. The program acquires natural area properties for the purposes of
conservation, that can have some recreational use.
