Dear Chair and Members of the Council:

I am the owner of Crestline Development Group, LLC, a land development consulting firm,
and a member of the Building Industry Association of Clark County (BIA). I work directly
with property owners, builders, and developers to navigate the land use planning and
permitting processes necessary to bring new housing and development to our community.

The decisions made through the 2025–2045 Comprehensive Plan Update will shape Clark
County's ability to accommodate growth and deliver housing that local families can afford for
the next 20 years.

I support the adoption of a Comprehensive Plan, but urge the Council to ensure the final plan
is grounded in sound, defensible analysis and reflects the real costs of development and their
impact on housing affordability.

I respectfully ask the Council to address the following before adopting the plan:

1. Validate the land capacity assumptions before relying on them for 20 years. The Vacant
Buildable Lands Model applies never-to-convert assumptions of 10 percent for vacant
residential land, 30 percent for underutilized residential land, and 0 percent for commercial,
business park and industrial land. We have not seen evidence that these percentages have been
tested against what has actually developed in Clark County. If they overstate buildable
capacity, every urban growth area and zoning decision built on them is less reliable, and
harder to fix after adoption than before. Please direct staff to complete a documented market-
factor sensitivity and validation analysis before the final capacity determination is relied upon.

2. Consider the combined cost of new fees - and who ultimately pays them.. Higher school
impact fees, new fire district impact fees, and higher land use and wetland review fees were
approved separately within weeks of one another. The Council should see the stacked total per
home, not a series of isolated votes. Builders and developers write the check at the permit
counter, but the homebuyer pays the bill, in the price of the home and in the mortgage they
carry for decades. We also ask the Council to confirm that the new fire district fees rest on a
clear showing that each charge is tied to the cost of growth-related fire service impacts.

3. Size urban growth area expansions to real, near-term demand and available infrastructure.
Battle Ground, Camas, La Center, Ridgefield and Yacolt each have expansion requests in this
plan. Each should be judged on demonstrated housing need and existing or funded
infrastructure, not treated as a one-time opportunity to add land regardless of how much the
market can absorb. Oversized or misplaced expansions can lock up land in the wrong places
for two decades while under-serving the places where housing is actually needed.

4. Make sure permitting and infrastructure capacity keep pace with planned growth. Capacity
on paper means little if permit review timelines, water and sewer capacity, and roads cannot
support the development the plan assumes. Every month a project waits in review adds
carrying costs that ultimately land on the buyer.

5. Keep the County’s middle housing code consistent with the cities’. The County’s Housing
Options Study and Action Plan governs unincorporated urban growth areas, while HB 1110
separately governs the cities. If standards, densities or review processes diverge meaningfully
across the line, builders face different rules project to project and buyers face uneven housing
choices. We ask the Council to confirm the County’s code aligns in substance with what its
cities are required to do.

6. Build in a way to correct course. If the assumptions behind this plan turn out to be wrong,
the County should not have to wait a full planning cycle to find out. Please include a defined
monitoring and mid-cycle review trigger tied to actual permit and absorption data.

7. State the affordability impact before you vote. Every fee increase and every acre of
constrained capacity has a measurable effect on the cost of a home. We ask that the effect be
stated plainly in the record for the decisions that make up this plan, not just discussed in
general terms.

We are not asking the Council to delay or reject the Comprehensive Plan. We are asking that
the record and the methodology be strong enough that the plan stands up to review and to the
next 20 years without reopening these fights.

Thank you for your service and for considering these comments. I would welcome the
opportunity to share more about my experience with you or your staff.

Councilmembers,

On behalf of the Building Industry Association of Clark County, we appreciate the chance to
add to the record before it closes at noon today, October 13. Our members followed the
October 1 hearing closely, and we recognize the significant effort the Council and staff have
put into this Comprehensive Plan update. Reaching the finish line on a project of this scope is
no small accomplishment.

Two issues surfaced late in the process that the public had little or no opportunity to weigh in
on. Our requests below support the plan’s overall direction. They are narrow fixes meant to
keep the plan working as intended once it is applied on the ground.

Keep clustering available in the AG zone

The BIA urges the Council to remove the recent amendment prohibiting cluster development
in the AG zone from the Comprehensive Plan ordinance.

Clustering is one of the more effective tools available for keeping farmland in production.
Rather than breaking land into a patchwork of 20-acre lots, a cluster approach groups the
allowed density where it makes sense and leaves behind a single large remainder parcel that
can actually support an agricultural operation. Eliminating that option risks producing exactly
the fragmentation the AG zone is meant to prevent.

We are also concerned about how this change entered the process. It was not included in
published notice, and neither the public nor the County’s advisory boards had a meaningful
chance to review it. A change with these implications deserves its own notice, analysis, and
public comment. We ask that it be set aside for now and brought back as a separate item.

Make sure density rules don’t work against family housing

Our second concern involves how minimum density is counted. When every dwelling counts
the same, a studio and a three-bedroom unit carry equal weight toward a project’s required
density. Under aggressive minimums, builders are pushed toward studios and one-bedrooms
simply because that is the most reliable way to hit the unit count within a fixed building
envelope. The result is fewer homes suited to families, which runs counter to the community’s
housing needs.

The City of Vancouver has already addressed this problem. Its code gives larger units extra
weight in the density calculation, with each two-bedroom unit counting as 1.25 units and each
three-bedroom unit counting as 1.5. That approach keeps overall housing production on track
while making room for the mix of home sizes our region needs.

This is not a new idea for the County. It appears in Exhibit 1 – Planning Commission/Council
Decision Table as part of the recommended medium- and high-density code revisions for the
Vancouver UGA (Items 11, 11A, and 65), so it has already been through public notice. It
simply did not receive full discussion during deliberations.

The BIA asks the Council to adopt a density credit for two- and three-bedroom units modeled
on Vancouver’s, or another approach that achieves the same result, so that projects are not
penalized for building family-sized homes.

We appreciate the Council’s attention to these two items and thank you again for your work on
the plan.

Dear Chair and Members of the Council:

Romano Capital is a developer, a lender, and a member of the Building Industry Association
of Clark County (BIA). We develop as well as provide lending to builders for single-family
housing and multifamily projects in Clark County, and the decisions you make in the
Comprehensive Plan Update will shape our ability to provide single-family lots to home
builders that local families can afford for the next 20 years. We support adopting a
Comprehensive Plan. We write ahead of your October 13 vote to ask that the final plan rest on
analysis that can withstand scrutiny and on costs that Clark County homebuyers can carry.

Romano Capital brings multiple properties to market each year in Clark County. The increase
in permitting times and fees is a cost that ultimately lands on the families buying the homes or
renting the apartment. Each of Romano’s projects supports local employees and
subcontractors. From framers and electricians to suppliers, when projects stall, those are the
Clark County workers who feel it first.

We respectfully ask the Council to address the following before adopting the plan:

1. Validate the land capacity assumptions before relying on them for 20 years. The Vacant
Buildable Lands Model applies never-to-convert assumptions of 10 percent for vacant
residential land, 30 percent for underutilized residential land, and 0 percent for commercial,
business park, and industrial land. We have not seen evidence that these percentages have
been tested against what has actually developed in Clark County. If they overstate buildable
capacity, every urban growth area and zoning decision built on them is less reliable, and
harder to fix after adoption than before. Please direct staff to complete a documented market-
factor sensitivity and validation analysis before the final capacity determination is relied upon.

2. Consider the combined cost of new fees — and who ultimately pays them. Higher school
impact fees, new fire district impact fees, and higher land use and wetland review fees were
approved separately within weeks of one another. The Council should see the stacked total per
home, not a series of isolated votes. Builders and developers write the check at the permit
counter, but the homebuyer pays the bill, in the price of the home and in the mortgage they
carry for decades. We also ask the Council to confirm that the new fire district fees rest on a
clear showing that each charge is tied to the cost of growth-related fire service impacts.

3. Size urban growth area expansions to real, near-term demand and available infrastructure.
Battle Ground, Camas, La Center, Ridgefield and Yacolt each have expansion requests in this
plan. Each should be judged on demonstrated housing need and existing or funded
infrastructure, not treated as a one-time opportunity to add land regardless of how much the
market can absorb. Oversized or misplaced expansions can lock up land in the wrong places
for two decades while under-serving the places where housing is actually needed.

4. Make sure permitting and infrastructure capacity keep pace with planned growth. Capacity
on paper means little if permit review timelines, water and sewer capacity, and roads cannot
support the development the plan assumes. Every month a project waits in review adds
carrying costs that ultimately land on the buyer.

5. Keep the County’s middle housing code consistent with the cities’. The County’s Housing
Options Study and Action Plan governs unincorporated urban growth areas, while HB 1110
separately governs the cities. If standards, densities, or review processes diverge meaningfully
across the line, builders face different rules project to project, and buyers face uneven housing
choices. We ask the Council to confirm the County’s code aligns in substance with what its
cities are required to do.

6. Build in a way to correct course. If the assumptions behind this plan turn out to be wrong,
the County should not have to wait a full planning cycle to find out. Please include a defined
monitoring and mid-cycle review trigger tied to actual permit and absorption data.

7. State the affordability impact before you vote. Every fee increase and every acre of
constrained capacity has a measurable effect on the cost of a home. We ask that the effect be
stated plainly in the record for the decisions that make up this plan, not just discussed in
general terms.

We are not asking the Council to delay or reject the Comprehensive Plan. We are asking that
the record and the methodology be strong enough that the plan stands up to review and to the
next 20 years without reopening these fights. Thank you for your service and for considering
our comments. We would welcome the opportunity to share more about our experience with
you or your staff.

Executive summary
When the County presented the Comprehensive Plan’s transportation funding in July,
the six-year program was shown in balance and the funding gap sat in the last 14 years
of the plan. Less than three months later, Public Works reported that the 2027–2032
TIP is projected to draw down Road Fund reserves even with a 1% levy increase. That
program starts only one year later than the one shown as balanced.

The near-term funding the plan relies on is now in doubt, and the long-term gap, still in
the range of $500 million, remains unresolved. The plan places density along the edges
of the urban growth areas, including along NE 72nd Avenue and SR 503, without a
funded program for the north-south capacity those areas need, a need Public Works
identified in 2014.

The public cannot see how road projects are prioritized. The one set of projects whose
reason for being on the TIP is known, the NE 179th Street projects required to lift the
urban holding, is barely underway nearly seven years later, with the remaining work
scheduled through 2033.

The Council should not adopt the Comprehensive Plan on October 13 while the land
use, transportation, and capital facilities elements remain out of balance.

1. The six-year program was presented as funded but no longer is
The July 15, 2026 Council work session presentation (pp. 11–16) showed revenue and
expenditures for 2026–2031 both at $246.8 million. The same presentation showed
$1,605.8 million in 20-year needs against $1,021.3 million in revenue, a gap of about
$585 million that fell almost entirely after 2031. Later revisions narrowed the gap, but
the gap remains in the range of $500 million.

On October 7, 2026, Public Works told the Council that operating costs are outpacing
recurring revenues, so Road Fund money available for capital shrinks each year. Public
Works reported that even with a 1% levy increase, the TIP is projected to draw on Road
Fund reserves by the end of the six-year period. The presentation listed the
consequences as jeopardized grant leverage, reduced ability to meet capital
commitments, and future discussions of level-of-service reductions.

The October 14, 2026 TIP presentation shows a $260.8 million program. Of its $74.9
million in grants, $38.2 million is projected rather than secured. Among the risks the
presentation lists are a shrinking Road Fund for capital and volatile traffic impact fee
revenue.

The plan’s funding analysis rests on a six-year program that Public Works now says
cannot be sustained without reserves. That raises concern about delivering what is
currently on the TIP, before the larger post-2031 gap is considered. RCW
36.70A.070(6)(a)(iv)(C) requires the plan to address how additional funding will be
raised, or how land use assumptions will be reassessed, to ensure level of service
standards will be met. I have been unable to find a revenue plan in the Comprehensive
Plan that closes the gap.

2. Density is being placed at the edges of the urban growth areas without the
north-south capacity to serve that density
At the February 12, 2014 Council work session, Steve Schulte of Public Works
described modeling of the adopted land use that showed NE 72nd Avenue failing
northbound from NE 119th to NE 219th Street. He said “72nd clearly is a need.” He also
said the County was not adding north-south capacity on SR 503, NE 50th, NE 29th, or
east of SR 503, and that 72nd was the only place new north-south capacity would be
built over 20 years.

The 2027–2032 TIP includes only NE 72nd Avenue from NE 119th to NE 139th Street,
scheduled for 2031–2032. The 2014 modeling showed the failure extending to NE 219th
Street.

NE 72nd Avenue and SR 503 carry traffic between Battle Ground and the Vancouver
urban growth area, and the plan adds density along both routes. NE 179th Street is
another area of significant growth in the plan. SR 503 is a state highway, and its
improvements are WSDOT’s responsibility. That does not relieve the County of planning
for the effect of the density the County places along a state route. I have been unable to
find a funded capacity project for SR 503 in this area.

Ridgefield’s growth adds to the load on County north-south roads. Without a direct
connection to I-5 on the south side of the city, Ridgefield traffic uses County roads,
including NE Delfel Road, to reach the NE 179th Street interchange.
3. The cumulative effect of concurrent development is captured only in part
RTC monitors traffic on the regional system, but counts show today’s conditions. They
do not include trips from development that is approved or in process but not yet built.
Those trips enter the analysis through developer-commissioned traffic studies. Each
study is scoped to a single development and includes other development only to the
extent that development is loaded into that study. With so much development in process
at the same time, the cumulative effect on a corridor can be understated.

NE 72nd Avenue shows the risk. The County produced an early version of PLS
Engineering’s failing-corridors map in response to public records request P056419-
051226 (p. 000180). That version marks segments of NE 72nd Avenue as failing, along
with segments on SR 503. The version submitted to the record of the 174th Street
Subdivision (PLD-2026-00002, Ex. 100) does not show the NE 72nd Avenue segments,
and the developer-commissioned traffic studies I have reviewed do not include NE 72nd
Avenue in their study areas. A corridor failure that does not appear in the studies used
to approve development cannot shape where density goes or which projects are
funded.

4. How road projects are prioritized is unknown
With constrained funds and density planned at the edges of the urban growth areas, the
order in which road projects are built determines whether infrastructure keeps pace with
growth. The October 14 TIP presentation lists the evaluation criteria, with concurrency
worth 10 points. The presentation does not show any project’s score, its rank, or why a
project is scheduled, deferred, or left off the TIP.

The NE 179th Street projects are the only TIP projects whose reason for being there is
known: they were required to lift the urban holding. Nearly seven years after the Council
voted to lift the holding, two left-turn lanes have been added at NE 50th Avenue and the
NE 29th Avenue intersection is under construction through 2027. The October 14 TIP
schedule places the remaining NE 179th Street work in 2028 through 2033, with NE
29th to NE 50th Avenue in 2032–2033, beyond the six-year TIP. If projects tied directly
to lifting the urban holding are moving this slowly, the public needs to see how other
projects are ranked, including the north-south capacity needed for the density this plan
adds.

Conclusion
The six-year program the plan relies on was presented as funded in July and is now
projected to need reserves. The 20-year gap, still in the range of $500 million, remains
unresolved. North-south capacity needs identified in 2014 remain unfunded, while the
plan adds density at the edges of the urban growth areas that depend on those roads.
The cumulative effect of concurrent development is captured only in part, and the public
cannot see how projects are prioritized.

The land use element is not matched by the transportation and capital facilities
elements. The plan adds density without showing that the roads serving that density can
be funded or built, and without reassessing land use as RCW 36.70A.070(6)(a)(iv)(C)
requires when funding falls short. The Council should not adopt the Comprehensive
Plan on October 13 while the elements remain out of balance.

WHAT WILL R32 ZONING MEAN FOR YOU AND OUR PLEASANT VALLEY NEIGHBORHOOD?

The proposed R32 rezoning of the 88-acre property at 139th Street and NE 50th Avenue isn’t
simply a change on a zoning map. It could fundamentally change the neighborhood many of us
have chosen to call home.

HIGH-DENSITY HOUSING NEXT TO OUR SINGLE-FAMILY HOMES

The proposed R32 zoning could allow development of approximately 2,464 apartments on this
property.

That means high-density, multi-family housing could be placed directly alongside an established
community of single-family homes.
Many of us live here because of the quieter, less-congested character of Pleasant Valley. We
enjoy our homes, our yards, our streets and the ability to let our children and pets enjoy the
neighborhood.

Placing this level of density next to existing single-family homes could dramatically change that
character.

THOUSANDS OF NEW PEOPLE

The projected development could bring approximately 6,899 additional residents into the area.

Think about what that means for a neighborhood that currently has a very different scale and
density.

Our streets, intersections, parks, schools, utilities and emergency services would all face
additional demands. This isn’t incremental growth. It is a massive increase in population
concentrated in one location.

THOUSANDS OF ADDITIONAL VEHICLES

The projected development could also bring an estimated 3,080 additional vehicles into the area.

When parking within the apartment development is limited, where will those vehicles go?

They don’t simply disappear. They spill onto surrounding streets and into neighboring
communities.

That means more traffic on roads that were not designed for this level of density—and potentially
more cars traveling through the streets where our families currently walk, bike, play and walk their
dogs.

OUR CURRENTLY SAFE NEIGHBORHOOD STREETS COULD BECOME BUSIER AND MORE
DANGEROUS

Many of us currently think of our neighborhood streets as relatively safe places for children to
play, families to walk and pets to be outside.

A dramatic increase in traffic changes that.

More vehicles means more opportunities for accidents, speeding, distracted driving and conflicts
with pedestrians, bicyclists, children and animals.

A street that feels safe today may not feel the same way after thousands of additional residents
and vehicles are introduced.

Our children and pets shouldn’t have to pay the price for inadequate planning.

THIS IS ABOUT MORE THAN APARTMENTS

This is about whether the County should place a high-density development of this magnitude
directly next to an existing single-family neighborhood without fully addressing the
consequences.

Before approving R32 zoning, we should have clear answers about:

* Traffic: Can the existing road network safely handle thousands of additional vehicles?
* Parking: Where will residents and visitors park when on-site parking is insufficient?
* Pedestrian safety: What will protect children, pedestrians and bicyclists from increased traffic?
* Neighborhood safety: How will increased vehicle traffic affect pets and children on our
residential streets?
* Infrastructure: Can existing roads, utilities, drainage and emergency services accommodate this
scale of development?
* Neighborhood character: Is it appropriate to place high-density housing immediately beside
established single-family homes?

PLEASE DON’T TREAT THIS AS JUST ANOTHER ZONING CHANGE.

For those of us who already live here, this is personal.

We aren’t opposed to growth or responsible development. We are asking for development that
fits the community rather than overwhelms it.

Once 2,464 apartments and thousands of new residents are built, the change is permanent.

Before the County approves R32 zoning, let’s make sure the people who already live in Pleasant

We write regarding the 88-acre site at the intersection of NE 139th Street and NE 50th Avenue. We
support redesignating the site from its current low-density residential zoning to a medium-density
residential zone. We also object to how the County plans to serve that density. The upzoning is
the right land use decision. Approving it without funded road, sidewalk, and parking solutions is
not.

Why medium density is right for this site
The Growth Management Act directs the County to plan for 20 years of population and housing
growth inside urban growth areas, and to do it with urban densities. Eighty-eight acres inside the
Vancouver urban growth area is a rare contiguous block. Zoned for low density, it yields a limited
number of large-lot homes. Zoned for medium density, it can deliver a broader mix of housing
types at prices more households can reach, and it makes better use of the water, sewer and
stormwater service the area already pays for.
Medium density also supports the County's own direction on middle housing and on
consolidating residential districts. It spreads land and infrastructure costs over more homes and
eases pressure to expand the urban growth area onto farm and forest land. [Insert site-specific
capacity figures, projected unit range and comparison to the current zone.

Where we dissent: the transportation plan does not match the density
More homes mean more trips. At medium density, the site will add households and daily trips to a
road network that was built for rural and low-density traffic. The comprehensive plan and capital
facilities program do not yet show how that demand will be served. We see four gaps.
First, road capacity. NE 139th Street and NE 50th Avenue carry traffic on rural cross sections with
limited shoulders and no turn lanes at key intersections. State and County rules require that
transportation improvements be made concurrent with development. Residents and applicants
across the north county have already raised concurrency concerns on parallel corridors,
including NE 179th Street. We ask the County to show, in the plan, which capacity projects serve
this site and when they will be built.
Second, sidewalks. There are no continuous sidewalks along the site frontage or on the routes to
nearby schools, transit stops and commercial services. Adding hundreds of households to roads
with no walking facilities puts residents, including children, on road shoulders. The County's own
transportation needs work tracks missing sidewalk length by roadway segment. The density we
are supporting should not take effect until a safe walking network exists.
Third, parking. The County's July 15, 2026, draft amendments to Clark County Code 40.340.010
would cut minimum off-street parking for multifamily development from 1.5 spaces to 0.5 spaces
per dwelling unit. The draft sets a minimum of 1 space per unit for single-family homes,
townhouses, duplexes, triplexes, quadplexes and cottage housing, and removes minimums for
units under 1,200 sq. ft., affordable and senior housing, and co-living. A medium-density plan for
88 acres will therefore be built with fewer on-site spaces than households own vehicles. That
demand has to go somewhere.
In this area, it has nowhere to go. The surrounding roads have no on-street parking lanes, no curbs
and no public parking lots. Nearby commercial, school and park uses park their own users on site
and have no spare capacity. Visitors, second and third household vehicles, service and delivery
trips, and overflow from community amenities would spill onto road shoulders, into neighboring
subdivisions and onto private lots. That blocks emergency access, narrows already limited
shoulders, and conflicts with the walking facilities we are asking the County to build. Lower
parking minimums work where transit, walkable streets and public parking already exist. This area
has none of the three.
Fourth, funding and timing. Road, sidewalk and parking projects that appear only on long-range
lists do not meet a household's need on move-in day. Without a funding source and a delivery
date, the burden shifts to existing neighbors and to future residents.
What we request
• Approve the medium-density designation for the site as proposed.
• Add the NE 139th Street and NE 50th Avenue road improvements and sidewalk connections
serving the site to the transportation element and the six-year transportation improvement
program, with identified funding.
• Tie development phasing to transportation delivery, so that building permits and occupancy
track the completion of frontage and off-site improvements.
• Require sidewalks, curb, gutter and bike facilities along all site frontage as a condition of
development, and identify off-site gap segments to schools, transit and services.
• Apply a site-specific parking standard. Where the reduced code minimums would leave this
area without a public or on-street parking option, require guest and overflow parking on site, set at
a ratio supported by a parking demand study, and allow shared parking among compatible uses.
• Require on-street parking bays, designated guest parking areas and enforceable no-parking
designations in the street design, so overflow does not block emergency access or sidewalks.
• Establish a fair, proportionate cost-sharing approach, such as a development agreement or
transportation impact fee credits, so that funds its share and the County funds the rest.
Our commitment
Construct frontage improvements, dedicate right-of-way, provide a parking program sized to
demonstrate demand, and work with County Public Works on a phasing plan. We ask the County
to match that commitment by scheduling and funding the network improvements that only the
public sector can deliver.
Density without infrastructure creates a problem for everyone. Density with infrastructure creates
