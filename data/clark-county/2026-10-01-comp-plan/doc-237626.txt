4    Summary
5    Transportation and transportation infrastructure issues have been central to the public discussion regarding the
6    preparation of an updated Comprehensive Plan for Clark County. This appendix represents a summary of the policy
7    discussions undertaken by the Board of County Councilors:

8           1. Adoption of a new 20-year transportation capital facilities plan to support the land use plan.

 9          2. Confirmation of the 6-year Transportation Improvement Program as the first 6 years of the
10             transportation capital facilities plan.

11          3. Amendments to the transportation concurrency standards contained both in the transportation element
12             of the comprehensive plan and in Clark County Code.

13   This appendix identifies the current and future needs of the county’s transportation system, along with the specific
14   actions and requirements for bringing facilities into compliance with the multimodal level of service standards
15   established in the Comprehensive Plan’s Transportation Element. A financial analysis section has been prepared to
16   compare projected revenue against anticipated costs to guide future policy discussions and actions regarding funding.
17   The final section of this report appendix describes the strategies and future policy actions which, in some combination,
18   could be adopted to address the projected shortfall in revenues to fully fund the proposed transportation capital
19   facilities plan.

16   The transportation CFP uses the analysis and findings from the Clark County Road Safety Plan (LRSP) to identify the

20         •   The most common crash type for severe crashes was roadway departure, at 39.4% of all fatal and serious
21             injury crashes.

24             key factors in fatal and serious injury crashes.
25         •   Over one in five fatal and serious injury crashes (21.5%) involved a motorcycle.

1    Level-of-Service Standards

 2   Level-of-service (LOS) standards serve both as a standard of measure in administration of the county’s transportation
 3   concurrency program and as a general indicator of congestion levels. The goals of the Transportation Concurrency
 4   Program and the Growth Management Act require a balance between land development and the transportation
 5   facilities serving that development. The variables in this balance include the growth rate, transportation investments
 6   and level-of-service standards. The growth rate was chosen from a range provided by the State Office of Financial
 7   Management. Transportation improvement investments were planned over the 20-year horizon based on
 8   transportation model analysis and a projection of current revenue streams. The LOS standards are subject to local
 9   discretion, but to some extent are dependent on the growth rate, revenue, capital improvements and the local level of
10   tolerance for peak hour traffic congestion. Maintaining current LOS standards would require either increasing
11   transportation investments through more revenue or a reduction in the chosen growth rate and the supply of
12   buildable lands.

13   Even with the capacity provided by the improvements in the transportation CFP and the Metropolitan Transportation
14   Plan, the transportation cost/revenue analysis shows that it may not be possible to maintain the current adopted
15   corridor level-of-service standards. The county population will increase by about 128,586 residents. The number of
16   jobs will also grow to approximately 101,153. These two major factors plus the projected increase in vehicle miles
17   traveled will likely result in levels of congestion that could require a lowering of standards in the future. One purpose
18   of the concurrency program is to prevent land development from greatly outpacing transportation facilities and
19   services. If specific areas of the county rapidly develop before the public and private investments are made in
20   surrounding corridors, moratoria declared by ordinance may be one result. The county also uses Urban Holding to
21   insure that critical improvements are reasonably funded before new areas are opened for urban development.

22   WAC 365-196-415(2)(b) recommends “Counties forecast needs for capital facilities during the planning period, based
23   on the levels of service or planning assumptions selected and consistent with the growth, densities and distribution of
24   growth anticipated in the land use element. The forecast should include reasonable assumptions about the effect of
25   any identified system management or demand management approaches to preserve capacity or avoid the need for
26   new facilities.” The needs analysis for the 2016-2035 Comprehensive Growth Management Plan utilized travel
27   demand forecast modeling to determine locations where improvements to the transportation system may be
28   necessary. This analysis focused on two types of transportation deficiencies: segments (link) and intersections.

29   Segment (Link) Analysis

30   The link deficiency analysis focused on the PM peak hour Committed 2035 RTC model. All links showing volume to
31   capacity (v/c) ratios greater than 0.90 were identified as corridor level deficiencies. Once the deficiencies were
32   identified, the PM Peak hour Capital Facilities Plan 2035 RTC model was analyzed for deficiencies, using the same link
33   level criteria (v/c > 0.9). The link level network improvements between the Committed model and Capital Facilities
34   Plan model were identified as projects and reviewed to determine which (if any) deficiency each project addressed.
35   The projects that met an identified link level deficiency were kept in the updated Financially Constrained Project list.
36   Projects included in the Financially Constrained model but not addressing any identified deficiencies were removed
37   from the updated Capital Facilities Plan Project list. All link deficiencies identified in the Capital Facilities Plan model
38   were addressed with new capacity improvement projects. These projects were added to the updated Capital Facilities
39   Plan Project list. Comparisons between the RTC models with the old land use and the updated land use indicated
40   significant trip loss within the Vancouver city limits, especially on the freeways (I-5 and I-205). As this trip loss was
41   attributed to some outdated land use projects within the Vancouver city limits, the major WSDOT projects on I-5 and


                                                                  8
1    I-205 were not compared to modeled deficiencies, but were kept unchanged on the updated Capital Facilities Plan
2    project list. The same approach was used when analyzing projects in urban areas near the Vancouver city limits.

3    All new segment projects were coded simply as increased link level capacity within the travel models. In addition, the
4    committed model network was updated to include all the committed projects from the most recent Capital Facilities
5    Plan.

6    Intersection Analysis

 7   The intersection deficiency analysis also focused on the PM peak hour Committed 2035 RTC model. The analysis
 8   focused on unsignalized intersections with forecasted volumes high enough to trigger possible improvements.
 9   Unsignalized intersection deficiencies were estimated based on the conflicting major/minor street unsignalized
10   capacities. The conflicting volume analysis helps identify intersections that may fail to meet LOS E standards or may
11   meet signal warrants. As all the intersection analysis was performed at the approach link level (turn volumes were not
12   analyzed). Intersections identified by this process do not necessarily require signalization and in some cases, other
13   intersection improvements may be sufficient. The potential deficiencies were revised after assuming some traffic
14   disaggregation on the modeled collector roadways, as the Committed 2035 RTC model is a simplified network with
15   aggregated volumes. For example, potential deficiencies that were triggered based on aggregated volumes from local
16   roads not included in the Committed 2035 RTC model were not included as intersection deficiencies since these
17   volumes would likely be spread across multiple intersections. Next, the intersection deficiencies were compared to
18   the corridor level deficiencies and overlapping deficiencies were grouped into one project. All remaining intersection
19   deficiencies identified were addressed with new intersection improvement projects. These projects were added to the
20   updated Capital Facilities Plan Project list.

3    FUNDING ANALYSIS
4    Clark County’s transportation system is the backbone upon which the community is developed. With a growing
5    population and increasing traffic, the county focuses on developing and improving the system to improve mobility and
6    safety for all users including those choosing active transportation options such as walking, biking and transit. Funding
7    for these projects is complex.

 8   A financial analysis was prepared for the Transportation CFP to demonstrate the ability of the county to fund planned
 9   roadway improvements. The Growth Management Act (GMA) requires that there be a balance between proposed
10   land use, new transportation improvements directed by the Multimodal Level of Service (MMLOS) standards, and
11   available revenue. The GMA requires that public facilities and infrastructure either be in place or included in the six-
12   year Transportation Improvement Program (TIP) before new development can be approved. The GMA also enables
13   the imposition of impact fees, which are used to recover a portion of the transportation CFP’s cost that is related to
14   new development.

15   A comparison of projected revenue and expenditures of the 20-year planning period, as shown in Figure A.1,
16   anticipates $554 million shortfall. While GMA does not require that the 20-year CFP be financially constrained, the
17   financial analysis demonstrates what the County will be able to reasonably provide with current funding levels.

3    REVENUE PERSPECTIVE EXISTING REVENUE SOURCES
 4   The following is a review of the projected revenue sources included in the financial analysis, which includes property
 5   taxes dedicated to transportation (“road fund”), gasoline tax distributions to the county, traffic impact fees, Public
 6   Works Trust Fund loans, expected grants and miscellaneous revenue streams that accrue for transportation
 7   purposes. The analysis accounts for road fund operating expenses that reduce the revenue available for capital
 8   facilities projects, which are outlined in WAC 136-15-030. The estimated available revenue for county capital
 9   transportation improvements over the 20-years of the land use plan is $533.1 million as adopted in 2014 (ORD 2014-
10   11-03). These revenue sources available for financing transportation improvements can be highly variable from year
11   to year depending on the amount of development activity occurring in the county, the number of available grant
12   programs and successful applications, and other local economic factors.

13   County Road Levy
14   The County generates revenue from property taxes to help fund new and maintain existing transportation
15   infrastructure. The property tax levy for the road fund is the primary source of local transportation-related funding,

2    The State of Washington levies a motor vehicle fuel tax (MVFT) upon the sale of gasoline and diesel. The tax is based
3    on the amount of fuel purchased and the rate is fixed and remains constant irrespective of fluctuations in the cost of
4    fuel. As of July 1, 2026, the Washington Department of Revenue lists the effective state rate at 56.5 cents per gallon
5    for gasoline and 59.5 cents per gallon for diesel. The revenue from this tax is used for state and local transportation
6    funding.

 7   Local jurisdictions receive allocations of the state’s MVFT through direct distributions and awards from grant
 8   programs. While it is perceived that the MVFT is a significant revenue source, it only accounts for a small portion of
 9   local transportation-related funding. In 2025, the County received $6,313,794 in direct distributions, accounting for
10   roughly 10% of all funding sources for the County Road Fund.

11   Grant Funding
12   There are multiple State and Federal funding programs that provide transportation funding through competitive and
13   need based grants. The Clark County 6-year TIP provides a comprehensive list of all funding programs that the
14   County regularly pursues. Each program has different requirements and eligible projects, and funding from these
15   sources is dependent upon successful applications fluctuate year to year. For the financial analysis, it was assumed
16   that 25% of overall project costs will be covered by grants.

17   Real Estate Excise Taxes
18   The State of Washington levies a real estate excise tax (REET) upon most sales of real property. The tax is calculated
19   based on the full selling price, including the amount of any liens, mortgages, and other debts given to secure the
20   purchase. The tax is due 30 days from the date of the sale and is collected by the county treasurer. The excise tax and
21   processing fee must be paid in full before the deed or vesting information will be recorded by the Auditor’s Office.

22   In addition to the state real estate excise tax, cities and counties may impose local real estate excise taxes. The two
23   main REET options for the county are:

24       ▪   REET 1, or the “first quarter percent”, a 0.25% REET which may be imposed by the county primarily for capital
25           projects and limited maintenance.
26       ▪   REET 2, or the “second quarter percent”, an additional 0.25% REET which may be imposed by the county
27           primarily for capital projects and limited maintenance. Clark County qualifies to utilize this funding option
28           because the county is fully planning under the Growth Management Act. REET 2 is only applied to the
29           unincorporated areas.

30   Regarding transportation, REET 1 and REET 2 revenues may be used for planning, acquisition, construction,
31   reconstruction, repair, replacement, rehabilitation, survey or improvement of a wide variety of projects identified in
32   the capital facility plan as established in RCW 82.46. This includes streets, roads, highways, sidewalks, street and road
33   lighting systems, traffic signals and bridges.

36   needed by growth. Clark County adopted an impact fee ordinance in September 1990 and has subsequently
37   amended that program multiple times to address increasing improvement costs.

1    TRANSPORTATION IMPACT FEE PROGRAM
2    Clark County’s Transportation Impact Fee (TIF) Program is one of four development impact fee programs that collect
3    funding to ensure that adequate facilities are available to serve growth and new development. The county first
4    adopted transportation impact fees beginning in 1990. In 2001, the program underwent an overhaul to synchronize
5    the county’s program with the city of Vancouver’s TIF program. An interlocal agreement was later executed in with
6    the city to jointly administer TIF program in 2009, and in 2016 the program underwent another overhaul which
7    created separate TIF programs for both the city of Vancouver and the county. There have been no substantial updates
8    to the program since 2016.

9    PROGRAM OVERVIEW

10   Rate Calculation Methodology
11   The TIF rates are calculated using the cost estimates from the 20-year Project List Section and the methodology was
12   approved with the 2016 program update. The rates are calculated as follows:

13       1. Transportation Analysis Zones (TAZs) from RTC’s Regional Travel Demand Model are assigned to a TIF
14           district based on geography;
15       2. Base-year (2025) pm peak-hour trip ends (PMPTs) are estimated for each district;
16       3. Future year (2045) PMPTs are estimated for each district;
17       4. Minimum district private share is calculated using: (PMPT 2045 – PMPT 2025) / PMPT 2045
18       5. District trip allocations are determined for each capital improvement project by identifying where trips
19           traveling along or through an improvement end;
20       6. A private share is established for each project by combining district private shares and district trip allocations;
21       7. Total private share cost is calculated for each project;
22       8. Each district’s share of a project’s private cost is calculated by distributing costs according to trip allocations;
23       9. All project-level private costs are summed for each district and divided by the district’s growth in trips to
24           determine the cost per PMPTs;
25       10. A cost per Average Daily Trip (ADT) is calculated by dividing the cost per PMPTs by 10

26   Trip Generation Rates
27   The TIF program utilizes the most recent version of the Institute of Transportation Engineers (ITE) Trip Generation
28   Manual and Trip Generation Handbook to determine the trip generation rates used in the Impact Fee Formula
29   outlined in CCC 40.6 - Development Impact Fees.

8    ANNEXATIONS
 9   Several listed projects are in areas that are likely to be annexed before county financing is available and would then

12   CONCURRENCY MANAGEMENT
13   The county modified the transportation concurrency program in 2014 to better protect against the unplanned use of
14   newly created roadway capacity, while allowing new developments to be permitted with predictability. In addition,
15   the revised program sets concurrency standards at a level that is consistent with the 20-Year Comprehensive Plan,
16   population and employment forecast, the Capital Facilities Plan and capital funding projections.
