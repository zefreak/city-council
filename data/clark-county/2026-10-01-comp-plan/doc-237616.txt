1    CHAPTER 2
2    Housing Element
3    Introduction
 4   The purpose of the Housing Element is to identify the need for and mechanisms that will lead to
 5   the construction and preservation of decent housing for all economic segments of the Clark
 6   County population. Regionallywide in orientation, the Housing Element addresses needs
 7   throughoutall of Clark County. It sets policy direction for lands under county government
 8   jurisdiction, is coordinated to the greatest extent possible with housing policies developed by
 9   cities and towns within greater Clark County, and provides practical implementation guidance.
10   The need for mechanisms to ensure a variety of housing prices and neighborhood designs is
11   discussed as well as the types of housing that should be available in the future.

12   Relationship to other elements and plans
13   The Housing Element of the 20-Year Plan builds upon principles and policies established in earlier
14   county comprehensive plans. Earlier plans discussed housing primarily in light of its land use
15   implications. This plan addresses housing in broader terms, reaching beyond land use patterns and
16   densities to discuss issues such as affordability, special needs, and community character, and
17   racially disparate impacts and displacement.
18
19   The Housing Element also builds upon principles and policy direction provided by the Countywide
20   Planning Policies and the Community Framework Plan. These policies, developed through an
21   extensive public participation process, are intended to provide long-term, overall guidance for
22   Clark County and its cities in developing the Housing Element for the 20-Year Plan.
23
24   RCW 36.70A.070 outlines GMA climate change and resiliency requirements. Chapter 14 –
25   Climate Element contains most climate change and resilience policies in the Comprehensive Plan,
26   but other elements also include climate policies. Climate-related goals and policies within this
27   element are noted in the goals and policies section of this chapter. See Chapter 14 for a
28   comprehensive discussion of climate policies, some of which overlap with housing.

 5   Organization of the element
 6   The Housing Element consists of three sections: the Background and Existing Conditions section
 7   including statistics supporting the county's Housing Element. It summarizes existing conditions
 8   and information in Clark County and focuses on inventory data, which support the policy
 9   orientation on growth management; 2) the Goals and Policies section, on an issue by issue basis,
10   presents a comprehensive set of goals and policies to guide the implementation of the plan; and 3)
11   the Strategy section consists of a set of planning strategies related to housing in Clark County. See
12   the Consolidated PlanHCD for additional information on county housing issues.

13   Background and existing conditions
14   The housing needs of Clark County are determined by the characteristics of its existing and
15   projected population (age, household size, income, special needs, etc.), when compared to the
16   characteristics of the existing and expected housing supply (size, cost, condition, etc.). Clark
17   County is expected to add approximately 128, 586190,754 people or 48,340103,698 households
18   over the next twenty years. The issue facing local governments is where to direct this growth
19   given environmental constraints and the cost of providing public services and how to ensure that a
20   range of housing types and prices are available.
21
22   Much of the data contained in this section comes from the HCDConsolidated Plan prepared by
23   Clark County Department of Community Services, and the Housing Inventory and Analysis: Clark
24   County Unincorporated Vancouver Urban Growth Area which was issued in March 2021 in
25   conjunction with the Housing Options Study and Action Plan.
26
27   The HCDConsolidated Plan is required under the Cranston-Gonzalez National Affordable
28   Housing Act of 1990. All jurisdictions eligible for funding under this act and wishing to participate
29   in the program are required to prepare a plan identifying the different types of housing needed in
30   the community and setting priorities for addressing them.
31
32   This report also contains data from the U.S. Bureau of the Census for 1990, 2000 and 2010the
33   years 2016 – 2020, as provided . Between the 2000 and 2010 census, data was being collected
34   through the American Community Survey (ACS). Some of the data previously collected in the
35   decennial census was no longer being asked in the long form decennial census and was only
36   available through the ACS. This makes comparison to previous decennial census information a
37   challenge. The ACS data provided in this report is primarily of the 5-year survey which is more
38   reliable than the 1-year or 3-year survey. In addition to the Consolidated Plan and the ACS, data
39   from the Housing Inventory and Analysis (HIA) report as contained within the larger Housing
40   Options Study and Action Plan is also referenced herein. The HIA’s purpose is to summarize
41   quantitative analysis and qualitative information collected through stakeholder interviews to
42   paint a picture of current housing issues in the unincorporated portion of the Study Area. The
43   findings in this report provide a coherent analysis of housing supply, demand, needs, and
44   preferences throughout the Study Area to provide context for evaluating potential actions.

5    MARKET AND INSTITUTIONAL INFLUENCES ON HOUSING
6    PRODUCTION
7    Typical of most communities in the Unites States, the primary influences on housing price in the
8    county include, but are not limited to:

 9      •   land use controls which limit both the areas where housing may be built and the density of
10          development, with a resulting impact on land cost and development costs;
11      •   building code requirements (such as those related to the Americans with Disabilities Act;
12          energy efficiency, etc.) which may increase construction costs and ultimately increase
13          housing price;
14      •   off-site improvement requirements;
15      •   finance costs such as interest rates, other loan costs;
16      •   tax limitations measures that limit the public sector’s ability to subsidize housing
17          development;
18      •   materials and construction costs; and,
19      •   in-migration and mismatches in housing supply and demand.
20
21   Most notably, the construction costs and home purchase prices rise with interest rates. Since the
22   large number of savings and loan failures in the late 1980s, federal regulators have reduced the
23   percentage of an institution's portfolio, which can be in real estate development. This has resulted
24   in making financing of residential development more difficult. Similarly, increases in land costs or
25   construction costs will increase the cost of the housing which is developed unless more units can
26   be built on the same site. Rising energy costs increase the costs of construction and maintenance
27   of housing units; however, conservation measures can reduce lifecycle costs for energy.

28   THE HOUSING NEEDS CONTINUUM IN CLARK COUNTY
29   Housing affordability issues impact all households in all income groups. Every household has an
30   income at one level or another and must find housing that meets but does not exceed the
31   requirements of thethat income level. Sometimes this relationship is called "attainability."
32   Households at higher incomes have fewer housing affordability problems, largely because their
33   incomes allow greater flexibility to access housing at, or less than, their incomes. In addition, there
34   are generally more housing units available within their income ranges. Persons with lower
35   incomes have more housing affordability problems partially because their ability to access housing
36   in their target price range is limited by persons from higher ranges "buying down," and by limited
37   numbers of units. In addition, the lower the income range, the less potential the household has for
38   "buying down".

41   officials and ordinary citizensresidents in different ways. Lenders generally claim that affordable
42   housing is housing expenditure at or below 30 percent of household income. A household earning

35   FAIR HOUSING
36   The goal of fair housing is to encourage freedom of choice in the sale or rental of dwellings. Fair
37   housing rights are established both through federal law (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968)
38   and Washington State legislation (WAC 49.60.222 through 224). The private sector and public
39   sector housing agencies are very familiar with these principles as they apply to buyer/seller or
40   landlord/tenant relationships. Discrimination based on race, color, age, sex, religion and national
41   origin is prohibited.
42
43   In 1988, the Federal Fair Housing Act was amended in a manner that makes it evident that it is not
44   legal to deny persons with disabilities the opportunity to live in a community. The amendment also
45   makes it clear that persons may not be discriminated against on the basis of family status. The
46   Washington Housing Policy Act, adopted in 1993, reinforces these principles by prohibiting local
47   ordinances that treat households with disabilities differently from other households. For the
                                                       24
 1   purpose of this 20-Year plan, written in 1993 and updated in 2002, low-income persons are not
 2   considered a group protected under fair housing laws. If applied, income tests must be applied to
 3   all groups (e.g., disabled, racial, national origin) equally.
 4
 5   Several of the groups specifically noted in fair housing laws are commonly referred to as "special
 6   populations." Special populations include the physically disabled, mentally disabled, mentally ill,
 7   homeless and other persons who may experience barriers to housing because of a disability or
 8   condition. Special needs populations are among the most noticeable persons needing fair housing
 9   protection. Fair housing, however, is a broader concept that attempts to protect all
10   citizensresidents from unfair or discriminatory treatment.
11
12   In the development of land use regulations, communities must examine whether the effect of a
13   regulation, action or policy is exclusionary. Local land use policies, regulations and actions must
14   not have the effect of excluding individuals from Clark County or cities within Clark County.
15   Persons should be able to find a variety of housing opportunities.
16
17   The 20-Year Plan proposes that an essential element in the continued achievement of fair housing
18   is a land use regulatory approach that allows anyone seeking housing to take "managed risks".
19   That is, regulations should protect public health and safety, but not to the point that the
20   regulations have the effect of excluding populations from finding housing that they can afford. Fair
21   housing should not become a paternalistic approach to protection that eventually excludes the
22   disabled, elderly, or other individuals. The 20-Year Plan also works toward fair housing by using
23   the household, rather than the family, as the basic definition for an assemblage of persons in a
24   dwelling unit.
25
26   Household is a broader term that allows for non-nuclear families, unrelated individuals, domestic
27   partnerships, caregivers and other arrangements. A household orientation reflects the increasing
28   diversity of living arrangements in the county.
29
30   Incentives for fair housing and a greater awareness of how the principle serves to protect all
31   persons will be increasingly necessary in the future as Clark County's population grows and
32   diversifies. Fair housing requires the attention of many segments of the community. Appropriate
33   land use practices are a necessary step. These practices must be reinforced by fair lending
34   practices, underwriting standards, appraisals, bonding and by other implementation policies and
35   procedures that effectuate, on a daily basis, principles of fairness. Central to fairness is a clear
36   understanding of both the income characteristics of the community and the characteristics of
37   housing. Over time, the county's racial structure, household living arrangements, number of
38   special needs persons, etc. will change. There is a continuing need to educate government officials
39   and citizensresidents to their individual rights and to the rights of others.

29   NEIGHBORHOOD CHARACTER AND VITALITY
30   Clark County's residential neighborhoods vary in size, density, housing type and amenities. The
31   character of a neighborhood, both its livability and identity, is closely associated with its design,
32   the characteristics of the residents and the services provided. Regardless of the character of the
33   neighborhood, residents generally want a feeling of comfort and security, privacy and a sense of
34   belonging. Neighborhood character is an important element of the Community Framework Plan and

43   physical abilities has the potential to create strong and vital neighborhoods. The contribution of

 6   INFILL
 7   In order to achieve the goals of the 20-Year Plan, Clark County and other jurisdictions must
 8   encourage the use of infill parcels for homes and also must ensure that development is compatible
 9   with the surrounding neighborhood. Infill is a term used to describe development of parcels that
10   was "passed over" in a first phase of development. Some lots in the urban area were not developed
11   because they continued in rural uses such as horse lots, orchards, etc. In some cases, there was
12   insufficient demand for the land or people chose not to develop right away. The physical
13   development constraints of some parcels, such as drainage ways, steep slopes, etc. may also lead
14   to them being "passed over." The parcels are now surrounded by development, which may be
15   residential, commercial or industrial in nature. In some areas, infill will mean mixing housing with
16   commercial development and may require special consideration of physical constraints, existing
17   infrastructure and adjacent land use.
18
19   Infill development is central to achieving target densities and to reducing sprawl. Targeted infill
20   development sites can also serve to focus public investment in areas, which have existing urban
21   development but need additional infrastructure to support increased densities. This type of infill
22   development could include co-locating employment centers with housing, thereby reducing
23   vehicle miles traveled, lessening congestion and reducing the overall costs for infrastructure.

25   ACCESSORY UNITS
26   Accessory units are another method for increasing density in a manner that may be affordable.
27   Accessory housing units are complete living quarters constructed within an existing single family
28   lot. They occur through conversion of an attic, a basement, a garage or other space. They are
29   always secondary in size to the existing dwelling, usually less than 1,000 square feet. Common
30   names for these units include granny flats, mother-in-law apartments and bachelor units. Some
31   communities allow accessory units to be free standing. Freestanding units are generally called
32   echo units or accessory cottages. Accessory units combine the advantages of small size,
33   maximizing use of existing dwellings and income for homeowners as advantages. Clark County has
34   had an Accessory Dwelling Unit ordinance in effect since 1992.

29   REHABILITATION AND PRESERVATION
30   The Framework Plan concept, with its emphasis on a hierarchy of identifiable places, supports the
31   preservation and rehabilitation of existing structures. Because existing structures provide much
32   of the character of places, their preservation into the future will provide a basis for definition of
33   community character. Existing structures also provide an opportunity for increased residential
34   density with minimal community disruption when accessory units are allowed within structures
35   and on existing lots. In addition, accessory units and existing houses are often among the most
36   affordable units in the real estate market. Rehabilitation of existing structures also reflects an
37   environmentally conscious approach to neighborhoods with an orientation toward stewardship
38   and reuse of existing resources.
39
40   The Community Framework Plan, with its emphasis on increased density in urban areas also
41   acknowledges that over time existing structures may be replaced with higher density structures,
42   mixed uses or other innovations in land use. In concept, the removal of a housing unit in residential
43   areas either through demolition or conversion to another use should be accompanied by the
44   replacement of a residential use in the same neighborhood district. The intent of this plan is to

                                                      28
 1   place a priority upon rehabilitation and preservation of structures, while acknowledging that over
 2   time not all structures can or should be retained. In every case, all costs of rehabilitation, including
 3   life cycle costs and potential tax credits, must be considered.
 4
 5   Housing rehabilitation must be integrated with a concern for the persons in a structure and must
 6   respond to their social and service needs. Rehabilitated strategies must be tailored to the
 7   character of the area served. Rehabilitated buildings should be safe and habitable, but should not
 8   be required to meet the same codes as new construction.
 9
10   Rehabilitation strategies specifically tailored to the condition of the neighborhood, integrating
11   physical, demographic and economic needs provide an opportunity to re-use existing housing
12   stock. Not only is this wise conservation of natural, human and physical/cultural resources, it also
13   preserves the units most likely to be affordable to low and moderate income persons. As a
14   technique to provide affordability, rehabilitation cannot be overlooked or underappreciated.
15
16   It is the intent of this Chapter to advocate for safe and habitable housing for all Clark County
17   residents. In order to accomplish this aim and also to preserve affordability, it is essential that
18   building codes allow a tiered approach to acceptable building condition. The cost of rehabilitating
19   structures to the same standards as new construction often is prohibitive, dissuades owners from
20   making improvements and increases the cost of dwellings. Provision also should be made for

28   AFFORDABILITY
29   The concern for housing affordability is a nationwide issue. Much of Clark County's growth in the
30   last 20 years can be attributed to its affordability compared to the surrounding region. Clark
31   County and city officials see maintenance of affordability into the future as an important
32   objective. The 20-Year Plan, as a government document, provides an opportunity to focus on the
33   leadership role that local government can take to work cooperatively with all segments of the
34   community in order to increase affordability within the context of protecting public health, safety
35   and welfare. Provision of affordable housing for the individual should not come about at the cost
36   of the community as a whole. The interests of the community as a whole, however, include the
37   need to provide housing which is affordable for individuals.
38
39   Consumer expectations also play an important role in affordability. Consumers in all income
40   ranges exhibit a trend toward increasing expectations for size and amenities. These add to the
41   cost of housing. For most consumers, a house is their single most significant financial investment.
42   Houses are more than a place to live, they impose a significant financial responsibility upon
43   owners and offer and important windfall profit opportunity. In the 1980s and 1990s, homeowners
44   have become increasingly protective of the value, both real and perceived, of their homes. This is
45   often exhibited as NIMBYism (Not In My Back Yard) where property owners strenuously object to
46   the introduction of new housing that differs from existing housing in type or value. Many of these

                                                       29
 1   objections are based in the fear of people of differing incomes, race, age, or ethnicity and their
 2   perceived impacts on the value of property. NIMBYism influences housing affordability and it
 3   results in excessive delays in permit review processes or effectively excludes legitimate housing
 4   types or income groups from neighborhoods. In the planning process, this intolerance must not be
 5   underestimated but must be recognized and planned for in education programs, public hearing
 6   processes and in programs that attempt to create a sense of community that extends beyond the
 7   financial commitment of a house purchase.
 8
 9   Just as supply and demand interact to influence cost, housing price and local wages interact to
10   influence affordability. A dual effort to increase local wages while also retaining moderately priced
11   housing, will keep housing affordable to more of the population. Housing affordability is a
12   relationship between an individual's income and the price of housing.
13
14   From the perspective of community planning, it is desirable to provide both jobs and housing
15   within a community for the benefit of individuals and the community tax base. The relationship of
16   jobs and housing is described in two ways:

17      •   the wage/housing balance is the relationship between the wages earned by people in the
18          community and the housing price. Ideally, there is a sufficient number of housing units
19          affordable to all levels of wage earners and
20      •   the jobs/housing balance is the relationship between the location of jobs and the location
21          of housing. Ideally, jobs are created in locations that are convenient to housing.

22   In both relationships, the planning objective is to create opportunities so people who want to live
23   close to work may do so. There is no requirement (or assurance) that the people working a
24   particular job will live in the proximal housing, or vice versa. The two principles may be applied
25   separately or together when looking at a community's affordability strategy. The intent of the
26   Housing Element is to assure that communities investigate both relationships and attempt to
27   achieve both a jobs/housing and wage/housing balance within their urban area.
28
29   Traditionally, planners have looked at a jobs/housing balance and have tried to promote housing
30   opportunities in locations close to the workplace. This helps community diversity and reduces
31   commute trips. Now with the interest in affordability, communities are also looking at
32   wage/housing balances, trying to promote availability of housing that workers can afford close to
33   their jobs.

41   INCLUSIONARY ZONING
42   An innovative tool to provide affordable housing is a voluntary inclusionary zoning program. A
43   voluntary inclusionary program would be based on incentives for developers to build a percentage
44   (usually 10 - 15%) of housing in their subdivision as affordable. The units would be smaller, simpler
45   homes in a compatible design with the rest of the subdivision. In exchange for the affordable units,
                                                      30
 1   developers would be given incentives that would make it profitable for them, such as density
 2   incentives, expedited review, or impact fee waivers.
 3
 4   Inclusionary zoning does several things. It can integrate low and moderate income housing units
 5   into market rate subdivisions and avoid concentrations of low-income housing into one
 6   neighborhood. Increased density in some neighborhoods can encourage the viability of transit.
 7   Employers located in suburban communities benefit as well by having an accessible low and
 8   moderate-income workforce.
 9
10   Neighborhood character is important to preserve and inclusionary zoning allows this to happen by
11   giving the design control to the developer. Unlike infill, the affordable units are part of the
12   subdivision plan and can be designed to be similar to their surrounding homes. This allows a
13   community to retain its character while accommodating affordable housing. Inclusionary zoning
14   can be an important tool to provide affordable housing to the growing number of households in
15   Clark County that are priced out of the market. Homeownership prices have been escalating in the
16   county, which has priced many working families out of homeownership.
17
18   Inclusionary zoning can provide homeownership opportunities for those families by making it
19   profitable for developers to build homes that those families can afford. Inclusionary zoning,
20   through partnerships with non-profits and public agencies, can also provide affordable rental
21   opportunities in new subdivisions.
22
23   Several other tools are available to the public sector to help influence the availability of affordable
24   housing, such as publicly subsidized rental housing and community land trusts to foster a
25   permanent inventory of affordable housing. Clark County has invested in the Southwest
26   Washington Community Land Trust.

28   FINANCING AFFORDABLE HOUSING
29   Finance of housing and in particular affordable housing is a specialized market niche that requires
30   the cooperation of land developers, builders, government and lenders. Finance plays a vital role in
31   the final cost of housing and its associated infrastructure. The intent of this plan is to both identify
32   and advocate for finance mechanisms for housing and associated infrastructure that are stable.
33   Both housing and infrastructure improvements are long-term investments. Mechanisms that are
34   predictable over time may stabilize risk and increase the potential for project funding. This does
35   not imply that new finance mechanisms and institutional structures will not or should not arise
36   over time, or that interim finance mechanisms are not appropriate. Rather a long-term view of
37   finance mechanisms is necessary. In the last twenty years, the nation's financial institutions,
38   lending systems and local taxing mechanisms have undergone radical change. More change should
39   be expected in the next twenty years.
40
41   Another important component of this plan is the recognition that the public, not-for- profit and
42   private finance sectors all play an important role in housing finance. A healthy and complete
43   housing finance system will involve the participation of all three sectors in a manner that most
44   appropriately reflects public purpose, capital requirements, costs, interest rates and other
45   influences on the financial markets. Public sector financing of housing is traditionally identified
46   with housing for the lowest income groups and involves the deepest direct subsidies. The public

                                                       31
 1   sector is also involved in middle and high-income subsidies to housing through tax policies. The
 2   public sector's role is changing as the need for partnership approaches to finance emerges.
 3
 4   The not-for-profit sector is an emerging finance sector. Working with private sector partners, the
 5   nonprofit sector can access public funds in order to meet a public purpose while the private sector
 6   partner can offer the deep financial backing required to develop housing. The Vancouver Housing
 7   Authority has partnered in this way to develop eleven properties that use bond financing to
 8   develop affordable housing for households at or below 80% of area median income. Two of the
 9   developments include tax credit equity, allowing the rents to be affordable to households at or
10   below 60% of area median income. These properties will remain a community asset to be used for
11   future affordable housing needs.
12
13   Private sector finance is the mainstay of housing development. Increasingly, in order to meet the
14   needs of low and moderate income persons, the private finance institutions need the assistance of
15   the public and not-for-profit sector. The private sector also has responsibilities to invest in
16   communities through the Community Reinvestment Act. CRA goals often give impetus both to
17   partnerships with the other sectors and to innovative financing techniques.

19   HOUSING TYPES, HOUSING TENURE, SUFFICIENT LAND
20   AND ALL INCOME GROUPS
21   The Growth Management Act is clear in its direction that comprehensive plans are to provide
22   sufficient land and opportunities for a variety of housing types, ranging from site built to offsite
23   manufactured. It is clear in its direction that special needs housing must be accommodated within
24   the community. It is also clear that the housing and land use elements of local plans must be
25   structured in a manner that makes it possible for persons of all income groups to have a degree of
26   choice in their geographic search for housing. Sufficient land must be available for housing so that
27   all income groups can exercise a choice to live in a community.
28
29   The Land Use Element, Chapter 1 of the 20-Year Plan provides, in both policy and mapped form, is
30   a vision of the location of land uses in the future. The Land Use Element contains areas planned for
31   residential, commercial, industrial, forest, agricultural, recreation and other land uses. The
32   residential plan identifies areas for single and multiple family uses at a variety of densities. It
33   includes mixed-use areas where combinations of commercial, institutional and residential uses are
34   allowed. It also includes agricultural and forest areas where residential uses are allowed at a low
35   density. The Land Use Element specifies target densities for the uses.
