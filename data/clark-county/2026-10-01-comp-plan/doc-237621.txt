3    Introduction
4    This Transportation Element (TE) is prepared in accordance with the GMA. Contained within the
5    TE are projects and implementation measures necessary to effectively serve planned land use
6    throughout unincorporated Clark County. Importantly, this element provides guidance for the de-
7    sign, construction and operation of transportation facilities and services through the year 2035.

8    Purpose and Background

 9   The purpose of the TE is to present a plan for transportation facilities and services needed to sup-
10   port the county’s 2015-2035 future land use map. The TE recommends specific arterial roadway
11   projects for the unincorporated county in order to meet roadway safety and capacity needs. How-
12   ever, it The Transportation Element of the Clark County Comprehensive Growth Management
13   Plan 2025-2045 (20-Year Plan) provides the community guiding framework for transportation in-
14   vestment and priorities over the next 20 years. The Growth Management Act (GMA) requires that
15   the Transportation Element be prepared to implement, and be consistent with, the Land Use Ele-
16   ment. The Transportation Element helps to ensure that Clark County will be able to accommodate
17   increased population growth and changes to land use over time, while it also aims to promote
18   healthy, economically vibrant and livable communities through the development of a safe, accessi-
19   ble and efficient multimodal transportation system that is not dependent on vehicles to move peo-
20   ple throughout the county.

11   Description of Historical Growth and Development
12   Clark County’s population was estimated at 448,500 in 2015 making it the 5th most populous
13   county in Washington State. The county was the fastest-growing in the state in the 1990s and was
14   second-fastest over the past decade. Growth was spurred by in-migration of new residents, alt-
15   hough in 2010, more people moved out of the county than moved in for the first time since 1984.
16   Even with this decline of in-migration between 2000 and 2010 Clark County still experienced a
17   28.3% increase in population from 2000 to 2010 which is above the state increase of 18.2%.

18   The Washington State Office of Financial Management (OFM) provides counties and cities in the
19   State of Washington with county-level growth forecasts to accommodate their planning processes
20   under GMA. OFM’s 2012 GMA population projections have a high, medium and low growth series
21   for each county. The projections of 2035 total population for Clark County are:

22       • High – 681,135
23       • Medium – 562,207
24       • Low – 459,617
25   OFM considers the medium series to be the most likely projection. The Clark County Council used
26   the medium series — 2035 countywide population of 562,207 — when adopting 2035 initial popu-
27   lation targets. The land use assumptions used to estimate future travel demand for this Transpor-
28   tation Element use a 2035 forecast of 577,431 for countywide population.

29   The Clark County Council adopted a 2035 initial employment target for Clark County of 91,200
30   jobs. The land use assumptions used to estimate future travel demand for this Transportation Ele-
31   ment use a 2035 forecast of 232,500 for countywide employment. Much of the employment
32   growth is expected to occur within the incorporated cities; however, the county will plan for its
33   share of job growth that will occur in unincorporated UGAs.

14   AIR TRANSPORTATION
15   An important example of an economic benefit that can be derived from airports is the ability to at-
16   tract compatible land use developments (e.g., commercial or industrial) on or near airport prop-
17   erty. In many instances, land immediately on or adjacent to an airport is flat, easily developed and
18   relatively inexpensive when compared to more centrally located business district sites.

19   One of the several requirements of the GMA is that the comprehensive plan of each jurisdiction
20   should include a process for identifying and siting essential public facilities, including airports and
21   state and regional transportation facilities, per RCW 36.70A.200.

22   Local
23   Publicly-owned general aviation airfields within Clark County include Pearson Field and Grove
24   Field. Pearson Field, located east of downtown Vancouver off SR-14, is operated by the city of
25   Vancouver and covers 134 acres owned by the National Park Service. Pearson is designated as a
26   part of the regional transportation system. Grove Field is a Basic Utility Stage I Airport operated
27   by the Port of Camas-Washougal. Located in the Ferm Prairie area 5 miles north of downtown
28   Camas, it has a 2,832-foot paved illuminated runway, an above-ground self-fueling station and
29   hanger space for over 70 aircraft. In addition, Clark County is home to several private airfields.

30   Regional
31   Air passenger transportation service for Clark County is primarily provided by the Portland Inter-
32   national Airport (PDX), located in Portland, Oregon, to the southwest of the I-205 Glenn Jackson
33   Bridge. This is a regional airport with domestic and international passenger and freight (cargo) ser-
34   vice. The sixty-six passenger airlines serving PDX include Air Canada, Alaska Airlines, America
35   West, American, Condor, Delta, Frontier, Hawaiian, Icelandair, Jet Blue, Pen Air, Sea Port Airlines,
                                                         9

19   Port of Vancouver USA
20   The Port of Vancouver USA is situated at the terminus of the Columbia River’s deep draft channel
21   and forms a natural gateway to the river-barge ports of eastern Oregon/Washington and northern
22   Idaho. The Port operates international cargo docks. It is the third-largest port in the state of
23   Washington. It has five marine freight terminals, provides 13 deep-draft vessel berths and has two
24   140-metric ton mobile harbor cranes to enable heavy lift cargo.

25   The Port is located within 2 miles of I-5 and is also served by the Burlington Northern Santa Fe and
26   Union Pacific Railroads, the Canadian National and the Canadian Pacific railroads. The Port has
27   800 acres of developed industrial and marine property with over 50 industrial tenants. Over 2,300
28   people are directly employed by these businesses and nearly 17,000 jobs are connected to port
29   activities. The Port has over 500 additional acres of land for future development. Work began in
30   2004 on the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) process for this additional land’s develop-

32   velopment includes the Columbia Gateway area. The Port focused attention on rail access im-

34   West Vancouver Freight Access Project.

2    Active Transportation
 3   Active transportation refers to any form of human-powered transportation, including people
 4   walking, using mobility devices, or riding bikes. Active transportation modes include wheelchairs,
 5   assisted mobility devices, two-wheel bikes, cargo bikes, e-bikes, inline skates, scooters, and skate-
 6   boards, among others. In addition to providing a low-cost and accessible form of transportation,
 7   active transportation facilities offer many additional benefits to communities that aim to create an
 8   environment where these forms of transportation are convenient, safe and normal. Active Trans-
 9   portation Facilities are defined in RCW 36.70A.030 as “facilities provided for the safety and mo-
10   bility of active transportation users including, but not limited to, trails as defined in RCW
11   47.30.005, sidewalks, bike lanes, shared-use paths, and other facilities in the public right-of-way.”

16   The following provides a brief description of each of Clark County’s roadway Comprehensive Plan
17   designation:

18   Urban Parkway Arterial

19   The Padden Parkway is the only road with this, the highest classification within the county’s func-
20   tional system. Their purpose of a parkway arterial is to move high volumes of relatively long- dis-
21   tance traffic speedily across the county or region. Direct land access is prohibited or minimal and
22   then only to major activity centers of regional impact. The level of fixed route transit service is
23   high; b Bicycle and pedestrian activity is on a parallel trail separated facility.

24   Urban Principal Arterials

25   Urban principal arterials (such as NE 78th Street or NE Fourth Plain Road) permit traffic flow
26   through the urban area and between major elements of the urban area. Principal arterials connect
27   major traffic generators to other major activity centers and carry a high proportion of the total ur-
28   ban area travel on a minimum of roadway mileage. They move high volumes of traffic speedily

                                                         24
1    across the county or region, but with volumes and speeds below those of the principal arterial
2    parkway classification. Access is generally limited to intersections with other arterials and collec-
3    tors. Direct land access is minimal and controlled, but less restrictive as compared to principal ar-
4    terial parkway. Frequently principal arterials carry important intra-urban as well as intercity bus
5    routes.

6    Urban Minor Arterials

 7   Urban minor arterials (such as Hazel Dell Avenue or NE 99th Street) collect and distribute traffic
 8   from principal arterials to streets of lower classifications or allow for traffic to directly access des-
 9   tinations. Access to land use activities is generally permitted. They are primarily designed to ac-
10   commodate through-traffic but may provide direct access for more intensely developed proper-
11   ties. Fixed route transit, bicycle and pedestrian activity is moderate.

12   Urban Collectors

13   Urban collectors (such as NE 88th Street) provide for land access and traffic circulation within and
14   between residential neighborhoods and commercial and industrial areas. Collectors do not handle
15   long through-trips and are not continuous for any great length. Fixed route transit service is low
16   while bicycling and pedestrian activity ranges from moderate to high.

17   Urban Local Streets

18   Most of the county road network is comprised of local streets. Urban local streets provide direct
19   access to abutting land and access to the higher classification facilities. They offer the lowest level
20   of mobility and usually contain no bus routes. They are not intended to carry through traffic but
21   make up a large percentage of the total street mileage. New local streets are typically provided as
22   part of development projects.

23   Rural Major Collectors

24   Rural major collectors (e.g. NW 31st Avenue and NE 72nd Avenue) are usually extensions of urban
25   principal arterials and some urban minor arterials into the rural area. Their primary purpose is to
26   link rural activity centers with larger towns nearby and to connect them to state arterial routes.

27   Mostly, they serve intra-county travel. Land access remains subordinate to traffic movement. The
28   level of fixed route transit, bicycle and pedestrian activity is low.

29   Rural Minor Collectors

30   Rural minor collectors (e.g. NE Davis Road and NE Kelly Road) are rural extensions of urban collec-
31   tors and some urban minor arterials. They connect rural areas to major collectors and state routes.

20   The Growth Management Act (GMA) requires local jurisdictions to establish Multimodal Level of
21   Service (MMLOS) standards for all locally owned arterials, locally and regionally operated transit
22   routes that serve Urban Growth Areas (UGAs), state owned or operated transit routes that serve
23   urban areas, if applicable, and active transportation facilities. MMLOS standards represent the
24   minimum desirable performance level desired for transportation facilities and services within the



                                                             34
1    region. They are used and serve as a gauge for evaluating to judge the quality performance of ser-
2    vice on the overall transportation system. These standards are used to identify deficient facilities
3    and services in the transportation system for capital planning and concurrency management.

4    The forecast needs for county-owned capital facilities are based on the MMLOS selected and con-
5    sistent with the growth, densities and distribution of growth anticipated in the Land Use Element.
6    The forecast should include reasonable assumptions about the effect of any identified system
7    management or demand management approaches to preserve capacity or avoid the need for new
8    facilities.

 9   The GMA defines concurrency as the requirement that adequate transportation infrastructure is
10   provided “concurrent with development” to accommodate new growth and offset the impact of
11   development. This concept allows coordination between the timing of development projects and
12   transportation improvements such as new streets and intersection improvements. Clark County
13   and each municipal government therein maintain concurrency programs. Such programs consist of
14   two key components: a Comprehensive Plan which establishes transportation MMLOS standards,
15   and an ordinance which defines the implementation of concurrency requirements.

24   The GMA states that "level-of-service MMLOS standards shall be established for all locally owned
25   arterials, and locally and regionally operated transit routes, and active transportation facilities to
26   serve as a gauge to judge the performance of the system. The GMA directs that these standards
27   should be established locally and coordinated regionally for local arterials and for highways of re-
28   gional significance. The standards are used to identify deficient facilities and services in the exist-
29   ing transportation system. Highways of statewide significance (RCW 47.06.140) have a level-of-
30   service MMLOS standards set by the state.

31   MMLOS Level-of-Service standards can be based on performance along a segment of a roadway
32   or at an intersection. The Highway Capacity Manual includes different level-of-service MMLOS
33   definitions and descriptions of operating characteristics for freeways, highways, urban streets and
34   signalized intersections, because driver expectations and the measures of effective performance
35   are different for each type of facility.
36

                                                       36
1    For freeways and highways, LOS is described in terms of the relationship of actual travel speeds to
2    free-flow speeds, the freedom to maneuver within the traffic stream and the effects of minor inci-
3    dents or breakdowns on the traffic stream. The descriptions of each LOS for highways are listed
4    below for illustrative purposes.

5       Level-of-Service A: describes completely free flow conditions. The operation of vehicles is vir-
6       tually unaffected by the presence of other vehicles and operations are constrained only by the
7       geometric features of the highway and by driver preferences. Maneuverability within the traf-
8       fic stream is good. Minor disruptions to flow are easily absorbed without a change in travel
9       speed.

10      Level-of-Service B: also indicates free flow, although the presence of other vehicles becomes
11      noticeable. Average travel speeds are the same as in LOS A, but drivers have slightly less free-
12      dom to maneuver. Minor disruptions are still easily absorbed, although local deterioration in
13      LOS will be more obvious.

14      Level-of-Service C: the influence of traffic density on operations becomes marked. The ability
15      to maneuver within the traffic stream is clearly affected by other vehicles. On multilane high-
16      ways with a free flow speed above 50 miles per hour, the travel speeds reduce somewhat. Mi-
17      nor disruptions can cause serious local deterioration in service and queues will form behind
18      any significant traffic disruption.

19      Level-of-Service D: the ability to maneuver is severely restricted due to traffic congestion.
20      Travel speed is reduced by the increasing volume. Only minor disruptions can be absorbed
21      without extensive queues forming and the service deteriorating.

22      Level-of-Service E: represents operations at or near capacity, an unstable level. The densities
23      vary, depending on the free flow speed (FFS). Vehicles are operating with the minimum spac-
24      ing for maintaining uniform flow. Disruptions cannot be dissipated readily, often causing
25      queues to form and service to deteriorate to LOS F. For the majority of multilane highways
26      with free flow speeds between 45 and 60 miles per hour, passenger-car mean speeds at capac-
27      ity range from 42 to 55 miles per hour, but are highly variable and unpredictable.

28      Level-of-Service F: represents forced or breakdown flow. It occurs either when vehicles arrive
29      at a rate greater than the rate at which they are discharged or when the forecast demand ex-
30      ceeds the computed capacity of a planned facility. Although operations at these points – and
31      on sections immediately downstream – appear to be at capacity, queues form behind these
32      breakdowns. Operations within queues are highly unstable, with vehicles experiencing brief
33      periods followed by stoppages. Travel speeds within queues are generally less than 30 miles
34      per hour.

 7   The rise of work-from-home employment after the COVID-19 pandemic, including both fully re-
 8   mote and hybrid work arrangements, has significantly shifted commute travel patterns in Clark

30      4.   Unincorporated Clark Count (UGA)

 4   Roadway speed plays a role in how safe the transportation system is for all users based on
 5   WSDOT’s Level of Traffic Stress (LTS) level of service standard as shown in Figure 5.11: Active
 6   Transportation Level of Traffic Stress Measurement. Reducing roadway speeds can also reduce

 9   projected densities. Therefore, transportation infrastructure may be developed that meets the

12   Transportation Demand Management
13   Transportation Demand Management (TDM) is about reducing auto trips, shortening some trips
14   and eliminating others that are unnecessary. TDM is a strategy to maximize the existing transpor-
15   tation system’s efficiency. Common TDM strategies are transit use, carpooling, vanpooling, work-
16   ing flexible hours/compressed schedule, and remote work. TDM can also include getting users to
17   mode shift into walking or biking.

18   Vehicle Miles Traveled
19   Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT) is the measure of all the miles traveled by motor vehicles on the
20   transportation system. It is a helpful measure of system use and tailpipe greenhouse gas emis-
21   sions. Resources are directed to actions that help improve performance and reduce greenhouse
22   gas emissions.

23   Per capita VMT means the number of miles traveled using cars and light trucks in a calendar year
24   divided by the number of residents in Washington. The calculation of this value excludes vehicle
25   miles driven conveying freight.

26   HB 1181 (2023) encourages efficient multimodal transportation systems that will reduce green-
27   house gas emissions and per capita vehicle miles traveled. VMT reduction efforts are increased
28   through a number of funding programs such as the Commute Trip Reduction program, Complete
29   Streets program and Mitigation and Air Quality improvement program. The Washington State De-
30   partment of Commerce shall compile, maintain, and publish a summary of the per capital VMT an-
31   nually in the unincorporated portions of each county.

2    The term Transportation System Management (TSM) is applied to a wide range of transportation
3    system improvements that tend to have low or no capital cost but address impediments to effi-
4    cient operation of the transportation system. TSM measures can be applied on a spot or corridor
5    basis. Clark County currently employs TSM measures to gain additional operational capacity on
6    major arterial corridors. Active TSM measures in place include:

 7      •   corridor access management;
 8      •   channelization of traffic at intersections;
 9      •   traffic signal coordination; and,
10      •   Intelligent Transportation System (ITS).

11   One of the most effective TSM measures is a program to address inappropriate land use access to
12   arterial roadways. While new development is required to comply with the county transportation
13   standards (CCC Chapter 40.350), existing land use on county arterials may have permitted inap-
14   propriate access to those arterial roadways. The most efficacious approach to corridor-level ac-
15   cess management is to address access issues when arterial capacity is expanded.

16   Another TSM approach involves the identification of small capital improvements that can be
17   demonstrated to add significantly to the capacity of an arterial. For example, at an intersection
18   having a shared through and left-turn lane, the traffic signal must be timed to separate that ap-
19   proach from the approach facing it (to allow for free flow of the left-turning traffic). The necessity
20   of splitting that phase of the traffic signal timing creates an inefficiency, which could be removed if
21   a separate left-turn lane is constructed.

22   A third approach, which is most applicable to high-volume roadways (e.g., Interstates and parkway
23   arterials), is to provide incident management services in a single or series of corridors to address
24   traffic management during incidents (e.g., vehicle collisions, breakdowns) so that such incidents
25   are cleared quickly. Washington State Department of Transportation has a program to provide

30   RTC is the federally designated Metropolitan Planning Organization (MPO) for the Vancou-

32   cess (CMP), which is a systematic process for managing congestion that provides information on
33   transportation system performance and on alternative strategies to alleviate congestion and en-
34   hance the mobility of persons and goods.

35   Quality of life and economic prosperity in the region depends on efficient mobility for both people
36   and goods. There is recognition that strategic expansion of capacity is needed at key bottlenecks.
37   Capacity expansion should take place after lower cost efforts have been made to improve the op-

1    erations of existing facilities. The CMP is intended to identify and manage congestion using per-

3    The CMP is implemented as part of the overall metropolitan planning process.

 4   The Clark County region was designated as a Transportation Management Area under the federal
 5   Transportation Act, ISTEA, in 1991. The region is designated as a TMA because it has a population
 6   greater than 200,000. In addition to meeting all the specified metropolitan transportation plan-
 7   ning process requirements, MPOs representing Transportation Management Areas must meet
 8   additional requirements. In TMAs, the MPO must have a Congestion Management Process as de-
 9   scribed above. The CMP provides for the effective management of new and existing facilities

14   corridors, for evaluating alternatives to address congestion and for managing the performance of

2    CONCURRENCY REQUIREMENTS
 3   The concurrency requirement of the GMA mandates that local jurisdictions adopt and enforce or-
 4   dinances that prohibit development approval if the development causes the LOS on certain trans-
 5   portation facilities to decline below the standards adopted under the comprehensive plan, unless
 6   transportation improvements or strategies to accommodate impacts of the development are
 7   made concurrent with the development. Concurrently with development means that improve-
 8   ments or strategies are in place at the time of development, or that a financial commitment is in
 9   place to complete the improvements or strategies within six years. Clark County will meet these
10   requirements through implementation of the adopted concurrency ordinance.

11   Concurrency policies are applied to local arterials identified in the capital facilities plan. Highways
12   of statewide significance are exempt from local policies. The concurrency requirements of the
13   GMA closely match the State Environmental Policy Act (SEPA) short-term impact analysis re-
14   quirements as they both evaluate transportation impacts (namely the roadway, corridor and inter-
15   section LOS) at the year of opening of the development or a specified short-term analysis year. A
16   State Environmental Policy Act (SEPA) transportation impact analysis would specify a study area.
17   Concurrency requires an evaluation of area-wide impacts and specific mitigation of those impacts
18   concurrent with the development opening.

19   CONCURRENCY MANAGEMENT SYSTEM
20   The concurrency management system must address concurrency monitoring and concurrency
21   regulation for new development. The county and its cities are responsible for concurrency moni-
22   toring, and the project applicant is responsible for demonstrating concurrency of the proposed de-
23   velopment. The concurrency management system will include all designated corridors along iden-
24   tified arterials and their intersections on the regional system, except for facilities of statewide sig-
25   nificance or intersections with facilities of statewide significance. In addition, all intersections of
26   regional significance will also be subject to concurrency testing. Implementation of concurrency
27   monitoring in the county and with local jurisdictions consists of the following strategies:

9    Future Conditions

10   An adequate level of mobility should be maintained for freight and goods movement in Clark
11   County and in the Vancouver-Portland metropolitan area as a whole to sustain the economic ac-
12   tivity of the metropolitan region and the States of Washington and Oregon. As traffic congestion
13   continues to increase in more locations and for longer periods, the freight industry will experience
14   longer shipping schedules and delays. This will likely increase the cost of transporting the goods.
15   Of particular concern is the I-5 Bridge over the Columbia River, which is already operating at ca-
16   pacity. In addition, the long queues of traffic resulting from congestion on I-5 could block truck ac-

18   state and local levels of government will limit the amount of funding for roadway improvements
19   including those for upgrading pavement conditions on restricted truck routes. This will place more

9    TRANSPORTATION ELEMENT POLICIES
10   Transportation policies that seek to provide for the mobility of people and goods must consider
11   increases in travel demand caused by growth in population and employment. The transportation
12   system must be affordable and minimize environmental impacts to maintain the quality of life. The
13   Transportation Element policies encourage efficient multimodal transportation systems that will
14   reduce greenhouse gas emissions and per capita vehicle miles traveled. A safe, efficient transpor-
15   tation system can work to enhance economic development within a region in conjunction with
16   supportive land use plans.

17   Climate-related goals and policies within this element are notes as helping achieve greenhouse gas
18   reduction [GHG], resilience [RES], or both [GHG, RES]. Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT) reduction
19   policies are tracked in Appendix I. The Transportation Element policies are based on local and re-
20   gional priorities and coordinated with both county and city comprehensive plans.

21   The 20-Year Plan policies listed in this element directly follow the mandates of the GMA and the
22   Clark County Community Framework Plan, adopted by Clark County in May 1993 pursuant to
23   GMA.
