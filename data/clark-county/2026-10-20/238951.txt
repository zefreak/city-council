        BACKGROUND
        Clark County Public Works has partnered with the Cowlitz Indian Tribe (Tribe) to provide grant match/in-kind
        services (general staff reviews) for the Tribe’s delivery of fish passage where NE Boulder Creek Road crosses
        over Jones Creek in the upper Washougal River basin (approximately 9 miles north of the City of Camas). The
        Tribe has applied for and received a grant from the Washington State Recreation and Conservation Office
        (RCO) Salmon Recovery Funding Board to complete the design and engineering for this project.

        Between the original funding in 2021 and planned construction in 2027, costs have risen and the Tribe was
        able to obtain additional RCO funding to support rising costs in administrative, utility and construction. That
        increase has resulted in a roughly 27% rise in the County’s in-kind match. In addition, the original 2021
        agreement has been mutually restructured into the attached Memorandum of Cooperation. That new
        agreement was successfully signed by the Tribal Council recently by their Chairman, Bill Iyall.

        The memorandum establishes the relationship between the County and the Tribe and defines the
        responsibilities and obligations for the work. As part of the Tribe’s grant award the County will contribute
        $190,886 to the project largely towards the purchase of the bridge.

              1 INTRODUCTION

              Jones Creek is a perennial tributary to the Little Washougal River, which feeds into the
              Washougal River near the town of Camas, Washington (Figure 1). Two undersized road
              crossings have been identified by the Cowlitz Indian Tribe and Washington Department of
              Fish and Wildlife (WDFW) as partial barriers to fish passage; Site 1) Jones Creek at NE
              Boulder Creek Road, and Site 2) a tributary to Jones Creek which flows under NE Jones
              Creek Road. The City of Camas uses the NE Jones Creek road to access a seasonal water
              diversion dam operated from November 1st to May 14th. The Jones Creek Boulder Road
              culvert is a barrier to fish passage due to depth and velocity and the Jones Creek Tributary
              due to depth, outfall drop and velocity. The design objective is to create engineered
              drawings at each site and provide clearance at 100-year flood flow while matching natural
              channel conditions. Both sites contain populations of coho salmon, steelhead, cutthroat and
              resident trout.

              5 HYDRAULICS
            A HEC RAS 1D model was developed for both sites. The existing conditions model was
            developed without the culvert crossings. Both sites have undersized culverts which are being
            replaced, so the existing conditions model was developed assuming the culverts were not in
            place. For the proposed conditions the bridges with channel dimensions and channel
            modifications were included.

            Jones Creek

            For the existing conditions the depth, velocity, top width, flow area and shear stress are
            summarized in Figure 4 for the 2-year and 100-year flood. Average water depth and velocity
            for the 100-year flood is 4.3 feet and 6.6 fps respectively. The shear stress provides an indication
            of the scour potential of the existing bed at each site. The shear stress average value at the 100-
            year flood is 1.6 lbs./sq ft. Using a relationship between grain diameter and shear stress for
            entrainment (Shields), a grain size of 5 inches is estimated for entrainment. From Figure 4, a

              6 SCOUR ANALYSIS
            Methodology for the scour analysis comes from U.S. Department of Transportation Federal
            Highway Administration Hydraulic Engineering Circular No. 18, Evaluating Scour at Bridges
            (HEC No. 18), and Circular No. 20, Stream Stability at Highway Structures (HEC No. 20). From
            Table 2.1 (HEC No. 18) the minimum scour design flood frequency is the 100-year flood. The
            estimated 100-year flood is 903 cfs. Flow, depth and velocity were all modeled using a HEC
            RAS 1D Model.


            Jones Creek

            From Figure 6, the proposed conditions show the water surface top width and velocity
            upstream of the bridge being equal to or less than the conditions under the bridge (i.e. no
            contraction scour). Two types of scour need to be assessed for a bridge placement 1) contraction
            scour and 2) long term scour. Contraction scour occurs when the flow area of the stream at
            flood stage is reduced by the bridge. As noted above there is no contraction scour. For long
            term scour the channel downstream of the culvert was analyzed based on (HEC No. 20) Section
            6.4 Vertical Channel Stability. These calculations were done assuming no large wood in the
            channel. Section 6.4 uses calculations for incipient motion, shear stress and channel armoring.
            From the HEC RAS output the shear stress in the proposed bridge area at the 100-year flood
            averages 1.6 lbs./sq. ft. From Figure 4, the estimated sediment particle size for incipient motion
            is 5 inches. Looking at the Pebble Counts from Figure 4, this is about the D75 pebble size.
            The gradation curve from Figure 4, indicates that 75 percent of the bed material is smaller than
            or equal to this particle diameter. Therefore, 25 percent of the bed is courser than the critical
            particle size, and armoring is possible (greater than the 5 percent threshold). From Equation
            6.16 (HEC No. 20 ), the estimated depth of scour at which an armor layer can form is
            approximately 30 inches (2.5 feet). To prevent future scour the bed material under the bridge
            will be increased slightly from the natural sediment size. The proposed gradation is shown in
            Figure 4. In addition, the riprap revetment to protect the bridge footings will extend 3.5 feet
            below the proposed bed elevation. The scour depth calculated for this new sediment gradation
            is 10 inches. Scour calculations are shown in Figure 8.

              7 ALTERNATIVE ANALYSIS
            Site 1: Jones Creek

            For the culvert replacement, a new culvert and bridge were both considered. The channel
            width was measured at 23 feet. For a culvert (using the WDFW Stream Simulation Guidelines)
            the culvert span at the bed level would need to be 30 feet. Given there is only 3 feet of cover on
            the existing culvert this limits the configurations for culverts to low profile or concrete box type
            culverts. These culvert types are much more expensive compared to regular CMP type culverts.
            In addition, the culvert length would have to be increased in proportion to the road height
            being increased. Cost estimates considering all of these factors tends to show the culvert option
            more expensive and the bridge does provide more width than the culvert.


            For the channel profile (see Figure 7), the regrade potential is minimal and will fit into the
            existing 2.5 to 2.2 percent slope range. The general rule for stream simulation design is to keep
            the bed within 25 percent of the natural channel, so a slope up to 2.9 percent can be used.




            Figure 9 - Stream profile options (Jones Creek).


            Site 2: Jones Creek Tributary

            Three options were considered to correct fish passage, a 40 foot span bridge, and two different
            shape culverts. Given the lack of clearance, how steep the channel is (13 percent downstream
            and 6 percent upstream), and working around the waterline the bridge option was selected as
            the preferred alternative. Costs would be similar for all other options. Options for addressing
            the stream profile is shown in Figure 7.

            VAP 1 – Vertical Adjustment Profile 1 would start downstream of the 13 percent slope section
            and extend upstream at a 6 percent slope. The excavation in the streambed would be 3 to 3.5
            feet and extend upstream 160 feet.


            VAP 2 - Vertical Adjustment Profile 2 would start at the first bed control downstream of the
            culvert and extend upstream at a 6 percent slope, matching grade about 40 feet upstream of the
            culvert.


            VAP 3 - Vertical Adjustment Profile 3 would start at a lower point than VAP2 and extend
            upstream at a 6 percent slope, matching grade about 130 feet upstream of the culvert.


            VAP 4 - Vertical Adjustment Profile 4 would start at VAP3 and extend upstream at a 6 percent
            slope to a point 40 feet above the culvert and then a steeper 10 percent grade for a length of 20
            feet to match grade.


            For all the channel profile options, excavation upstream would result in a big disturbance to the
            riparian area and potentially destabilize the existing channel. The channel flattens out about
            200 feet upstream of the culvert so a large regrade is not preferred here. VAP 4 provides the
            best overall approach to minimize channel disturbance and provide fish passage. The 10
            percent section is intended to slightly degrade over time and the slope lessened.
