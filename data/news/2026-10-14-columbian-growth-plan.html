Clark County Council approves 20-year growth plan

Several councilors raise concerns about rural cluster developments

By Shari Phiel, Columbian Staff Reporter
Published: October 14, 2026, 11:00am

With one last-minute change, the Clark County Council on Tuesday unanimously approved an updated plan that will guide the county’s growth for the next two decades.

The plan will now go to the state Department of Commerce, which oversees compliance with Washington’s Growth Management Act.

It’s taken the county three years to update its comprehensive growth management plan, which guides development needed to support population and employment growth. The plan includes mandatory planning elements for land use, housing, transportation, capital facilities, public utilities, parks and recreation, economic development and climate change. It also includes an element for rural and natural resource lands, which addresses development permits, agricultural activities and limited rural uses.

During deliberations early this month, the council approved code changes with new development standards for rural cluster developments, which concentrate residential or mixed-use development on smaller portions of land while preserving open space.

During Tuesday’s meeting, Councilor Matt Little said he had second thoughts about his vote for the changes and asked to reconsider them. The changes would allow owners of agricultural and resource lands to divide out small parcels for eventual development while preserving the remaining land.

“I’ve been doing some thinking about it, and I heard the concerns of Councilor (Glen) Yung and Councilor (Michelle) Belkot about not having enough time to review the implications of it. My concerns about it are actually about conservation,” Little said.

He said he also heard from residents that the intent of the development code was to not only allow farmers to have a little bit of extra income but more importantly to allow the landowner to conserve parcels.

Chair Sue Marshall said the code hasn’t been consistently applied but its intention is to preserve resource lands while allowing cluster development on poor soil.

“I think what’s needed really is to examine how it’s been applied because I don’t think there’s been care in really sticking to the requirements,” she said.

Marshall suggested putting a six-month moratorium in effect to give the council more time to study the issues, look for recommendations on how to move forward and make a better informed decision.

The council is required to hold a public hearing on the moratorium within the next 60 days.

A previous moratorium on rural cluster development went into effect in 1994 and wasn’t lifted until 2016.

Along with staff researching options for rural development, Yung asked that the agricultural advisory commission weigh in on the matter.
