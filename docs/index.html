Rules of Procedure — ordinance rewriting the public comment rules

The Council is amending Exhibit “A” to CCC 2.09.040 — its own rules on how meetings run and what the public may say. Read from the draft ordinance already cached in this project (markup dated 27 July 2026, unadopted: ordinance number blank, adoption date blank):

The decorum clause (§VII.L.vi) is being rewritten. The struck-out old text barred remarks “defamatory to County staff, uncivil, rude, vulgar, profane.” The replacement bars statements concerning private activities, lifestyles or beliefs of others; topics unrelated to county business; and “professional duties and performance of County employees or employees of its elected officials.” That last category is the ordinary substance of public comment at a county council — it is how a permit backlog, a jail medical contract or a named official's conduct gets raised in public at all.

Point of order widens. New text: “Any Councilor may ask for a point of order,” after which the Chair or Vice Chair may tell a speaker to stop. Today that starts with the Chair alone.

Two changes that are good, and worth saying so. Open public comment moves earlier in the Tuesday order — to right after Amendments to the Agenda, instead of after the hearings and bid awards, so you no longer sit through three hearings to use your three minutes. And a new subsection provides that “members of the public may provide public testimony on removed agenda items during Public Comment,” closing the trick where pulling an item from Council Time makes it un-commentable under the agenda-items-only rule. Additions to Council Time must also now be “time sensitive in nature.”

Nothing is posted for this hearing, and that is now the story. Every link on the Council's own 1 September listing row was fetched twice on 26 August (18:42 and again at 21:40). The eleven posted documents are the budget resolution, the budget reports, the CAPER staff report and presentation, the minutes, three appointment letters and three separate-business staff reports. There is no ordinance and no Exhibit “A” for the Rules hearing — five days out. The agenda entry is three lines. Ask for the ordinance to be posted and the hearing continued if it is not; that is a thing to say in three minutes.

Firm and not firm. That the cached draft is unadopted and contains these changes: firm. That the ordinance heard on 1 September is this draft: unverifiable from the agenda, because nothing is published, and the draft is five weeks old. The underline / strikethrough reading is inferred from PDF text-layer artefacts, not from seeing the marked-up page; whether the “professional duties and performance” clause is new or carried over is not settled. Read the posted ordinance first, if it appears. It wins.

What it buys: 14.00 new permanent FTE — 10 Deputy Sheriff positions and 4 Corrections Officer positions, effective 08/2026, listed as Regular, not project-term (Attachment B, pp.3–4).

The part worth reading twice. Under RCW 43.101.540 (ESHB 2015) the state runs a $100M grant programme available only through 30 June 2028. It pays 75% of these costs; the county's 0.1% Criminal Justice Sales and Use Tax pays 25%. Then, in the county's own words: “Upon expiration of the grant in 2028, the dedicated sales and use tax will assume responsibility for the ongoing costs of these Council-approved positions.” So a regressive local sales tax picks up 100% of roughly $1.79M a year, before any compensation increases. That is the standard grant-cliff shape — stated plainly in the county's own document rather than buried, which is to the Budget Office's credit.

Know this before spending three minutes: the hearing is largely formal. The narrative says the staffing was already approved by Staff Report 174-26 on 21 July 2026 and the funding strategy by 098-26 on 21 April. Tuesday is appropriation authority for a decision already taken; the live questions are the 2028 cliff and the 2027–28 phases, which go through the annual budget processes. Two document problems, both small: the narrative says grant revenue is “$1,626,698” where every schedule says $1,626,298; and it calls the 0.1% tax “voter-approved” once and “Councilmanic” twice, which are opposite claims about whether the public ever voted on it. Unresolved here — a fair question for staff.

Micromobility grant applications (corrected — the earlier warning was wrong)

Authorises applications to the Autzen Foundation, BNSF Railway Foundation and other nonprofits. This page previously warned that undefined “safety measures” could mean enforcement against riders. Having read the staff report: it does not. The item is about county parks, trails and naturalized areas, not roads, and the named activities are park and trail signage, a consultant for youth-focused behaviour-change messaging, educational materials, and summer safety presentations at up to six of the county's twelve public high schools. No enforcement funding, no citations, no Sheriff's equipment. Up to $120,000, scaled to whatever is raised. Approval “does not imply approval of an eventual grant agreement”; any award returns to Council. Timing explains the urgency: the Autzen letter of interest is due 4 September, the full application 11 September.

The quiet channel is next to this item, not in it. The report notes Council “has requested a work session” on motorised micromobility and that Public Works “is creating a new motorized micromobility device policy.” The grant funds signs and education; the policy — where any restriction on where people may ride would live — is being drafted separately and shaped in the one format that takes no public comment at all (Rules §VIII). That work session is the thing to watch for on a future Wednesday agenda.

Title 20 update: affordability requirements for HB 1491 compliance

The most consequential housing item on any agenda this cycle. Vancouver is deciding how to comply with HB 1491's affordability mandate near transit, and staff have already argued on the record that the law is "partially funded inclusionary zoning." This hearing is where the size of the carve-out gets set.

HB 1491 (2025) requires 50-year affordability within a quarter-mile of Bus Rapid Transit stations — 10% of units at 60% AMI, or 20% at 80% AMI, or 10% at 80% AMI where a tenth are two-bed or larger. Cities must offer offsets: a 20-year MFTE exemption, a 50% impact fee reduction, minimum FAR, density bonuses, no parking minimums.

Vancouver has 51 stations across two existing Vine BRT routes — one of few jurisdictions outside Puget Sound with high-capacity transit, so a law written for Sound Transit light rail lands unusually hard here.

From the 10 August council deck: staff argue 50 years of required affordability against at most 20 years of offset, that the other incentives are already available citywide and so offset nothing at the margin, and that the result "decreases overall production and directs development away from transit." Their proposed approach is exclusions — downtown and other minimum-FAR areas, plus roughly 25% of parcels near stations.

Worth engaging rather than dismissing: the production argument is about volume, while HB 1491 targets tenure and depth. Trading one for the other is a choice, not an arithmetic result.

Tri-Mountain Golf Course — a lease of county land for up to 50 years

Presented under old business, which is why it read as informational. It is not. The presentation — fetched and read after the meeting — is a lease RFP with a release date of 9 September. A county-owned golf course goes to a private operator for “management, operations, and maintenance… with minimal County operational support.”

Initial term ten years, 1 Jan 2027 – 31 Dec 2036, and the county “reserves the right to extend… four (4) additional terms, in ten (10) year increments” — a maximum of 50 years. The county may terminate on 90 days' notice if the lease is not being met satisfactorily, “solely in the county's judgment.”

The scoring is the finding

CriterionPoints
Operating plan25
Proposal approach / quality20
Creativity / experience15
Work history / references15
Capital improvements / maintenance15
Lease payment to the County10
Interview, finalists only+50 (of 150)

The money the public receives is the lowest-weighted of the six criteria — 10% of the base score, 6.7% once the interview is counted — while the county's own stated goals include “maximizing the return on its assets.” Those do not fit together. The interview alone is worth five times the lease payment, which is a large discretionary component in awarding a half-century lease. Two stated goals also pull against each other: “affordable rates for the citizens” and maximising return. Whichever way that is resolved, it belongs in the RFP, not in post-selection negotiation.

No dollar figure exists. “Total lease terms and payments to the County, including extensions, will be negotiated determined by evaluating the selected proposal(s)” — the price is an output of the process, not an input to it.

The timeline has no public comment step and no council vote on it: released 9 Sep · pre-submittal 18 Sep · questions close 25 Sep · proposals due 13 Oct · evaluation 14–21 Oct · interviews 26–29 Oct · selection committee recommendation 30 Oct · negotiation and execution 1–17 Nov · lease begins 1 Jan 2027. It runs from selection committee straight to execution. Whether it returns to Council for approval is not stated — that is the question to ask, and the window is before 9 September, because after that the terms are fixed in a released RFP.

Not verified: the RFP document itself, which is not public until 9 September — this is a summary deck of a document no one outside the county has read. Also unretrieved: what the course currently costs the county, what it returns, who operates it now, and its acreage. One slide carries a stale “8/9/17” footer and the timeline says the lease begins “January 1, 2026” where everything else says 2027 — both look like deck reuse, neither was confirmed.

Work session — no public comment · decks now read

North County transfer station — four sites left, and the last cut is about willing sellers

Both presentations have now been read. The funnel: 158 possible sites → 15 → 7 → 4. The four recommended for further consideration are sites 12/13, 116, 137 and 141. Two Ridgefield sites were dropped as commercial/employment zoned; site 117 was dropped as lowest-scoring. The facility is 1,500 tons per day on 20 usable acres, built around 2032 on a stated 60-year horizon.

The finding. Parametrix says in the same three slides that “further site study… is not likely to score sites 12/13, 116, 137 and 141 differently,” that potentially willing sellers were found at 5 of 7 sites, and that the county should “move to an environmental assessment (EIS) for the two sites with the strongest likelihood of willing sellers.” Read together: the four finalists are tied on the merits, so the tiebreak is landowner willingness. That is not irrational — a site whose owner will not sell costs years — but it means a 35-criteria scoring system built over two years stops governing at the last and most consequential cut. The question is: what are the four sites and who owns them?

Credit where it is due, and a correction. This page previously said siting studies “reliably land facilities next to the households least able to object.” The primary screen here excluded residentially zoned land and flood zones at the first cut, which is better than that framing assumed. The qualification stands: not residentially zoned is not the same as not near residents, and the 35 criteria and their weights are images in the deck with no figures in the text layer, so whether proximity to housing is scored at all is unknown.

Two things to act on. Staff asked Council to approve an amendment authorising Phase 2 — a contract item that must come to a Tuesday meeting — and to conduct a public hearing on the siting. That hearing would be the first formal comment right this project has had; it is not yet scheduled, and it is worth asking for by name. There is also a project public comment form among the county's listed channels.

The open question nobody is scheduled to answer. The county's own slide 8 says the siting process “does not determine who will own or operate the facility,” and lists private ownership and operation (the current model), public ownership with contracted operation, and public ownership with public operation as live options, to be “evaluated separately.” No date, forum or process for that evaluation appears anywhere in either deck. For a 60-year piece of essential infrastructure sited with public money, that is the decision to get in front of — and it is currently unscheduled.

Vancouver Tue 25 Aug · special meeting

Culture, Arts and Heritage Commission

3a
Special meeting — 24 hours' notice · found late

A city commission hired its own law firm, on an open-ended hourly contract

This was missed when it happened and found on a re-check. It is the channel that most easily passes unnoticed: a special meeting — RCW 42.30.080 requires only 24 hours' notice — with five items, whose single action was “Vote for Approval of CAHC Legal Counsel.” The firm presented in a workshop, then the Commission voted on the firm, in the same sitting.

The attached engagement letter and services agreement retain Madrona Law Group as general counsel at $340/hour for the lead attorney and $360 for the back-up, on a 2026 card of $285–$360 for attorneys and $160 for paralegals. Billing is in tenth-of-an-hour increments with a tenth-hour minimum per activity; travel is billed at full rate; every phone call is chargeable; when two staff work a matter at once, both bill. Late payment draws 12% a year.

There is no cap and no end date. The agreement runs “until terminated by either party” and no not-to-exceed figure, annual estimate, budget line or funding source appears anywhere in either document. An hourly agreement with no cap and no term is not a number — it is whatever the Commission uses.

Say the fair part too: a body that drafts policy and negotiates contracts without legal advice makes expensive mistakes, and small public bodies are routinely under-lawyered. The scope is ordinary general-counsel work and the Commission can terminate at will. The structure is the issue — public bodies commonly cap these and revisit on renewal, and no reporting requirement to the Commission or to Council appears in either document.

The discrepancy worth a records request. The engagement letter describes the Commission as “a public development authority.” The City's own website lists its PDAs as the City Center Redevelopment Authority, Downtown Redevelopment Authority, Public Facilities District Board and Transportation Benefit District — the Culture, Arts and Heritage Commission is not among them, and is described as a body that “works with the Parks, Recreation and Cultural Services Department.” Those are not compatible, and it matters: a PDA is a separate legal entity that can hold money, contract and sue. Which is correct is unresolved here and I will not guess. Also unverified: whether the motion passed (no minutes yet), whether the agreement has been executed (the copy in the packet has blank signature lines), and whether any selection process or RFP preceded it.

Two openings. Four of eleven seats are vacant, including the Vice President. And item 4a is a Boards & Commissions Open House, Wednesday 9 September, 2:30–4:30pm, Cascade Park Community Library, 600 NE 136th Ave — the recruitment channel for those seats. This Commission takes public comment on any issue at every meeting, three minutes, written comment to parksrecculture@cityofvancouver.us by 5pm the day before. No further CAHC meeting is on the city calendar through 25 September.
