Title 20 update: affordability requirements for HB 1491 compliance

Correction to an earlier version of this page: the packet was described here as unpublished. It has since posted — 62 pages — and has been read. What is still unpublished is the staff report for this item, the one carrying the actual proposed code language. Staff's own memo promised it “10 days in advance of the public hearing” — that was 29 August. The Commission is scheduled to vote a recommendation on code text the public has not seen.

What the law requires. Within a quarter-mile walkshed of Vancouver's 51 Vine BRT stations: no off-street parking requirements, a 50% impact fee cut for projects using the 20-year MFTE, and 50-year affordability — 10% of units at 60% AMI, or 20% at 80% AMI, or 10% at 80% AMI with a tenth of units at two bedrooms or more. It must be met in each individual building; a master planned community cannot concentrate the affordability in one block.

What staff have done with the exemptions. The statute lets cities exempt parcels already zoned at the FAR threshold on 1 January 2025, plus a discretionary 25% of the remaining parcels. Staff exempted the whole City Center Vision subarea and CX Plan District, and then — in their own words — “identified parcels making up to 25% of otherwise non-exempt station area that are anticipated as most likely to redevelop and added them to the exemption list.”

That sentence is the finding. The 25% could have gone anywhere. It was applied to the parcels most likely to actually be built on — so the mandate falls heaviest where development is least likely and lightest where it is most likely. Staff's stated reason is that HB 1491 is “effectively a partially funded inclusionary zoning requirement” — 50 years of affordability against at most 20 years of MFTE — that pushes development away from transit. That argument deserves engaging, but note what it is: an argument about volume, aimed at a statute about depth and tenure. Trading one for the other is a choice, not an arithmetic result.

And the choice lives in a map that may not be law. The memo says staff “does not envision codifying this map, but will post it to the City's website.” The presentation says the mapping is “to be referenced and linked in code.” Those are different things: a map in code changes by ordinance, a map on a website changes by staff decision. Ask which.

Vancouver is non-compliant right now, and the state's rules apply in the gap. The city's deadline was 31 December 2025; it adopted 1 June 2026. Commerce has finalised a model code that preempts local zoning for non-compliant cities, and staff say it “applies to impacted, non-exempt parcels until the City adds the affordability components to its code.” Vancouver is the only city currently subject to HB 1491. So the question is not whether there is an affordability requirement near BRT — there is one today — but whether Vancouver's replaces it with something narrower.

Evergreen Public Schools: the school impact fee goes to zero

The agenda title says “capital facilities plan.” The substance is a fee schedule. Table 36 of the Comprehensive Plan is amended so Evergreen's school impact fee drops from $6,432 to $0 on a single-family home and $3,753 to $0 on a multi-family unit, plus $938.25 to $0 on an ADU, for the six years to 31 December 2032.

This is legally compelled, not a giveaway. Impact fees may fund only capital needed for new growth; Evergreen's board found adequate capacity at every grade level and declining enrollment, and its counsel wrote to the City that the district “will not be entitled to collect school impact fees.” Council already imposed a moratorium on collection in June and affirmed it in August.

The question worth asking anyway. The same packet notes the district's upcoming process for consolidating existing facilities, while the district's own letter says the City is planning for “at least 38,000 additional housing units (about 44% more than the City has now)” and enrollment “is likely to gradually increase.” So capacity is surplus now because the district is shrinking and closing buildings, and the growth that would refill them lands inside the 20-year plan but outside the six-year fee window. Whatever capacity is then needed gets paid for by levy or bond — by existing residents — rather than by the development that caused it. The district can amend its plan annually. On what trigger, and will the City ask?

Proportional impact fees — smaller homes pay less, bigger homes pay more

State law now requires residential impact fees to be proportional, so small units pay less. Vancouver's current fees, from a memo that is not in the packet PDF and had to be fetched separately:

FeeSingle familyMulti-family
Park impact fee, three districts$6,130$4,480
School impact fee, Vancouver SD$2,786$2,486
Traffic impact fee, three districts$247–$439 per vehicle trip

Staff want square-footage brackets — illustrated as 0–1,500 / 1,500–2,500 / 2,500+ sq ft — rather than a per-square-foot rate, working with ECOnorthwest. ADUs are statutorily capped at 50% of the main unit's fee. The design constraint to watch is that staff intend it to be revenue neutral: every dollar taken off a small unit is added to a large one. That is a genuinely progressive redistribution and worth supporting — but revenue neutrality is a choice, not a requirement, and it is being settled now, in a workshop, with no vote. Follow-up workshop 13 October; hearings “likely at the end of the year.”

Two City documents in one packet disagree by $90. The Vancouver school impact fee is $2,786 in this memo and $2,876 in the Evergreen staff report. One is a transposition. Not resolved here. Also worth knowing: HB 1491's mandated offsets include a 50% impact fee cut, so a proportional redesign changes what that 50% is calculated against. Items 5 and 9 are not independent.

“Parking Scofflaw Program” — and no document says what it is

The agenda has been read; the packet is not published, so there is no attachment for this item and no way to know what is proposed. Presented by the Parking Project Coordinator and the Lead Parking Officer. Scofflaw programmes elsewhere typically mean escalating enforcement against vehicles with accumulated unpaid citations: boot, tow, impound.

If that is what this is, the reason to care is specific rather than general. Towing turns an unpaid fine into loss of the vehicle and then into storage fees larger than the original debt — falling on people who could not pay the first ticket, and intersecting directly with vehicle residency. Three questions with yes/no answers: is there an ability-to-pay determination, is there a payment plan that halts escalation, and is there a protocol for vehicles someone is living in? Asking before a programme is designed is worth more than objecting after.

Public comment here is open on any issue — the agenda says “the public is invited to speak regarding any issue” — three minutes, but you must pre-register by 5pm Tuesday 8 September: email callie.taylor@cityvancouver.us or phone 360-487-8650. Note the domain has no “of” in it; that is what the agenda prints, three times.

Item 4 is a Parking Code Review & Update, also with no published presentation. It runs the day after the Planning Commission's HB 1491 hearing — a law whose mandated offsets include eliminating off-street parking requirements near all 51 BRT stations. Whether the code review implements that, works around it, or does not touch it is not knowable from the agenda.

The grant clause is the sharpest one, because of when it lands. The 12 August minutes record Council approving an FAA grant, a WSDOT grant and the Century West contract for a Pearson Field master plan with an 18–24 month run and a kickoff two weeks out. At this same meeting the committee nominates its representative to that plan's advisory committee — with no attachment saying what the seat is. Deleting the grant clause removes the only lay review between a federal grant application and a Council consent vote.

A new clause also requires members to work “respectfully” to advance the “best interests of the airport”. Council can already remove a member by majority vote; this gives that vote a hook it does not currently have, and the section still reserves a seat for a neighbourhood representative — whose job includes objecting.

Not all of it runs one way. The redline adds the public works director to those advised, broadens “economic development projects” to “land use projects”, extends plan review to plan updates, and states plainly that the committee is advisory only — which is already the law. Nine seats to seven partly ratifies reality: the August roll call was six. The three edits that should not survive on any reading are the grant review deletion, the airport-manager approval on the committee's own business, and the loyalty clause.

How to comment. “Community Communication – Public Comment” is item 3. The minutes up for approval describe a vote taken without a quorum

From the 25 August special meeting minutes: the commission voted to direct the President to sign the Madrona Law Group engagement letter “contingent on consent from commissioner Bennett to ensure quorum… The motion was later approved by Bennett and unanimously passed.” Bennett had already left the meeting; President Lewis was absent throughout. So the body voted knowing it was short, then collected the missing member's assent outside the meeting.

The Open Public Meetings Act (RCW 42.30) requires final action to be taken at a meeting open to the public. This is not a claim that the retention is void — that is for the City Attorney, and the minutes may describe something more careful than they read. The point is narrower: the minutes as drafted describe a defective vote and they are item 2a for approval. Re-taking the vote at a meeting with a quorum costs nothing and removes the problem. And the member whose after-the-fact consent carried it is no longer on the roster — Bennett is gone from the 10 September agenda masthead, with no departure recorded anywhere in the packet.

Who can be a fiscal sponsor was put off, so the narrow reading stands

Applicants must be nonprofits, or groups and individuals with a nonprofit fiscal sponsor whose mission is “aligned with advancing or preserving arts, culture, heritage, or science”. Staff put the choice to the commission on 13 August: narrow — the sponsor's primary purpose must be arts/culture; or broad — the sponsor must do such work but may have another primary purpose. Staff's own note: asking groups who already have a mission-aligned sponsor to find a new one “could limit program accessibility”, and organisations already offering sponsorship “could have an advantage” — they also collect a fee of up to 10%.

It was deferred to a later cycle because this one is already open. So the narrow reading governs a $1.75m round that closes at 11:59pm on the day of this meeting. The groups it excludes are the obvious ones: a cultural project carried by a neighbourhood association, a tenants' organisation, a mutual aid group, a labour council. The counter-argument is real — pushing public money through sponsors with no grant administration record is its own risk, and staff flagged capacity gaps. The answer that fits both is a published exceptions route for this cycle, not a rule change mid-round. Item 4b is where to raise it, and it has no attachment.

2
Quiet — one line at the bottom of a hearing block

A third extension freezes site-specific rezones into late 2027

Resolution 2026-09-01 extends the suspension of the annual site-specific comprehensive plan amendment cycle to 30 October 2026. The chronology, from the resolution's own recitals: the periodic update was statutorily due December 2025 under RCW 36.70A.130(5)(b) and is “now expected to be approved in mid-October 2026”. The freeze has run since 1 October 2023. Pre-applications reopen 31 October 2026 — but a final adopting ordinance can come “no sooner than one year after the adoption of the 2025–2045 comprehensive plan”, so no site-specific rezone can actually be granted until roughly late October 2027, four years after the freeze began.

It points both ways and the criticism belongs on the delay. Four years is a long time to close the only route by which a single parcel's designation can be corrected outside a once-a-decade update; the one alternative opening, the periodic update itself, closed its written record on 3 September. But the annual cycle is also how urban growth boundary expansions get picked off one parcel at a time outside any comprehensive capacity analysis, and RCW 36.70A.130(2)(a) genuinely forbids running it against a pending update. The suspension is legally required. The ten-month overrun is not.

County development fees rise 6–42%, and a flat subdivision fee hits small plats five times harder

An ordinance amending CCC 6.110A.010, 6.110A.040, 6.140.020-1 and 6.140.030-1 moves Land Use Review and Wetland & Habitat Review to 100% cost recovery plus a 2.5% technology fee. Average increases 8% and 7%; $163,109 of extra revenue in 2027. Process to date: one Council work session (5 August), one advisory board presentation (3 September), one legal notice (28 August).

Largest movesNowProposedChange

Conditional use permit (Type III)$8,291$10,636+28%

Most lines rise 6%. The big jumps are not padding: the two hearings examiners' rate went from $200 to $275 an hour on 1 May 2026. And cost recovery is the defensible default — the alternative is the General Fund subsidising review of private development applications.

Two places it strains. The subdivision fee is flat up to 16 lots: $1,153 per lot at 16, but $6,151 per lot at three — a fixed cost falling five times harder on small infill than on a large plat. A per-lot band is the fix and this is the hearing to ask for it. And a 2.5% technology fee on top of “100% of current costs of service” is, by construction, 102.5%; the staff report's own worked example is a $687 cost charged at $704.

Buried in the same ordinance, unexplained anywhere in the staff report: the list of building components eligible for deferred submittal is cut hard — fire sprinkler, fire alarm, exit illumination, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, auxiliary power, and floor and roof trusses all come off. Deferred submittal is what lets a builder pull a permit and start while those designs are still being engineered. For multifamily and commercial work that changes the construction timeline.

Fire impact fees are new — every one of these districts is at $0 today. Fire District 3 proposes $992 single family / $505 per multi-family unit; District 6, $791 / $563; Clark-Cowlitz Fire Rescue, $654 / $308. Fire District 10 proposes $3,975–$4,714 for a single-family home, five times its neighbours, and its staff report presents two methods rather than one recommendation. Both directions, honestly. A $13,665 per-unit multi-family fee in Woodland being higher than the single-family fee is backwards from any density-supporting position, and Washington's new proportional-fee law exists precisely because flat per-unit fees fall hardest on small units — three commissioners voted to deny. But the alternative funding source is a levy or bond, which falls on existing residents including renters, and which rural districts often fail to pass. A fee legally restricted to growth-caused capacity is not the regressive option here.

No fire impact fee may be collected until the district signs an interlocal agreement with the county (CCC 40.630.090), so adoption is not the last step. The four fire districts have their own case numbers — CPZ2025-00010 through -00013 — where the meeting listing repeated -00010 for all four.
